The manager's question

The manager's question

Your bank's fraud system flags a ₹42,000 card payment at 2 a.m. The vendor's brochure says the system catches 99% of fraudulent payments and wrongly flags only 2% of genuine ones. Roughly 1 payment in 500 is actually fraud (all numbers hypothetical).

Your operations head wants every flagged card blocked on the spot. “The system is 99% accurate, so a flag means fraud.” Before you agree: if a payment is flagged, what is the real chance it's fraud? 99%? 98%? Something else entirely?

Every probability is “out of” something. Conditional probability changes the “out of”, and Bayes' theorem tells you how far new evidence should move your belief: no more, no less.

(The answer, by the way, is about 9%. By the end of this page you'll see exactly why, why that doesn't make the detector useless, and what a sensible manager does with a flag.)

Level 1 · Basics

The idea in plain words

P(A∣B)P(A \mid B)P(A∣B), read “the probability of A given B”, is the probability of A once you know that B has happened.

Here is the whole trick. When you learn that B happened, everything outside B stops mattering. Your universe shrinks to B. So you count how much of A sits inside B, and divide by the size of B. That's it. People call this “restricting the universe”.

Think of it like

An Excel filter. You have 2,887 marketers in a sheet. Turn on a filter: Business focus = B2B. Now only 1,184 rows are visible. Count the rows that say “Yes, we'll increase LinkedIn”: 912. So P(Yes∣B2B)=912/1184=0.77P(\text{Yes} \mid \text{B2B}) = 912/1184 = 0.77P(Yes∣B2B)=912/1184=0.77. Conditional probability is just filter, then count.

Conditional probability, P(A∣B)P(A \mid B)P(A∣B)
Probability of A among the cases where B is true. The bar “|” means “given”.
Joint probability, P(A∩B)P(A \cap B)P(A∩B)
Probability that A and B both happen, out of everyone.
Marginal probability, P(A)P(A)P(A)
Probability of A on its own, ignoring B. A total in the margin of the table.
Independent events
Knowing B tells you nothing new about A: P(A∣B)=P(A)P(A \mid B) = P(A)P(A∣B)=P(A).
Prior
Your probability for a hypothesis before the new evidence. Example: 5% of cardholders default.
Likelihood
How probable the evidence is if the hypothesis is true: P(missed payment | defaulter).
Posterior
Your updated probability after the evidence: P(defaulter | missed payment).

The direction of the bar matters

P(A∣B)P(A \mid B)P(A∣B) and P(B∣A)P(B \mid A)P(B∣A) sound alike but answer different questions, and they can be wildly different numbers. Class Problem 8 shows this beautifully. Every customer who defaults has missed a payment, so P(miss | default) = 1. But most customers who miss a payment do not go on to default.

This is the conditional formula rearranged, and it's what you do along every branch of a probability tree. If 25% of customers open a promotional email and 12% of openers click, then P(open∩click)=0.25×0.12=0.03P(\text{open} \cap \text{click}) = 0.25 \times 0.12 = 0.03P(open∩click)=0.25×0.12=0.03: 3 clicks per 100 emails sent.

Mutually exclusive

Can't happen together: P(A∩B)=0P(A \cap B) = 0P(A∩B)=0. Gold card vs Platinum card for one customer. If you know one happened, the other's probability drops to 0, so they are dependent.

Independent

Can happen together, but one tells you nothing about the other: P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B)P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B). A customer's birth month and whether they buy this week.

Bayes' theorem: turning the bar around

Often you know the probabilities “the wrong way round”. The bank knows P(miss | default) from history, but it needs P(default | miss) for the customer in front of it. Bayes' theorem flips the condition.

Look at the formula again and you'll see a tree hiding in it. The top line is one path (prior × likelihood). The bottom line adds up every path that produces the evidence. Drawing the tree is often the safest way to apply Bayes in an exam.

Explore it yourself

Prior 0.5; the agency asks for information on 75% of winning bids and 40% of losing bids.
  • Leave everything as it is: the posterior should read 0.652, the answer to Problem 7(c).
  • Make management more pessimistic: set the prior to 0.30. The same request now lifts the chance only to about 0.45. Evidence moves you from where you started.
  • Set the false-positive rate (unsuccessful bids that still get a request) equal to 0.75. The posterior equals the prior. When the evidence is equally likely either way, it tells you nothing.
Prior 0.05; every defaulter misses a payment, and so do 20% of customers who never default.
  • Read the posterior: about 0.21, just above the bank's 0.20 recall threshold. Look at the frequency grid and find the 50 defaulters among the 240 missers.
  • Nudge the false-positive rate from 0.20 to 0.22. The posterior drops below 0.20 and the decision flips. How confident is the bank in that 0.20?
  • Drag the prior down to 0.01, as for a very safe customer segment. Even with a perfect “sensitivity” of 1, a missed payment now signals only about a 5% default risk.

Level 3 · Application

Where managers use it

Banking & credit

Who to call first

Collections teams rank accounts by P(default | behaviour so far): missed payments, utilisation, bureau score. Bayes is how a single new signal updates that risk.

Fraud & risk operations

How good is an alert?

The useful number is P(fraud | alert), the alert's precision, not the detection rate. It depends heavily on how rare fraud is, which is why rule tuning never ends.

Marketing

Don't flip the funnel

“80% of buyers saw our ad” is P(saw ad | bought). The budget question is P(bought | saw ad), which can be tiny. Attribution claims often quietly swap the two.

HR

Attrition risk given engagement

If 30% of low-engagement employees leave within a year versus 8% overall, the engagement survey is predictive and worth acting on. Same logic as churn given a complaint.

Operations & quality

Root cause of defects

Given a defect, which shift, machine or vendor is most likely behind it? A Bayes table on production data points the investigation in the right direction.

Insurance

Pricing by segment

P(claim | young driver, metro city) vs P(claim | overall) is the basis of risk-based premiums. If a factor doesn't change the conditional probability, it shouldn't change the price.

Indian context

Suppose a Dehradun NBFC (hypothetical) finds that 4% of its two-wheeler loan borrowers eventually default. Its early-warning rule flags 90% of the borrowers who later default, and 15% of those who don't. Out of 1,000 borrowers: 40 will default and 36 of them get flagged; 960 won't, but 144 of them get flagged too. So 180 are flagged and only 36/180 = 0.20 of them are real defaulters. Four in five flagged borrowers are fine. A polite reminder call is the right first move, not a recovery agent. And remember the COD numbers from the basics page? P(returned | COD) = 180/900 = 0.20 against P(returned | prepaid) = 90/1,500 = 0.06. Same “given” logic, different business.

Mini-case: what should the bank do with a fraud flag?

Only about 9 flags in 100 are real fraud. The other 91 are genuine customers, many of them paying for something at 2 a.m. for perfectly good reasons. Auto-blocking every flag would annoy almost 2,000 good customers to stop 198 frauds.

But the detector is far from useless. Before the flag, the chance of fraud was 0.002. After it, 0.090: about 45 times higher. And a payment that is not flagged is almost certainly fine (2 in 97,806). So the sensible design is step-up checks: a flag triggers an OTP or a quick call, not a block. Cheap for the 91 genuine customers, and enough to stop most of the 9 frauds.

How a consultant would present it

“Of every 100 flagged payments, about 9 are fraud. A flag makes fraud 45 times more likely than average, so it's worth acting on, but with a verification step, not a block.” Natural frequencies and one recommendation. No formula on the slide.

Level 4 · Pro

Nuance, pitfalls and depth

The base-rate fallacy

The single most common error with conditional probability is ignoring the prior, the base rate. People hear “catches 99% of fraud” and think a flag means 99% fraud. What they forget is that genuine payments vastly outnumber fraud, so even a small false-alarm rate on that huge group produces more false flags than the few true ones. The medical-screening analogy

Doctors meet the same puzzle. Suppose 1% of people screened have a condition, the test picks up 90% of real cases, and it gives a false positive for 10% of healthy people. Of 1,000 people: 10 have it and 9 test positive; 990 don't, and 99 of them test positive anyway. So P(condition | positive) = 9/108 = 0.083. That's why a positive screening result leads to a second, more specific test rather than straight to treatment. Banks, fraud teams and HR screening tools face exactly this arithmetic.

Interview-style insight

“Our model is 99% accurate” is the start of a conversation, not the end. The first question back is: what's the base rate? The second: accurate on which side, sensitivity or specificity? Precision is what decides whether an alert is worth a human's time.

Sequential updating: today's posterior is tomorrow's prior

Evidence often arrives in instalments. Bayes handles that naturally: after each piece of evidence, the posterior becomes the new prior. Take the Problem 8 customer and suppose they miss a second payment, with the same likelihoods as before.

PriorP(default) = 0.05
Miss #10.05 × 1 ÷ (0.05 + 0.95 × 0.20) = 0.208
New prior0.208
Miss #20.208 ÷ (0.208 + 0.792 × 0.20) = 0.568

Two missed payments push the default risk from 5% to about 57%. You get exactly the same 0.568 by doing it in one step with P(two misses | no default) = 0.20 × 0.20 = 0.04: 0.05/(0.05 + 0.95 × 0.04) = 0.05/0.088 = 0.568. One caution, though. This assumes the two misses are independent signals given the customer's true type. If good customers who miss once are very likely to miss again (say, a salary delay), the second miss carries less information, and the true posterior is lower.

Confusing the inverse (the prosecutor's fallacy)

“If this customer were honest, a pattern this unusual would happen only 1% of the time, so there's a 99% chance he's a fraudster.” That sentence quietly swaps P(evidence | honest) for P(honest | evidence). It shows up in audit findings, fraud investigations and even courtrooms. The fix is always the same: ask for the base rate and run the numbers through Bayes.

Watch out for Simpson's paradox (conditioning on a third variable)

Conditional probabilities can reverse when you split by a third variable. Two telesales agents, A and B (hypothetical):

Easy leadsHard leadsAll leads
Agent A160/200 = 0.8020/100 = 0.20180/300 = 0.60
Agent B270/350 = 0.775/50 = 0.10275/400 = 0.69

A converts better on easy leads and on hard leads, yet B looks better overall, because B was handed mostly easy leads. Comparing P(convert | agent) without conditioning on lead type would reward the wrong person. Anderson et al. discuss this in Chapter 2 (Simpson's paradox, p. 58).

Small gaps in sample data

The textbook test “is P(A | B) exactly equal to P(A)?” is fine for exam exercises. With real survey data, the two numbers will almost never be exactly equal, even when the variables are truly unrelated, simply because of sampling noise. Whether a gap is real is a job for the chi-square test of independence in the Relationships pillar. See the note on Problem 5 below.

Class problem 5

Name-brand milk, given gender

Same table as Problem 2 on the basics page. Now filter by gender column.

(a)
P(N∣M)=319/1138=0.280P(N \mid M) = 319/1138 = 0.280P(N∣M)=319/1138=0.280
(b)
P(N∣F)=341/1138=0.300P(N \mid F) = 341/1138 = 0.300P(N∣F)=341/1138=0.300
(c)

Pro note

Look how small that gap is: 28.0% vs 30.0%. Is it real, or sampling noise? A chi-square test on name-brand vs everything else by gender gives p ≈ 0.31: no real evidence of a difference. But the full three-row table gives p ≈ 0.002, because women choose “something else” more often (148 vs 102). So the instructor's verdict holds for preference as a whole; it just isn't driven by name-brand milk. You'll learn this test in the chi-square topic. For the exam, use the exact-equality rule as the instructor does.

Case study

Hamilton County Judges Exam core

Anderson et al. (12e) · Chapter 4 · Case Problem · p. 212

A newspaper wants to rank 38 judges by how often their verdicts are appealed and reversed. The heart of the case is a conditional probability: P(reversed | appealed) for each judge and each court. Ranking by P(appealed) and ranking by P(reversed | appealed) can give different “best judges”, so the choice of condition is itself the managerial judgment. Common mistakes

WrongRight
“Every defaulter missed a payment, so a customer who misses a payment will default.”That's P(miss | default) = 1. The question needs P(default | miss) = 0.21. Check which side of the bar you are on.
P(Yes | B2B) = 912/2,887.That's the joint probability. Given B2B, divide by the B2B total: 912/1,184 = 0.770.
“The detector is 99% accurate, so a flag is 99% fraud.”Bring in the base rate. With fraud at 0.2% and 2% false alarms, P(fraud | flag) = 0.09.
P(open and click) = P(open) × P(click), using the overall click rate.Clicking depends on opening. Use P(open) × P(click | open). Multiply plain marginals only for independent events.
“Mutually exclusive events are independent.”The opposite: if they can't co-occur, knowing one rules out the other, so they're dependent.
Posteriors of 0.652 and 0.400.Posteriors across all hypotheses must add to 1 (0.652 + 0.348). If they don't, the denominator is wrong.
“0.280 ≠ 0.290, so gender strongly drives milk preference.”Unequal means dependent in the exercise sense, but small gaps in sample data can be noise. Size the gap, and test it with chi-square.
Updating twice on the same evidence (or on two signals that are really one).Each update must use genuinely new information. Correlated signals carry less evidence than two independent ones.

Quick check

In a telecom's data, P(churn | complaint) = 0.30. Which sentence says the same thing?

  1. Of the customers who complained, 30% churned
  2. 30% of the customers who churned had complained
  3. 30% of all customers both complained and churned
  4. Complaining raises churn by 30 percentage points
The condition (after the bar) is the group you restrict to: customers who complained. Option b is P(complaint | churn), option c is the joint probability, and option d needs P(churn | no complaint) as well.

Using the LinkedIn table from class Problem 4, what is P(B2B | plans to increase LinkedIn)?

  1. 0.770
  2. 0.316
  3. 0.498
  4. 0.410
Restrict to the 1,832 marketers who said Yes; 912 of them are B2B: 912/1,832 = 0.498. 0.770 is the reverse, P(Yes | B2B); 0.316 is the joint 912/2,887; 0.410 is the marginal P(B2B).

P(A) = 0.4, P(B) = 0.5 and P(A ∩ B) = 0.2. Which statement is correct?

  1. A and B are mutually exclusive
  2. A and B are independent, because P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B)
  3. A and B are dependent, because P(A ∩ B) is not zero
  4. You can't tell without the full contingency table
0.4 × 0.5 = 0.2, exactly the joint probability, so P(A | B) = 0.2/0.5 = 0.4 = P(A). They can't be mutually exclusive since P(A ∩ B) = 0.2 ≠ 0. A non-zero overlap says nothing about dependence by itself.

40% of job applicants would perform well. A screening test is passed by 80% of those who would perform well and by 30% of those who wouldn't. Given that a candidate passes, what is P(performs well)?

  1. 0.80
  2. 0.50
  3. 0.32
  4. 0.64
Joint (good and pass) = 0.40 × 0.80 = 0.32; joint (not good and pass) = 0.60 × 0.30 = 0.18; P(pass) = 0.50. Posterior = 0.32/0.50 = 0.64. Option a is the sensitivity, b is P(pass), c is the joint.

A fraud detector catches 99% of fraud and false-alarms on 2% of genuine payments. Fraud is 1 payment in 500. A payment is flagged. Which is closest to P(fraud | flag)?

  1. 99%
  2. 50%
  3. About 9%
  4. 98%
Per 100,000 payments: 198 frauds are flagged, but so are 1,996 genuine ones. 198/2,194 = 0.09. Ignoring the tiny base rate is what makes 99% feel right.

One-glance summary

Conditional probability in 60 seconds

  • Given = restrict the universe: P(A∣B)=P(A∩B)P(B)P(A \mid B) = \dfrac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}P(A∣B)=P(B)P(A∩B)​. From a count table: cell ÷ total of the given row or column.
  • Multiplication law: P(A∩B)=P(B) P(A∣B)P(A \cap B) = P(B)\,P(A \mid B)P(A∩B)=P(B)P(A∣B). Multiply along tree branches.
  • Independence: P(A∣B)=P(A)P(A \mid B) = P(A)P(A∣B)=P(A), equivalently P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B)P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B). Mutually exclusive events are dependent.
  • Bayes: P(A1∣B)=P(A1)P(B∣A1)∑iP(Ai)P(B∣Ai)P(A_1 \mid B) = \dfrac{P(A_1)P(B \mid A_1)}{\sum_i P(A_i)P(B \mid A_i)}P(A1​∣B)=∑i​P(Ai​)P(B∣Ai​)P(A1​)P(B∣A1​)​. Table: prior × likelihood = joint; sum joints; joint ÷ sum = posterior; posteriors add to 1.
  • Base rates rule: a rare hypothesis plus an imperfect signal gives a modest posterior. Use natural frequencies (“5 of every 24”).
  • Excel: COUNTIFS(…)/COUNTIF(…); Bayes in one cell =B2*C2/SUMPRODUCT(B2:B3,C2:C3); PivotTable › Show Values As › % of Column (or Row) Total.
