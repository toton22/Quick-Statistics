The manager's question

The manager's question

You've just joined a growing snack brand in Dehradun as a management trainee. On day one, three things land on your desk. A 24-question customer survey from 600 buyers that nobody has made sense of. A supplier invoice showing input prices “up a lot” since last year, and a sales head asking whether the company's 33% revenue growth is real. And a complaint from the plant that pack weights “keep drifting”.

Different problems, same need: squeeze a pile of messy numbers into a few you can act on.

Unit 5 is a toolkit. Each technique compresses many numbers into a few: many survey items into a handful of factors, thousands of customers into a few segments, a basket of prices into one index, a stream of measurements into a clear “in control or not”.

This module is examined with theory questions, so the page leans on clear ideas, pictures and how to read output. There are light numericals where they help: index numbers, control limits and Cp/Cpk are exactly the kind of short calculation that can appear inside a theory answer.

Level 1 · Basics

Six tools in plain words

1 · Factor analysis and PCA

Customers answer 30 questions, but they really only think about a few things: taste, price, convenience. These methods find those few hidden themes (factors) behind many correlated questions.

2 · Cluster analysis

Groups customers so that people in a group are alike and different groups are different. It's the statistics behind market segmentation.

3 · Index numbers

One number that says how much prices (or quantities) have changed compared with a base period, which is set to 100. The CPI is the famous one.

4 · Statistical quality control

Control charts tell you whether a process is stable or something has gone wrong; capability indices tell you whether a stable process is good enough for the customer.

5 · Survey research

How to ask good questions, measure attitudes on scales, code the answers, cross-tabulate them and show them on a dashboard.

6 · Capstone project

Putting the whole course to work: one real business problem, solved end to end in Excel, from question to dashboard and report.

Think of it like

Packing for a trip. Factor analysis puts thirty loose items into four labelled bags. Cluster analysis sorts travellers into groups who want the same trip. An index number is the single price tag on the whole bag. A control chart is the luggage scale that beeps when something's off. And a survey is how you found out what everyone wanted to pack in the first place.

Factor (component)
A hidden theme that several observed variables share, built as a weighted combination of them.
Segment (cluster)
A group of customers who are similar on the variables you chose.
Base period
The reference period whose index value is fixed at 100.
Control limits
Lines about three standard errors either side of the centre line on a control chart; points outside signal trouble.
Specification limits
What the customer or the law requires (pack weight 494–506 g). Not the same as control limits.
Scale (in surveys)
A structured way of measuring an attitude, such as a 1–5 Likert agreement item.

Level 2 · Theory · Tool 1

Factor analysis and principal component analysis

Purpose: reduce many correlated survey items to a few underlying factors, and find out what those factors mean.

Suppose a food-delivery app asks 250 customers to rate eight statements on a 1–7 agreement scale: on-time delivery, food arrives hot, rider courtesy, order accuracy, prices are fair, useful offers, fair delivery fee, value for money. (The responses here are simulated for teaching, but they behave like real survey data.) Looking at the correlations, the first four items correlate 0.49 to 0.61 with each other, and the last four 0.52 to 0.66. Across the two groups, correlations are only 0.02 to 0.26. Customers seem to be answering two questions, not eight: “how good is the delivery?” and “is it good value?”

PCA and factor analysis: the difference in one line each

Principal component analysis

Builds new variables (components) as weighted sums of the originals so that the first captures as much of the total variance as possible, the second the most of what's left, and so on. A data-reduction tool.

vs

Factor analysis

Assumes a few unobserved latent factors cause the items to correlate, and models only the variance the items share. A tool for finding constructs like “service quality”.

With clean survey data the two usually tell the same story, and many software packages start factor analysis with a principal-components extraction. For an MBA exam, know the purpose of each and how to read the output.

When can you use it? The assumptions

  • Metric items. Interval or ratio scales; multi-point Likert items are commonly treated as interval for this.
  • Enough correlation. Items must actually correlate (see Correlation); many correlations above about 0.3 is a good sign. No correlation, nothing to reduce.
  • Sample size. A common rule of thumb is at least five, and preferably ten, respondents per item, and not fewer than 100 in total. Our 250 for 8 items is comfortable.
  • KMO measure of sampling adequacy. Compares correlations with partial correlations; ranges 0 to 1. Above 0.6 is usually acceptable, above 0.8 good. Ours: 0.83.
  • Bartlett's test of sphericity. Tests whether the correlation matrix is just an identity matrix (no correlations at all). You want it significant. Ours: χ2=777.1\chi^2 = 777.1χ2=777.1, df = 28, p<0.001p \lt 0.001p<0.001.
  • Linear relationships, no extreme outliers, and no item that is a near-copy of another.

Step 2: how many factors to keep? Three common rules, and here all three agree on two:

  • Kaiser rule: keep components with eigenvalue above 1 (each explains more than a single item would). Two qualify.
  • Scree plot: plot eigenvalues and keep those before the “elbow”, where the curve flattens into rubble (scree is the loose rock at the foot of a cliff).
  • Variance explained: keep enough to explain around 60% or more. Two components give 67.3%.

Step 3: loadings. A loading is the correlation between an item and a factor. Loadings above about 0.5 in size are usually treated as “this item belongs to this factor”.

Step 4: rotation. The first, unrotated solution is often hard to read: here every item loads 0.51 to 0.74 on component 1 (a general “I like the app” factor) and component 2 contrasts delivery with price. Rotation turns the factor axes, without changing how much total variance is explained, until each item loads strongly on one factor and weakly on the others. Varimax is the standard choice and keeps the factors uncorrelated.

Step 5: communalities. An item's communality (h2h^2h2) is the share of its variance explained by the retained factors: the sum of its squared loadings. For “On-time delivery”, 0.832+0.172=0.720.83^2 + 0.17^2 = 0.720.832+0.172=0.72, which matches the 0.71 in the table once you allow for rounding of the loadings. Low communalities (below about 0.4) flag items that don't fit any factor; consider dropping them.

Step 6: name the factors. Look at which items load together and give the factor a business name: F1 = Delivery experience, F2 = Value for money. Naming is judgement, so justify it from the items.

What managers do next

Save each respondent's factor scores (their position on F1 and F2) and use them downstream: track them over time, compare cities, or feed them into cluster analysis to build segments. Two clean scores beat eight noisy, overlapping items.

Excel · how far Excel can take you

Excel has no built-in factor analysis. You can get the correlation matrix with Data › Data Analysis › Correlation and check that items correlate; the eigenvalues, loadings and rotation need an add-in (such as Real Statistics or XLSTAT) or a package like SPSS, R or Python. In the exam you're asked to read the output, not produce it.

Cluster analysis for market segmentation

Purpose: put customers into groups so that members of a group are similar and the groups are clearly different, then design a strategy for each group.

Cluster analysis doesn't need a “right answer” column; it finds structure in the data on its own. That's why it's the workhorse of segmentation, the S in STP (segmentation, targeting, positioning).

Hierarchical clustering and the dendrogram

The agglomerative method starts with every customer as their own cluster and repeatedly merges the two closest clusters until everyone is in one. You choose how to measure the distance between two groups: the closest pair (single linkage), the farthest pair (complete linkage), the average of all pairs (average linkage), or Ward's method (merge whichever pair adds least to within-group spread).

Take eight customers (hypothetical) described by visits per month and spend per visit in ₹100s: A (4, 9), B (2, 8), C (9, 1), D (9, 3), E (8, 3), F (2, 1), G (1, 3), H (2, 4). Both variables run on similar ranges here (SDs 3.46 and 2.98), so we can skip standardising.

The rule for reading a dendrogram: cut it where the vertical gap between successive merges is largest. Merging beyond that point would force together groups that are genuinely far apart.

k-means clustering, step by step

  1. Choose k. Decide the number of clusters (the elbow chart below helps).
  2. Pick k starting centroids. These seeds are often k customers chosen at random.
  3. Assign. Put every customer in the cluster of the nearest centroid.
  4. Update. Move each centroid to the mean of the customers now in it.
  5. Repeat. Go back to step 3 until nobody changes cluster.

Choosing k: the elbow method

Run k-means for k = 1, 2, 3… and plot the WCSS. It always falls as k rises (with k = 8 every customer is their own cluster and WCSS is 0), so look for the point where adding a cluster stops buying much.

Then profile each segment: its size, its average on each clustering variable, and other descriptors you didn't cluster on (age, city, channel). A segment is only useful if it's big enough, distinct, reachable and actionable.

About 60 customers by annual spend and visits. Step through k-means, then change k and watch the within-cluster SS.
  • Press Step slowly with k = 3: watch the assign and update moves alternate.
  • Set k = 2, then 5. Does WCSS fall a lot or a little each time? That's your elbow.
  • Run it a few times with new starts. Do you always get the same segments? (This is why software runs many starts.)

The Consumer Price Index in India

Indian context

India's headline CPI is compiled monthly by the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation (MoSPI), as separate Rural, Urban and Combined indices. It measures the change in retail prices of the goods and services households buy, each weighted by its share of household spending. From the January 2026 release the base year moved from 2012 to 2024 = 100, with a wider basket (358 items) and a smaller weight on food than before. The RBI's inflation target (4%, with a band of ±2%) is set on CPI-Combined.

Year-on-year inflation is just the percentage change in the index: if an index is 123.1 one year and 118.0 the year before, inflation is (123.1−118.0)/118.0×100=4.3%(123.1 - 118.0)/118.0 \times 100 = 4.3\%(123.1−118.0)/118.0×100=4.3%.

Level 2 · Theory · Tool 4

Statistical quality control

Every process varies. The job of statistical process control is to tell normal variation from a real problem, quickly and with few false alarms.

Common (chance) causes

The many small, unavoidable sources of variation built into the process: tiny differences in raw material, temperature, operator timing. A process with only common causes is in statistical control. Don't tinker with it; that makes things worse.

vs

Assignable (special) causes

Something specific that has changed: a worn nozzle, a new supplier's batch, an untrained operator on night shift. These show up as signals on a control chart. Find them and fix them.

Reading out-of-control signals

  • A point outside the control limits: the primary signal. Investigate at once.
  • A run on one side: a long string of points (seven or eight in a row is a common rule) all above or all below the centre line, even inside the limits, suggests the mean has shifted.
  • A trend: six or seven points in a row steadily rising or falling, typical of tool wear or operator fatigue (Anderson et al., Ch. 19).
  • Cycles or other patterns: shift changes, day-of-week effects.
  • Hugging the centre line: almost no variation can mean the limits were computed wrongly or the data are being “tidied”.

Control limits are not specification limits

Control limits come from the process (the “voice of the process”); specification limits come from the customer (the “voice of the customer”). Never draw spec limits on an x̄ chart: the chart plots sample means, which vary much less than individual packs.

Six Sigma and DMAIC

Six Sigma is a quality-improvement approach, made famous by Motorola and General Electric, that aims for processes so good that there are no more than 3.4 defects per million opportunities (DPMO). The 3.4 comes from a convention: if the nearest spec limit is six standard deviations from the mean, and the mean is allowed to drift by 1.5σ over time, the remaining gap is 4.5σ, and the normal tail beyond 4.5σ is 3.4 per million.

Designing and analysing survey research

A survey is only as good as its questions. Most survey errors are made at the design stage and can't be fixed in Excel afterwards.

Questionnaire design principles

  1. Start from the objectives. Every question should map to a research objective (step 1 of the research process). If you can't say how you'll analyse an answer, cut the question.
  2. Use plain, specific words. “How many times did you order food online in the last 30 days?” beats “Do you order often?”
  3. One idea per question. “Was delivery fast and polite?” is double-barrelled: what if it was fast but rude?
  4. Don't lead or load. “Don't you agree our prices are fair?” pushes the answer. So do emotionally loaded words.
  5. Make options exhaustive and non-overlapping. Age bands 18–25, 26–40, 41+, not 18–25, 25–40.
  6. Order with care. Easy, general questions first; sensitive ones (income) near the end; screening questions at the very start; avoid earlier questions colouring later ones.
  7. Keep it short, and pilot it. Test it on 10–20 people from the target group. If you translate it (Hindi and English, say), translate it back to check the meaning survived.

Question types

Open-ended

“What one thing would make you order more often?” Rich and unexpected, but slow to code and analyse.

Closed: dichotomous and multiple choice

Yes/No; pick one channel (App / Website / Store); or tick all that apply. Quick to answer and easy to tabulate.

Rating scales

Agreement, satisfaction or likelihood on a 5- or 7-point scale; 0–10 “how likely are you to recommend us?”

Ranking and allocation

Put options in order, or split points between them. Forces trade-offs that rating scales let people dodge.

Scaling techniques

Each format produces data on a particular measurement scale, which decides what you can compute later (see the four scales).

Likert scale
Degree of agreement with statements, usually 5 or 7 points from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”. Several items are often summed into a scale score.
Semantic differential
A 7-point scale between two opposite adjectives (old-fashioned … modern). Good for brand image profiles.
Rank order
Respondents order options from most to least preferred. Ordinal data: you know the order, not the gaps.
Constant sum
Respondents divide a fixed total (100 points) across attributes. Shows relative importance, and how much more one matters than another.

Coding responses and the codebook

Coding turns answers into numbers you can analyse; the codebook is the dictionary that says what each code means. Without one, your file is unreadable to anyone else, and to you in three weeks.

Nuance, pitfalls and depth

Factor analysis

  • KMO wording. Kaiser's labels are handy in reports: 0.90s marvellous, 0.80s meritorious, 0.70s middling, 0.60s mediocre, 0.50s miserable, below 0.50 unacceptable. Our 0.83 is meritorious.
  • Orthogonal or oblique rotation? Varimax forces the factors to be uncorrelated. If factors are likely to be related (people who like the delivery may also feel it's better value), an oblique rotation such as promax or oblimin can fit better.
  • Reliability of each factor Beyond the syllabus: before summing a factor's items into a score, check internal consistency with Cronbach's alpha (0.7 or more is the usual bar). For our simulated survey, alpha is 0.82 for the delivery items and 0.84 for the value items.
  • Garbage in, garbage out. If the questionnaire didn't include an important theme, no factor analysis will find it.

Cluster analysis

  • k-means depends on the starting seeds; software runs many starts and keeps the lowest WCSS. Our elbow chart reports the best over every possible set of seeds.
  • It's pulled about by outliers and by unscaled variables. Standardise, and consider removing or capping extreme customers first.
  • Hierarchical clustering is easy to explain with a dendrogram but slow for tens of thousands of customers; a common workflow uses it on a sample to pick k, then k-means on everyone.
  • Statistics can't name a segment. The business test (big enough, reachable, different response to marketing) decides whether it's worth having.

Index numbers

  • Substitution bias. When a price rises, people buy less of it. Laspeyres keeps the old quantities, so it tends to overstate the cost-of-living rise; Paasche tends to understate it. Fisher sits between and passes the “time reversal test” (going forward then back gets you to 100).
  • Rebasing. To move an index to a new base year, divide every value by the new base year's value and multiply by 100.
  • Which index? CPI measures retail prices paid by households; the Wholesale Price Index tracks prices in bulk trade; core inflation strips out volatile items like food and fuel. Use the one that matches what you're deflating.

Quality control

  • Three-sigma limits mean a stable process puts a point outside by pure chance about 0.27% of the time, roughly once in 370 samples. Adding run rules catches small shifts sooner but raises false alarms.
  • Samples should be rational subgroups: items made close together in time, so variation within a sample is common-cause only.
  • Set limits on in-control data (phase I), then freeze them and monitor (phase II). Recomputing limits every week hides slow drifts.
  • The 1.5σ shift behind “3.4 DPMO” is a convention, not a law of nature. Quote it as such.

Surveys

  • Watch for acquiescence (agreeing with everything), social desirability (giving the “nice” answer) and non-response bias. Reverse-coded items and anonymity help.
  • Response rate matters as much as sample size: 400 replies from 4,000 invitations is a 10% response, and the 90% who didn't reply may differ.
  • The sample size you need comes from the margin of error you can live with; see the Estimation page. How you pick respondents is on the Sampling page.
How a consultant would present it

Lead with the business meaning, not the technique: “Your 600 customers fall into three groups; the value-hunters are 41% of buyers but only 22% of revenue” beats “k-means with k = 3 gave WCSS of …”. The method goes in a single appendix slide (variables used, standardised or not, how k was chosen, stability checked).

Interview insight

Asked “what's the difference between control limits and specification limits?”, most candidates fumble. Say: control limits describe what the process does (from data, about ±3 standard errors); spec limits describe what the customer needs (from the design). A process can be in control but not capable, or capable but out of control.

Metropolitan Research, Inc.

A consumer-research firm checks owners' complaints about early transmission failures against real repair records.

The situation

Metropolitan Research is a consumer-research organisation that runs surveys on all kinds of products and services. In one study it sent a questionnaire to owners of a full-size car made by a big Detroit manufacturer. The survey kept turning up the same complaint: transmissions failing early.

Complaints are opinions, so the firm went after hard evidence. A transmission-repair business in the Detroit area supplied a sample of actual repair records, giving the number of miles each car had been driven when its transmission failed. Now the question is whether those records back up what owners said in the survey, and how precisely the firm can pin down the typical mileage at failure.

Seen as survey research, this is a classic two-stage design: a questionnaire identifies the problem, and a second, behavioural data source sizes it. Notice the frame, though: one repair shop, and only cars whose transmissions have already failed.

What the manager wants

  1. Summarise the miles-at-failure data with suitable descriptive statistics.
  2. Build a 95% confidence interval for the mean miles driven before failure, for the population of these cars whose transmissions failed, and explain what it means for management.
  3. Discuss what the results say about owners' belief that some transmissions failed early.
  4. Work out how many repair records would be needed to estimate the mean with a margin of error of 5,000 miles at 95% confidence.
  5. Say what other information you'd want to judge the transmission problem properly.

Exam-style theory questions

Ten model questions in the 2-, 5- and 10-mark styles. Attempt each before you open the model answer. In longer answers, a labelled diagram (scree plot, dendrogram, control chart) earns marks.

What is an eigenvalue in principal component analysis? What does the “eigenvalue greater than 1” rule mean?

Model answer

An eigenvalue is the amount of total variance a component explains, in units of “one standardised variable”. With ppp items the eigenvalues add to ppp. The Kaiser rule keeps components with eigenvalue above 1, i.e. those that explain more than a single original item. (E.g. eigenvalues 3.39 and 1.99 for 8 items: keep two, explaining 67.3%.)

Distinguish between common causes and assignable causes of variation.

Model answer

Common causes are the many small, random sources of variation inherent in a process (slight material or temperature differences); a process with only these is in statistical control. Assignable causes are specific, identifiable changes (a worn tool, a bad batch, an untrained operator) that show up as signals on a control chart and should be found and removed.

Define a price relative. The price of a kilogram of rice rose from ₹90 to ₹104. Find its price relative.

Model answer

Explain the purpose of factor analysis and the conditions under which it is appropriate. How are the KMO measure and Bartlett's test used?

Model answer
  • Purpose: data reduction and discovery of underlying constructs: many correlated variables (survey items) are summarised by a few factors, which can then be named and used as scores (e.g. 8 delivery-app items → “delivery experience” and “value for money”).
  • Conditions: metric (interval) items; substantial correlations among items (many above 0.3); adequate sample (rule of thumb 5–10 respondents per item, at least 100); linear relationships; no extreme outliers or duplicate items.
  • KMO (0 to 1) compares observed correlations with partial correlations; values above about 0.6 are acceptable and above 0.8 good, meaning the data are suitable for factoring.
  • Bartlett's test of sphericity tests H₀: the correlation matrix is an identity matrix (items uncorrelated). A significant result (p < 0.05) means there is enough correlation to proceed.

Describe the k-means clustering procedure. How would you decide the number of clusters?

Model answer
  1. Choose k and standardise the variables if units differ.
  2. Select k initial centroids (seeds).
  3. Assign each case to the nearest centroid by Euclidean distance.
  4. Recompute each centroid as the mean of its members.
  5. Repeat until assignments stop changing; report the within-cluster sum of squares (WCSS).

Choosing k: plot WCSS against k (elbow method) and choose the k after which WCSS falls only slightly (e.g. 145.9, 54.1, 11.2, 6.8: choose 3); a hierarchical dendrogram's largest jump gives a second opinion; and the clusters must make business sense (size, distinctiveness, actionability). Run several starts because results depend on the seeds.

Compare the Laspeyres, Paasche and Fisher price indexes. How is a price index used to deflate a value series?

  • Paasche =∑ptqt/∑p0qt×100= \sum p_t q_t / \sum p_0 q_t \times 100=∑pt​qt​/∑p0​qt​×100: current quantities; reflects today's basket but needs new quantity data each period; tends to understate.
  • Fisher =L×P= \sqrt{L \times P}=L×P​: geometric mean of the two; a compromise (“ideal” index).
  • Example basket: L = 106.4, P = 105.6, F = 106.0.
  • Deflating: real value = nominal value ÷ price index × 100. Sales of ₹160 crore with index 123.1 (base 100) are ₹130 crore in base-year prices: nominal growth of 33.3% from ₹120 crore is only 8.3% real growth.

State the main principles of questionnaire design and explain any three scaling techniques used in survey research.

Model answer

Principles: tie every question to an objective; simple, specific wording; one idea per question (no double-barrelled items); no leading or loaded wording; exhaustive, mutually exclusive options; logical order (easy and general first, sensitive last); brevity; pilot test (and back-translation if translated).

Scales: Likert, agreement with statements on 5/7 points, summed into scale scores; semantic differential, 7 points between bipolar adjectives (old-fashioned … modern), used for image profiles; rank order, options ranked 1, 2, 3 …, giving ordinal data and forcing trade-offs; constant sum, 100 points divided across attributes, showing relative importance (e.g. price 40, speed 30, pack design 17, eco packaging 13).

Explain the construction and interpretation of x̄, R and p control charts. Distinguish between a process being in control and being capable, with reference to Cp, Cpk and Six Sigma.

  • x̄ and R charts (variables data, samples of size n): CL = xˉˉ\bar{\bar{x}}xˉˉ, limits xˉˉ±A2Rˉ\bar{\bar{x}} \pm A_2\bar{R}xˉˉ±A2​Rˉ; R chart CL = Rˉ\bar{R}Rˉ, limits D3RˉD_3\bar{R}D3​Rˉ, D4RˉD_4\bar{R}D4​Rˉ.   • Interpretation: point beyond limits; runs of 7–8 on one side; trends of 6–7; cycles; hugging the centre line. Investigate, remove the cause, recompute limits.

A retail chain wants to segment its customers using cluster analysis. Explain how you would carry out the study, covering choice of variables, distance measures, standardisation, hierarchical and k-means methods, choosing the number of clusters and profiling the segments.

Model answer
  1. Objective and variables: segment for targeted offers; choose behavioural variables (spend, frequency, recency, category mix) and possibly attitudinal factor scores; avoid many highly correlated variables (use factor scores instead).
  2. Data preparation: clean, handle missing values, treat outliers; standardise (z-scores) because variables are in different units (₹ vs visits).
  3. Distance: Euclidean dij=∑(xik−xjk)2d_{ij} = \sqrt{\sum (x_{ik}-x_{jk})^2}dij​=∑(xik​−xjk​)2​ (or Manhattan); e.g. (4, 9) and (2, 8) are 5=2.24\sqrt{5} = 2.245​=2.24 apart.
  4. Hierarchical clustering (agglomerative; single, complete, average or Ward linkage), shown as a dendrogram; cut at the largest jump in merge distance to suggest k.
  5. k-means with that k: seeds, assign, update, repeat; several random starts.
  6. Choosing k: elbow in WCSS, dendrogram, and managerial usefulness.
  7. Profiling and validation: size and mean of each variable by segment; demographics; name segments (e.g. “occasional big baskets”, “frequent top-up”, “light”); check stability on a holdout sample.
  8. Action: targeting and positioning for each segment; track segment movement over time.

You are asked to design and analyse a customer-satisfaction survey for a food-delivery app and present the findings to management. Describe the full process, from questionnaire design to an Excel dashboard.

Model answer
  1. Objectives and information needs (satisfaction drivers, channel preference, segments).
  2. Questionnaire: design principles (plain words, no double-barrelled or leading items, logical order); question types (closed, rating, multi-select, one open question); scales (Likert items for drivers, a semantic differential for brand image, constant sum for attribute importance); reverse-coded items; consent statement; pilot test.
  3. Sampling: population of active users, frame from the app database, stratified random sample by city; target response and sample size from the desired margin of error.
  4. Coding: codebook with variable names, codes and missing-value rules; multi-select as 0/1 columns; reverse items recoded as 6 − x; open answers coded into themes.
  5. Cleaning in Excel: duplicates, straight-lining, blanks, validation.
  6. Analysis: frequencies and top-two-box %, medians; cross-tabs with row percentages (e.g. app preference 62% among 18–25 vs 25.7% among 41+) and a chi-square test; factor analysis of the driver items if many.
  7. Dashboard: PivotTables and PivotCharts, KPI tiles, slicers for city and age, one message per chart.
  8. Report: executive summary with recommendations, key charts, method and limitations (non-response, self-report bias).

A PCA of eight survey items gives eigenvalues 3.39, 1.99, 0.54, 0.50, 0.46, 0.44, 0.35, 0.33. Using the Kaiser rule, how many components should be kept?

  1. 1
  2. 2
  3. 3
  4. 8
Keep components with eigenvalue above 1: 3.39 and 1.99. The scree plot's elbow after component 2 agrees, and the two explain 67.3% of the variance.

In k-means clustering, what happens in the “update” step?

  1. Each centroid moves to the mean of the customers currently assigned to it
  2. Each centroid moves to the customer closest to it
  3. The two closest clusters are merged
  4. The number of clusters k is increased by one
k-means alternates assign (nearest centroid) and update (centroid = mean of members). Merging the closest clusters is what hierarchical clustering does.

The Laspeyres price index weights each item's price by:

  1. Current-period quantities
  2. Nothing: all items count equally
  3. Base-period quantities
  4. The average of base and current quantities
Laspeyres = Σ ptq0 / Σ p0q0 × 100: what the base basket would cost now. Paasche uses current quantities; the simple aggregate uses no weights.

Samples of n = 5 give a grand mean of 50 mm and an average range of 4 mm. With A₂ = 0.577, the UCL of the x̄ chart is:

A process has Cp = 1.5 but Cpk = 0.8. What should the manager conclude?

  1. The process spread is too wide for the specification
  2. The spread is fine, but the process mean is off-centre
  3. The process is out of statistical control
  4. The process is centred and highly capable
Cp looks only at spread versus the spec width, and 1.5 is comfortable. Cpk is much lower, so the mean sits close to one spec limit. Re-centre the process. Capability says nothing about whether the chart shows control.
