ANOVA

Compare three or more group means in one test: is the gap between the groups big compared with the scatter inside them?

The manager's question

The manager's question

A retail chain tries three store layouts in 15 similar stores, five stores per layout. After a month, average weekly sales are ₹24 lakh (Layout A), ₹29 lakh (Layout B) and ₹25 lakh (Layout C). The regional head wants Layout B in every store, starting Monday.

The CFO asks the obvious question: are these real differences, or just the normal week-to-week wobble you'd see between any 15 stores?

Comparing two means is a t-test (see Hypothesis tests). With three or more groups you need ANOVA, the analysis of variance. Odd name for a test about means, but you'll see why in a minute.

ANOVA compares the spread between group means (the signal) with the spread inside the groups (the noise). If the signal is many times the noise, the means really differ.

How much of this is examinable

Light coverage ANOVA (single- and two-factor tools in the ToolPak) is listed in Syllabus Module 3, but it is not covered in class slides; examinable at concept level as per syllabus. Know the idea, the hypotheses, how to fill and read an ANOVA table, and the F decision. The Pro section is for curiosity.

Level 1 · Basics

Signal versus noise

First, why not just run t-tests on every pair? Because each test carries its own 5% risk of a false alarm, and those risks pile up.

Three layouts means three pairs (A–B, A–C, B–C), so three t-tests. Five groups would need ten. If each test has α = 0.05, the chance that at least one of them cries wolf grows fast:

So ANOVA asks one question about all the groups at once: is there any difference among these means?

Think of it like

Three sections of the same course write the same exam. Section B averages 5 marks more. If everyone in each section scored within a mark or two of their section average, that 5-mark gap is obviously real. If marks inside every section ranged from 10 to 45, a 5-mark gap tells you almost nothing. You judge the gap against the scatter. That's ANOVA.

Between-group variation
How far the group means sit from the grand mean. The signal. Measured by SSB.
Within-group variation
How much units in the same group differ from each other. The noise. Measured by SSW.
F ratio
Signal per degree of freedom divided by noise per degree of freedom: MSB ÷ MSW.

Splitting the total variation

Take every store's sales, find how far it is from the grand mean, square it and add up. That's the total sum of squares. ANOVA's trick is that this total splits exactly into two parts: the part explained by the groups, and the leftover inside the groups.

Why F works

Here's the logic, in two lines. MSW estimates the natural store-to-store variance σ², whatever H₀ says. MSB also estimates σ², but only if H₀ is true. If the layouts really differ, the group means spread out and MSB gets inflated.

So when H₀ is true, F = MSB/MSW should be around 1. A big F (well above 1) says the group means are further apart than noise alone would produce. Same means can give very different verdicts depending on the noise:

The F distribution and the decision

F can't be negative and it is skewed right. Its shape depends on two df: numerator k−1k-1k−1 and denominator nT−kn_T-knT​−k. The test is always right-tailed: only a big F is evidence against H₀.

Compute F = MSB ÷ MSW with (k − 1, nT − k) df
Is F > F crit (or p ≤ α)?
No
Do not reject H₀. No evidence the group means differ. Stop here.
Yes
Reject H₀. At least one mean differs. Run a post-hoc test to find which.

Assumptions

IndependenceEach observation is separate: different stores, randomly assigned layouts. The one you can't fix afterwards.
NormalityWithin each group the response is roughly normal. ANOVA tolerates mild skew, especially with equal group sizes.
Equal variancesGroups have similar spread. Rule of thumb: largest SD ÷ smallest SD below 2. Ours: 2.12 ÷ 1.58 = 1.34. Fine.

Explore: pull the means apart, add noise

Starts with the 15 stores from Worked example 1 (weekly sales, ₹ lakh). Change the gap between means and the spread inside groups.
  • Shrink the difference between group means towards zero. Watch SSB and F fall. At what point does p cross 0.05?
  • Keep the means fixed and raise the spread within groups. Find the spread at which F drops below F crit (3.885). That's Figure 4 happening live.
  • Check the identity every time: does SSB + SSW always equal SST in the table?
Level 3 · Application

Where managers use ANOVA

Retail

Layouts, displays, formats

Do three store formats sell different amounts per week? Randomly assign formats to comparable stores, then ANOVA.

HR

Training methods

Classroom vs e-learning vs on-the-job: does average productivity differ? If not, choose the cheapest.

Marketing

Ad creatives

Three creatives, randomly shown: does average order value differ by creative? (For click-through rates, a proportion, use chi-square instead.)

Operations

Suppliers and shifts

Compare mean delivery lead time across four suppliers, or mean defects per batch across three shifts.

Finance

Fund categories

Do average annual returns differ across large-, mid- and small-cap funds? Watch the equal-variance assumption: small caps are usually more volatile.

Hospitality

Guest satisfaction

Average satisfaction score across four hotel properties. If scores are on a 1–5 scale, the rank-based Kruskal–Wallis test is a safer cross-check.

Indian context

Suppose a quick-commerce company promises “groceries in 20 minutes” in Bengaluru, Hyderabad and Pune. Before blaming one city's dark-store team, the operations head checks whether mean delivery times really differ, or whether the gap is within normal order-to-order variation.

Level 4 · Pro

After the F test, and beyond one factor

Which means differ? Post-hoc comparisons

Two-factor ANOVA and interaction Light coverage

Suppose the chain also suspects layouts work differently in metro and small-town stores. Now there are two factors: layout (3 levels) and city type (2 levels). A two-factor ANOVA tests three things at once: a layout effect, a city-type effect, and an interaction, which asks whether the layout effect depends on city type.

If the interaction is significant, don't report a single “best layout”. Report the best layout for each city type. In Excel, use Anova: Two-Factor With Replication; it prints rows for Sample (one factor), Columns (the other), Interaction and Within. Anderson's Case Problem 2 in Ch. 13, Compensation for Sales Professionals (p. 592), is a good two-factor practice case.

More connections worth knowing

  • Two groups only? ANOVA's F equals the square of the pooled t statistic. Layouts A vs B: t = −4.226, t² = 17.86 = F, same p = 0.0029. So a t-test is a special case of ANOVA.
  • Unequal variances? Welch's ANOVA handles them (not in the ToolPak). Beyond the syllabus
  • Ordinal or badly skewed data? The rank-based Kruskal–Wallis test is the non-parametric cousin. For the store data it gives H = 9.07, p = 0.011: same conclusion. Beyond the syllabus
  • Checking equal variances formally: Levene's test (Levine §11.1). Store data: p = 0.75, so no evidence the variances differ.

How a consultant would present it

Headline: “Layout B lifts weekly sales by ₹4–5 lakh per store versus A and C.”

Evidence: 15 stores randomly assigned; one-way ANOVA F(2, 12) = 11.05, p = 0.002; layout explains 65% of the sales variation. Tukey comparisons: B differs from A and from C; A and C do not differ.

Caveats: one month, five stores per layout, all urban. Check for a city-type interaction before a chain-wide roll-out.

Interview question

“Why use ANOVA instead of several t-tests?” Strong answer: one test for “any difference” keeps the overall Type I error at α; many t-tests inflate it (three tests at 5% give about 14%). Then, if F is significant, use Tukey to find which pairs differ.

Quick check

Why do we use one ANOVA rather than three separate t-tests to compare three group means?

  1. t-tests can't be used with small samples
  2. Several t-tests at 5% each push the overall chance of a false alarm well above 5%
  3. ANOVA tells you which group has the largest mean
  4. ANOVA does not need any assumptions
Three tests at α = 0.05 give roughly 1 − 0.95³ = 14% chance of at least one false alarm. ANOVA asks one question and keeps the risk at α. It doesn't identify the best group (that's post-hoc), and it does have assumptions.

Four packaging designs are each tested in 6 stores (24 stores in all). What are the degrees of freedom for the F test?

  1. (4, 24)
  2. (3, 23)
  3. (4, 20)
  4. (3, 20)
Between df = k − 1 = 4 − 1 = 3. Within df = nT − k = 24 − 4 = 20. (23 is the total df.)

An ANOVA with k = 3 groups and nT = 18 observations has SSB = 60 and SSW = 90. What is F?

  1. 5.0
  2. 0.67
  3. 10.0
  4. 1.5
MSB = 60/2 = 30; MSW = 90/(18 − 3) = 90/15 = 6; F = 30/6 = 5.0. With F crit(2, 15) = 3.68 at 5%, you'd reject H₀ (p = 0.022).

A one-way ANOVA comparing mean delivery time across five suppliers gives p = 0.002. The correct conclusion at α = 0.05 is:

  1. All five suppliers have different mean delivery times
  2. The supplier with the lowest sample mean is significantly fastest
  3. At least one supplier's mean delivery time differs from the others
  4. The suppliers' delivery times have equal variances
Rejecting H₀ means “not all means are equal”. To say which suppliers differ, run Tukey or Fisher's LSD. ANOVA's result says nothing about the variances; equal variances is an assumption you check separately.

Which of these is NOT an assumption of one-way ANOVA?

  1. Observations are independent
  2. The response is roughly normal within each group
  3. The groups have similar variances
  4. Every group has the same sample size
Unequal group sizes are allowed (the formulas use nj for each group). Equal sizes just make ANOVA more forgiving when the other assumptions are a little off.

ANOVA in 60 seconds

  • Use it to compare the means of 3 or more groups in one test (many t-tests inflate the false-alarm rate: 1−0.95m1-0.95^m1−0.95m).
  • H₀: all means equal. H₁: not all equal.
  • SST = SSB + SSW. SSB=∑nj(xˉj−xˉˉ)2\text{SSB} = \sum n_j(\bar{x}_j-\bar{\bar{x}})^2SSB=∑nj​(xˉj​−xˉˉ)2, SSW=∑∑(xij−xˉj)2=∑(nj−1)sj2\text{SSW} = \sum\sum(x_{ij}-\bar{x}_j)^2 = \sum(n_j-1)s_j^2SSW=∑∑(xij​−xˉj​)2=∑(nj​−1)sj2​.

  • Decide: F > F crit or p ≤ α → reject H₀ → post-hoc (Tukey / Fisher LSD) to find which pairs differ.
  • Assume: independence, normal within groups, similar variances (SD ratio < 2).
  • Excel: Data › Data Analysis › Anova: Single Factor · =F.DIST.RT(F,df1,df2) · =F.INV.RT(α,df1,df2)
  • Write it like this: “F(2, 12) = 11.05, p = 0.002, so we reject H₀: mean weekly sales differ across layouts. Layout explains 65% of the variation; Tukey shows Layout B is higher than A and C.”
