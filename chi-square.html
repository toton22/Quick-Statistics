Chi-square

Are two categories linked, or is the pattern in your cross-tab just chance? The chi-square test of independence, step by step, in Excel and by hand.

The manager's question

The manager's question

Your company has bought licences for an AI assistant. HR surveyed 1,440 employees: 64% of the under-30s use it, but only 45% of those aged 50 and above. The L&D head wants to split the training budget by age group, with extra hand-holding for senior staff.

Before you approve it: is that gap real, or the kind of difference you'd get from any random sample? If age and AI use are unrelated, one training plan fits everyone. If they're linked, the money should follow the gap.

The chi-square test compares the counts you observed with the counts you'd expect if the two categories had nothing to do with each other. Small gaps: chance. Big gaps: a real link.

Level 1 · Basics

The idea in plain words

So far in this pillar, both variables were numbers (ad spend, sales). Many business questions are about categories instead: male or female, North or South, prepaid or postpaid, placed or not placed. You can't average “North”. You can only count how many people fall in each box.

  • Does product preference differ between men and women?
  • Do customers in different regions choose different mobile plans?
  • Does an MBA's specialisation affect whether they get placed?
  • Do cash-on-delivery orders get returned more often than prepaid ones?

Each of these is the same question in disguise: are two categorical variables independent, or associated?

Think of it like

A wedding caterer knows that 60% of the 500 guests are vegetarian. At any table of 10 she expects about 6 veg plates, no matter whether it's a bride's-side table or a groom's-side table. That's what independent means: knowing the side tells you nothing about the food. Now suppose the groom's-side tables keep asking for 9 veg plates out of 10. That's too far from 6 to be luck. Side and food choice are associated. The chi-square test is the caterer's arithmetic, done properly.

The data sit in a contingency table (also called a cross-tab or two-way table): one row per category of the first variable, one column per category of the second, and a count in every cell. Here's the class example, drawn in 3D so you can see the shape.

Categorical variable
A variable whose values are labels, not measurements: region, gender, plan type, yes/no.
Contingency table
A grid of counts for two categorical variables. An r × c table has r rows and c columns (rows are named first).
Cell / observed count, O
The number of people in one row-and-column combination. Example: 312 under-30s use AI.
Marginal totals
The row totals and column totals “in the margins”. The grand total is n.
Expected count, E
The count a cell would have if the two variables were completely unrelated, given the same totals.
Independent / associated
Independent: the share in each column is the same for every row. Associated: at least one row behaves differently.

The whole test fits in five moves.

1 · HypothesesH₀: independent. H₁: associated.
2 · ExpectedE = row total × column total ÷ n for every cell
3 · Gaps(O − E)² ÷ E for every cell, then add them up: χ²
4 · Benchmarkdf = (r − 1)(c − 1); critical value or p-value
5 · DecideReject H₀ if χ² is beyond the cut-off; say it in business words
Level 2 · Theory

Formulas and how they work

The hypotheses

H₀ · independence

Age group and AI use are independent. The share of AI users is the same in every age group; the gaps in the sample are chance.

vs

H₁ · association

Age group and AI use are associated. At least one age group uses AI at a different rate.

Notice what H₁ does not say. It doesn't say which group is higher, or by how much. The test only answers “link or no link”. You find the direction afterwards by looking at the cells (see Pro).

Why this formula? Independence means probabilities multiply: P(row and column) = P(row) × P(column). The chance a random employee is under 30 is 490/1,440; the chance they use AI is 809/1,440. If the two are independent, the chance of both is the product. Multiply by n to turn a probability into a count:

E=n×Rn×Cn=R×CnE = n \times \frac{R}{n} \times \frac{C}{n} = \frac{R \times C}{n}E=n×nR​×nC​=nR×C​

For the first cell: EUnder 30, Yes=490×8091440=275.28E_{\text{Under 30, Yes}} = \frac{490 \times 809}{1440} = 275.28EUnder 30, Yes​=1440490×809​=275.28

So under H₀ we'd expect 275 AI users among the under-30s. We observed 312. That's 37 more than “no link” predicts. The other five cells work the same way.

Self-check that saves marks

Your expected table must have exactly the same row and column totals as the observed table (give or take rounding). If a row of E doesn't add to its row total, you've made an arithmetic slip.

The χ² statistic: add up the surprises

Chi-square test statisticExam formula
χ2=∑all cells(O−E)2E\chi^2 = \sum_{\text{all cells}} \frac{(O - E)^2}{E}χ2=all cells∑​E(O−E)2​

“chi-square” (chi rhymes with “sky”); always 0 or positive
  • Square the gap so that “more than expected” and “fewer than expected” both count as surprises (otherwise they'd cancel to zero).
  • Divide by E so each gap is judged relative to the size of its cell. A gap of 10 is shocking when you expected 5, and trivial when you expected 500.

Decide: two routes to the same answer

If H₀ were true, χ² would follow a known curve, the chi-square distribution with df degrees of freedom. It starts at 0, is skewed right, and its shape depends on df. A χ² far out in the right tail means the gaps are too big to blame on chance.

Critical-value route (pen and paper)

Find χα2\chi^2_{\alpha}χα2​ in the χ² table for your df and α. Reject H₀ if χ² > critical value. Class example: df = 2, α = 0.05 → 5.991. Since 37.26 > 5.991, reject.

Excel: =CHISQ.INV.RT(0.05,2) → 5.991

p-value route (software)

p = the area to the right of your χ². Reject H₀ if p < α. Class example: p = 0.000000008, far below 0.05, so reject.

Excel: =CHISQ.DIST.RT(37.26,2) → 8.1E−09, or =CHISQ.TEST(observed,expected) directly.

Exam tip: it's always a right-tailed test

χ² can only grow when observed and expected disagree, in either direction. So the rejection region is only in the right tail, and you use the full α there. Never halve α for a chi-square test of independence.

Conditions to check before you trust the answer

  1. Counts, not percentages or averages. The test needs the actual number of people in each cell.
  2. Each person counted once. Every observation falls in exactly one cell. Multiple-answer survey questions break this.
  3. A random sample (or a reasonable stand-in for one).
  4. Expected counts large enough. The class and Anderson's rule: every expected count should be at least 5. (Some books relax this: no more than 20% of cells below 5, and none below 1.) The check is on E, not on O. The campus-placement case in the class deck is built to test exactly this.

Another goodness-of-fit example: testing a market-share claim

A tea company (hypothetical) claims its three brands hold 45%, 30% and 25% of a city's market. A survey of 200 shoppers finds 72, 74 and 54 buyers. Expected: 200 × 0.45 = 90, 200 × 0.30 = 60, 200 × 0.25 = 50.

χ2=(72−90)290+(74−60)260+(54−50)250=3.600+3.267+0.320=7.187\chi^2 = \frac{(72-90)^2}{90} + \frac{(74-60)^2}{60} + \frac{(54-50)^2}{50} = 3.600 + 3.267 + 0.320 = 7.187χ2=90(72−90)2​+60(74−60)2​+50(54−50)2​=3.600+3.267+0.320=7.187, df = 3 − 1 = 2, critical value 5.991.

7.19 > 5.991 → reject the claim (p = 0.028). The second brand is doing better than claimed (37% of the sample vs 30%) and the first worse (36% vs 45%). Excel: =CHISQ.TEST({72,74,54},{90,60,50}) → 0.028.

Level 3 · Application

Where managers use it

Marketing

Segment × preference

Gender, age band or city × preferred variant, pack size or ad recall. Tells you whether one campaign fits all segments or you need separate creatives.

Telecom & operations

Region × plan or defect type

Region × plan mix decides where to push premium plans. In a factory, shift × defect type shows whether the night shift makes a different kind of mistake.

HR

Department × attrition

Stayed/left by department, or training attended × promoted. A significant link tells HR where to dig; the residuals tell them which department.

Banking

Customer age × channel

Branch, app, UPI or phone banking by age band. Drives branch staffing and app-feature priorities.

Hospitality

Guest type × room choice

Business vs leisure guests × room category or add-ons booked. Feeds pricing and package design.

Education & placements

Specialisation × outcome

The class Case 10. Placement offices use this to answer “does my specialisation matter?” honestly, assumptions included.

Indian context

Many Indian online shoppers still choose cash on delivery, especially outside the metros, and sellers often suspect that COD orders come back more. A chi-square test turns that suspicion into evidence one way or the other, using the seller's own order data. The mini-case below shows how.

Mini-case: do COD orders get returned more?

An online fashion seller (hypothetical) checks 1,500 recent orders. Of 600 COD orders, 90 were returned. Of 900 prepaid orders, 60 were returned.

Level 4 · Pro

Nuance, pitfalls and depth

Chi-square says “linked”, not “how”: read the residuals

A significant χ² is like a smoke alarm: it tells you there's a fire somewhere in the table, not which room. To find the room, compute each cell's standardised residual, (O−E)/E(O-E)/\sqrt{E}(O−E)/E​. Cells beyond about ±2 are the ones driving the result, and the sign tells you the direction.

Squaring a residual gives that cell's χ² contribution: 2.212=4.902.21^2 = 4.902.212=4.90, the first entry on slide 7. Software such as R and SPSS reports a slightly stricter “adjusted” residual; the deck's software slide mentions it.

How strong is the link? Cramér's V Light coverage

The p-value tells you whether a link is real; it says nothing about how big it is. With 1,440 people, even a modest difference becomes “highly significant”. Cramér's V rescales χ² to a 0-to-1 strength measure.

Cramér's VLight
V=χ2n (k−1),k=min⁡(r,c)V = \sqrt{\frac{\chi^2}{n\,(k-1)}}, \qquad k = \min(r, c)V=n(k−1)χ2​​,k=min(r,c)
VVV
0 = no association, 1 = perfect association

the smaller of the number of rows and columns

Class example: V=37.26/(1440×1)=0.16V = \sqrt{37.26/(1440 \times 1)} = 0.16V=37.26/(1440×1)​=0.16. Fragrance example: V=13.66/200=0.26V = \sqrt{13.66/200} = 0.26V=13.66/200​=0.26. COD example: V=27.78/1500=0.14V = \sqrt{27.78/1500} = 0.14V=27.78/1500​=0.14. A common rough guide for tables with k = 2 is about 0.1 small, 0.3 medium and 0.5 large. So the age–AI link is real (p ≈ 0.000000008) but modest in size. Both facts belong in the report.

Small expected counts: what to do when the rule fails

The χ² curve is an approximation, and it gets poor when expected counts are small. If some E are below 5 (the class rule), you have three options:

  1. Merge categories that make sense together (e.g. HR with another people-facing specialisation, or “6–6.9 hours” with “fewer than 6”), then retest.
  2. Collect more data so the expected counts grow.
  3. Use an exact test Beyond the syllabus: Fisher's exact test (2 × 2) or its larger-table version computes the p-value without the χ² curve. Most statistics software offers it.

Never fix the problem by deleting the awkward rows. That changes the question.

Yates' continuity correction (mention only)

For 2 × 2 tables some textbooks and software (R does this by default) subtract 0.5 from each |O − E| before squaring. It makes the test slightly more cautious. For the fragrance example it gives 12.63 instead of 13.66; the decision doesn't change. The class, Excel's CHISQ.TEST and our solutions use the uncorrected formula. If your software output differs slightly from your hand answer on a 2 × 2 table, this is usually why.

Independence vs homogeneity Light coverage

Same arithmetic, different story about the sample. Independence: one sample of 1,440 employees, and each person is classified on two variables (age, AI use). Homogeneity: you deliberately sample, say, 200 customers in each of three cities and ask one question (payment method), then test whether the three cities share the same mix. Anderson's section 12.1 (“equality of population proportions”) is the homogeneity version. Expected counts, χ² and df are computed identically; only the wording of H₀ changes (“the proportions are the same in every population”).

Goodness of fit Light coverage

One categorical variable, compared against a claimed distribution: market-share claims, “demand is uniform across weekdays”, “defects follow last year's pattern”. Expected count = n × claimed proportion; df = k − 1, where k is the number of categories (subtract one more for every parameter you had to estimate from the data). Worked example 3 and the market-share reveal above show both kinds.

The percentages trap

Never run χ² on percentages. Feed the row percentages of the class table (63.67, 36.33, …) into the formula as if they were counts and you get χ² = 7.61, not 37.26, because the test now thinks you surveyed 300 people. χ² depends on the actual sample size. Always use raw counts.

How a consultant would present it

“AI-assistant use differs by age group (χ² = 37.3, df = 2, p < 0.001; Cramér's V = 0.16). Usage falls from 64% among under-30s to 45% among staff aged 50+, and the 50+ group accounts for most of the difference. Recommendation: one company-wide rollout, plus a targeted hands-on track for the 50+ group.” Test result, size of the effect, where it comes from, what to do. In that order.

Interview-style question

“Your χ² test was not significant. So the two variables are independent, right?” Not quite. You failed to find evidence of a link with this sample. A small sample, or small expected counts as in the placement case, can hide a real association. Say “no evidence of association at the 5% level”, never “proved independent”.

Exercise 11 · ticket type × flight type

The big χ² (100.43) comes almost entirely from business class: those two cells contribute 68.2 of it. So the dependence is simple to state. International trips are booked in business class 43.5% of the time, domestic trips only 14.8%.

Common mistakes

WrongRight
Running the test on percentages or averages.Use raw counts. χ² depends on the sample size, so percentages give a meaningless number.
Skipping the expected-count check.Compute E first and check every E ≥ 5 (the class rule). If not, merge categories or say the result is unreliable.
Checking the observed counts against 5.The rule is about expected counts. An observed 2 is fine if its expected count is 6.
df = n − 1, or df = r × c − 1.For independence, df = (r − 1)(c − 1). Don't count the Total row or column. For goodness of fit, df = k − 1.
Halving α or looking in the left tail.The test is always right-tailed. Reject when χ² exceeds the cut-off for the full α.
“Specialisation causes placement” after a significant result.χ² shows association, not cause, and not direction. Look at the residuals or row percentages for direction.
“Not significant, so the variables are independent.”Say “no evidence of association at the 5% level”. Absence of evidence isn't proof.
Counting the same person in two cells (multi-select survey questions).Each observation must fall in exactly one cell. Analyse multi-select items one option at a time (ticked / not ticked).

Quick check

In a contingency table with n = 100, row 1 totals 60 and column 2 totals 30. What is the expected count for the cell in row 1, column 2 under independence?

  1. 30
  2. 18
  3. 42
  4. 12
E = row total × column total ÷ n = 60 × 30 / 100 = 18. Option (c) uses column 1 (70) instead, and (d) uses row 2 (40).

A survey cross-tabulates 4 customer segments against 3 preferred payment methods. How many degrees of freedom does the chi-square test of independence have?

  1. 12
  2. 11
  3. 7
  4. 6
df = (r − 1)(c − 1) = (4 − 1)(3 − 1) = 3 × 2 = 6. Options (a) and (b) count cells; (c) adds instead of multiplying.

A chi-square test gives χ² = 4.2 with df = 3. The 5% critical value is 7.815 (p = 0.24). What should you conclude?

  1. Do not reject H₀: there is no significant evidence that the two variables are associated.
  2. Reject H₀: the variables are associated.
  3. The variables are proven to be independent.
  4. The test is invalid because χ² is bigger than df.
4.2 < 7.815 and p = 0.24 > 0.05, so we don't reject H₀. That's “no evidence of a link”, not proof of independence, which rules out (c). There's no rule comparing χ² with df.

For 1,440 employees, a test of age group × AI use gives p < 0.0001 and Cramér's V = 0.16. What's the best reading?

  1. The association is very strong, because p is tiny.
  2. Age causes employees to avoid AI tools.
  3. The association is real (very unlikely to be chance) but modest in size.
  4. The test is invalid because the sample is too large.
p measures how surprising the data are under H₀; V measures strength. A large sample makes even modest differences highly significant. Chi-square shows association, not cause, and big samples are fine.

In a 2 × 3 table, the expected counts are 12.5, 8.0, 4.5, 15.0, 9.6 and 3.4. Following the class rule, what should you do?

  1. Go ahead: the observed counts are what matter.
  2. Merge sensible categories or collect more data, because two expected counts are below 5.
  3. Delete the two columns with small counts and test the rest.
  4. Convert the counts to percentages so they are all large enough.
The rule is that every expected count should be at least 5; 4.5 and 3.4 fail it. Merging categories (or more data) fixes it honestly. Deleting columns changes the question, and percentages are never valid input.

One-glance summary

Chi-square test of independence in one screen

  • Use it when: two categorical variables, counts in an r × c table. Question: independent or associated?
  • H₀: independent (same column shares in every row). H₁: associated.
  • Expected: E=R×C/nE = R \times C / nE=R×C/n (independence ⇒ probabilities multiply). Check every E ≥ 5.
  • Statistic: χ2=∑(O−E)2/E\chi^2 = \sum (O-E)^2/Eχ2=∑(O−E)2/E over all cells.
  • df: (r − 1)(c − 1). Goodness of fit: k − 1.
  • Decide: reject H₀ if χ² > CHISQ.INV.RT(α,df), or if p = CHISQ.DIST.RT(χ²,df) < α. Shortcut: CHISQ.TEST(observed,expected) → p. Always right-tailed.
  • Then: residuals (O−E)/E(O-E)/\sqrt{E}(O−E)/E​ for direction; Cramér's V =χ2/[n(k−1)]=\sqrt{\chi^2/[n(k-1)]}=χ2/[n(k−1)]​ for strength (light).
  • Write-up template: “Age group and AI use are associated (χ² = 37.26, df = 2, p < 0.001). Use falls from 64% (under 30) to 45% (50+); the 50+ group drives the result.”
