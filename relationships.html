What a “relationship” means for a manager

The manager's question

You run marketing for a regional snack brand. Some months you spent heavily on Instagram ads, some months hardly at all. Did sales actually move with the spend, and by how much per extra lakh? Meanwhile a telecom client wants to know if customers in the South pick postpaid plans more often than customers in the North.

Two questions, two kinds of data. Both are about one thing: does knowing X tell you something about Y?

Two variables are related when knowing one helps you guess the other. This pillar teaches you to measure that link, check it isn’t luck, and use it to predict.

Every tool on this page answers some mix of three questions. Keep them in your head and the whole pillar gets simpler.

1 · Is there a link at all?

Or is the pattern just chance in this sample? A test answers it: if the p-value is below α (usually 0.05), the link is “statistically significant”.

2 · Which way, and how strong?

Up together or one up, one down? Tight or loose? The correlation r (−1 to +1) and r² (share of variation explained) answer it.

3 · Can I use it?

A regression equation turns the link into a number you can act on: “each extra km adds 3.4 minutes” or “this outlet should sell about 462 combos a week”.

Think of it like

Two friends walking side by side. If one speeds up and the other usually does too, their speeds are correlated. If you can say “for every extra step he takes, she takes 0.8”, that’s regression. Neither tells you who is leading whom. That’s causation, and you need an experiment for it.

Pick the tool from the two variable types

Here is the one idea that sorts the whole pillar. Ask what type each variable is: a number (₹, km, marks) or a category (yes/no, region, brand). Then read off the cell.

Two groups only?

If X has just two groups (say, male vs female customers, or old vs new store layout) and Y is a number, that’s the two-sample t-test from the Inferential pillar. ANOVA is the same idea stretched to three or more groups.

Which relationship tool do I need?

Work down the ladder. Each “No” moves you one rung down; each “Yes” sends you to a tool. Five questions at most, and you’ve got your method.

Test yourself: name the tool for these five questions
  1. Does the colour of a pack (red, blue, green) affect whether shoppers pick it up (yes/no)? → Chi-square (category × category).
  2. Do average delivery times differ across four dark stores? → ANOVA (groups × number).
  3. How many extra units does each additional salesperson bring in? → Simple regression (you want the slope).
  4. Is there any link between hours of overtime and error rate on a production line? → Correlation (direction and strength only).
  5. Predict a flat’s price from its area, floor and whether it has parking. → Multiple regression with a dummy for parking.

Significant is not the same as strong

The p-value depends on sample size; r does not. Take r = 0.52. With 18 people, t=2.44t = 2.44t=2.44 beats the critical 2.12, so it’s significant (p = 0.027). With only 10 people the same r gives t=1.72t = 1.72t=1.72, below 2.31: not significant (p = 0.12). And with 400 people even r = 0.15 is significant (t = 3.03, p = 0.003), though it explains barely 2% of the variation. So report both: is it real (p) and does it matter (r, r², the slope).

Correlation is not causation

Ice-cream sales and AC sales rise together in May. Neither causes the other; summer causes both. Before you say “X drives Y”, ask: could a third factor move both? Could Y be driving X? Only a controlled experiment (or very careful design) settles cause.

Consumer Research, Inc.

Which customer traits predict how much a person puts on a credit card in a year? Syllabus case · Unit 4

The situation

An independent market-research agency is hired by a client that wants to understand credit-card users. The client’s question is practical: using things it can easily learn about a customer, can it predict how much that customer will charge on a card over a year?

The agency collects three numbers for each of 50 consumers: annual income, household size, and the total amount charged in the year. Your job is to find out which trait predicts spending better, and whether the two together beat either one alone.

What the manager wants

  1. A descriptive summary of all three variables, with comments.
  2. Two simple regressions (charges on income; charges on household size), and a verdict on which single variable predicts better.
  3. A multiple regression using both, with a discussion of what it shows.
  4. A predicted annual charge for a three-person household earning $40,000.
  5. Ideas for other variables that could improve the model.

Data you need

The file Consumer (Consumer.xlsx on the textbook companion site, or ask your instructor): 50 rows, with Income ($1000s), Household Size (people) and Amount Charged ($). The class handout is below.

How to crack it

  1. Lay out the sheetIncome in column B, Household Size in C, Amount Charged in D, labels in row 1 (so the data run to row 51).
  2. Describe firstData › Data Analysis › Descriptive Statistics, input B1:D51, tick Labels in first row and Summary statistics. Then run the Correlation tool on the same range: you want the correlation of each X with charges and of the two X’s with each other.
  3. Two simple regressionsData › Data Analysis › Regression with Y = D1:D51 and X = B1:B51 (income). Repeat with X = C1:C51 (household size). Compare R Square and Standard Error: higher R² and lower standard error win.
  4. The multiple regressionSame tool, X = B1:C51 (both columns together; they must be adjacent). Read the coefficients, R², adjusted R², Significance F and both p-values.
  5. PredictIncome is in $1000s, so $40,000 is 40: y^=b0+b1(40)+b2(3)\hat{y} = b_0 + b_1(40) + b_2(3)y^​=b0​+b1​(40)+b2​(3). Check that 40 and 3 lie inside the data’s range before you trust it.
  6. Check the residualsTick Residual Plots. A funnel or a curve means the straight-line model needs work.
What a strong managerial report includes

Our own computations on the textbook’s 50 rows. Use them to check your work; the full walk-through is on the Regression page.

  • A short descriptive picture. The typical consumer earns about $43.5k (median $42k, range $21k–$67k), lives in a household of about 3.4 people, and charges about $3,964 a year (SD ≈ $933).
  • The single-variable verdict. Household size predicts better on its own: r² ≈ 0.567 against 0.398 for income. Equations: Charges ≈ 2,582 + 404 × Household size; Charges ≈ 2,204 + 40.5 × Income.
  • The combined model. Charges ≈ 1,305 + 33.13 × Income + 356.3 × Household size, with R² ≈ 0.826 (adjusted ≈ 0.818). Both p-values are far below 0.01 and Significance F is essentially zero.
  • Interpretation, “holding the other constant”. Each extra $1,000 of income adds about $33 of annual charges for the same household size; each extra household member adds about $356 at the same income.
  • Why together beats alone. Income and household size are only weakly correlated (r ≈ 0.17), so each explains a different slice of the variation. Together they explain far more than either one.
  • The prediction. 1,305 + 33.13(40) + 356.3(3) ≈ $3,699 for a three-person household earning $40,000.
  • Other variables worth testing. Age, credit limit, number of cards held, education, employment type, rewards-programme membership, how often the person shops online. Say why each might matter.
  • Honest limits. 50 consumers; predictions only hold inside the observed ranges; correlation is not cause (a bigger household doesn’t make you charge more; it reflects bigger shared expenses).

Measuring Stock Market Risk

Is this share riskier than the market? The answer is the slope of a simple regression, and it has a name: beta.

The situation

The usual way to measure how risky a share is: look at the standard deviation of its returns. The catch is that SD ignores how the share moves with the market. Analysts prefer beta: regress the share’s monthly total return (price change plus dividends) on the market’s return, here the S&P 500, and read off the slope.

Beta = 1 means the share tends to move one-for-one with the market. Above 1, it swings harder (1.4 → about 40% more volatile than the market). Below 1, it’s calmer. The case gives 36 months of returns for eight widely traded shares and the index.

What the manager wants

  1. Descriptive statistics for each share and for the index. Which are the most volatile?
  2. Beta for each share. Which should do best when the market rises? Which should hold up best when it falls?
  3. How much of each share’s return is explained by the market?

Data you need

The file Beta (Beta.xlsx, textbook companion site): 36 monthly total returns (%) in nine columns, one per share plus one for the S&P 500.

How to crack it

  1. Volatility by SDRun Descriptive Statistics on all nine columns. Rank the shares by standard deviation.
  2. Beta for each share=SLOPE(share_col, index_col). Y is the share, X is the index. Copy across for all eight.
  3. Market-explained share=RSQ(share_col, index_col) gives r². The rest, 1 − r², is risk specific to that company.
  4. One full outputRun the Regression tool for one share to see the t test for the slope and the 95% interval for beta. Does the interval include 1?
  5. One summary tableShare · SD · beta · r², sorted by beta. That table is the heart of the report.
What a strong managerial report includes
  • A ranking table (SD, beta, r²) and one sentence per share.
  • Up market: the highest-beta shares should gain most. Down market: the lowest-beta (defensive) shares should hold value best.
  • The key insight: a high SD does not mean a high beta. A share can be jumpy for its own reasons (low r²) while barely tracking the market. Company-specific risk can be diversified away in a portfolio; market risk (beta) cannot.
  • Modest r² values are normal. They say most month-to-month movement is company news, not market mood.
  • Beta is an estimate from 36 months. Quote its 95% interval and say whether it is clearly above or below 1. A strong student tests H₀: β = 1 with t=(b1−1)/sb1t = (b_1 - 1)/s_{b_1}t=(b1​−1)/sb1​​.
Try it on Indian shares

Download 37 month-end closing prices for a few Nifty 50 companies and for the Nifty 50 index from the NSE website, turn them into 36 monthly returns (=B3/B2−1), and run exactly the same steps. You’ll have computed your own betas.

Wentworth Medical Center

Does where older people live relate to how depressed they feel? One-way ANOVA, run on two samples. Light coverage

The situation

A long-running study of people aged 65 and over looks at whether location is linked to depression. Researchers gave a standard depression test (higher score = more depressed) to 20 people in each of three states: Florida, New York and North Carolina.

They did this twice. The first sample was people in reasonably good health. The second was people with a chronic condition such as arthritis, hypertension or a heart ailment.

What the manager wants

  1. Descriptive statistics for both studies, with first impressions.
  2. An analysis of variance on each data set: hypotheses, decision, conclusion.
  3. Where it makes sense, comparisons of individual state means.

Data you need

Files Medical1 (healthy sample) and Medical2 (chronic-condition sample). Each has three columns of 20 depression scores, one column per state.

How to crack it

  1. DescribeDescriptive Statistics on the three columns of each file. Compare the means and SDs.
  2. Run the ANOVAData › Data Analysis › Anova: Single Factor, input all three columns, Grouped by Columns, Labels in first row, α = 0.05.
  3. DecideH₀: the three state means are equal. Reject if P-value < 0.05 (equivalently, F > F crit).
  4. If you reject, find which pairFisher’s LSD: tα/2MSW(1/ni+1/nj)t_{\alpha/2} \sqrt{MSW(1/n_i + 1/n_j)}tα/2​MSW(1/ni​+1/nj​)​, with df = 60 − 3 = 57. Any pair of means further apart than the LSD differs.
  5. Repeat for Medical2and compare the two stories.
What a strong managerial report includes

Our own computations from the data printed in the case.

  • Healthy sample (Medical1): means Florida 5.55, New York 8.00, North Carolina 7.05. F = 5.24, p ≈ 0.008, so reject H₀: mean depression differs by state. LSD ≈ 1.53: New York vs Florida (2.45 apart) clearly differs; Florida vs North Carolina (1.50) falls just short; New York vs North Carolina (0.95) doesn’t differ.
  • Chronic-condition sample (Medical2): means 14.50, 15.25, 13.95. F = 0.71, p ≈ 0.49: no evidence that location matters for this group.
  • The bigger story: scores in the chronic group (around 14–15) are roughly double those of the healthy group (around 5.5–8) in every state. Health status matters far more than geography.
  • Caveats: observational data (people who retire to Florida may differ in wealth or outlook), 20 people per group, a single test score.

A Bipartisan Agenda for Change

Do Democrats, Independents and Republicans answer reform questions differently, or does support cut across party lines?

The situation

A large poll in New York State found wide support for several reforms of how the state government works. To dig deeper, a follow-up survey asked 100 people in western New York for their party (Democrat, Independent or Republican) and for a Yes/No answer to three proposals: cut legislators’ pay for every day the budget is late; put more restrictions on lobbyists; and bring in term limits for legislators.

Answers are coded 1 for Yes and 2 for No. The question behind the case: is support for reform independent of party?

What the manager wants

  1. A descriptive summary, and a first view on whether the answers depend on party for each question.
  2. A test of independence between response and party for question 1, at α = 0.05.
  3. The same test for question 2.
  4. The same test for question 3.
  5. A verdict: is there broad support for change across all political lines?

Data you need

The file NYReform: 100 rows, one per respondent, with party affiliation and three columns of 1/2 codes.

How to crack it

  1. Make it readableRecode 1 → Yes, 2 → No (Find & Replace on a copy of the columns, or =IF(B2=1,"Yes","No")).
  2. One PivotTable per questionInsert › PivotTable: Rows = Party, Columns = the question, Values = Count. You get a 3 × 2 table. Add a copy showing % of Row Total for the description.
  3. Expected countsNext to it, =row total*column total/grand total for every cell. Check that every E is at least 5.
  4. Test=CHISQ.TEST(observed, expected) gives the p-value. For the hand route, χ² = Σ(O − E)²/E with df = (3 − 1)(2 − 1) = 2 and critical value =CHISQ.INV.RT(0.05,2) = 5.99.
  5. Repeatfor questions 2 and 3, then put all three in one summary table: question · % Yes by party · χ² · p · decision.
What a strong managerial report includes
  • The three cross-tabs with row percentages, so a reader sees the pattern before the test.
  • Three properly written tests (H₀, expected counts, χ², df, p, decision).
  • For any question where H₀ is rejected, a sentence on which party differs and how, using the row percentages and the biggest (O − E)²/E cells.
  • The subtle point. “Independent” means the Yes-share is similar across parties. That alone isn’t “broad support”: all three parties could be equally lukewarm. Broad support needs both independence and a high Yes-share in every party.
  • Limits: 100 respondents from one region; some party groups may be small, so check the E ≥ 5 rule every time.

A direct-to-consumer snack brand in Pune tracked its monthly digital ad spend and online orders for 12 months.

  1. Describe the relationship: direction, form and strength.
  2. Which is closest to the correlation coefficient for all 12 months: −0.76, 0.10, 0.76 or 0.99?
  3. Point P is a month when the website was down for four days. What happens to r if P is left out? Should you leave it out?
  4. Can the marketing head conclude that ads cause orders?
Worked solution

The data behind the chart: spend 4, 5, 6, …, 15 (₹ lakh, one value per month); orders 19, 23, 20, 26, 27, 31, 28, 34, 33, 38, 23 (P, at ₹14 lakh), 41 (’000).

(a) Positive (more spend, more orders), roughly a straight line, fairly strong. P sits well below the pattern.

(b) r ≈ 0.76. It can’t be negative (the cloud rises). It isn’t 0.10 (there’s a clear trend). 0.99 would need the points almost on a line, and P alone rules that out. The computed value is r = 0.761.

(c) Without P, r jumps to 0.967. One odd month dragged r from 0.97 down to 0.76: r is very sensitive to outliers. Don’t delete it quietly. Here you know the cause (an outage, which isn’t part of the normal ad-to-orders process), so it’s fair to analyse without P and say so, or report both figures.

(d) No. This is correlation in observational data. Festive months or price offers could raise both spend and orders. To claim cause you’d need something like switching ads off in a few cities and comparing.

Below are residual plots from two different simple regressions. Plot A: monthly grocery spend on household income, 24 households. Plot B: weekly units sold on discount %, 24 weeks.

Worked solution

(a) Plot A fans out: residuals get bigger as fitted values grow. The spread isn’t constant (heteroscedasticity): richer households vary much more in what they spend. Examiner’s notes: the five numbers that earn the marks

When a question hands you a regression output, almost every mark sits on five numbers. Find them, write one sentence about each, and you’ve answered most of the question.

Write-up template: a regression output

  1. Equationŷ = b₀ + b₁x₁ + b₂x₂, with real variable names and units.
  2. Slopes“Holding the other X’s constant, each extra unit of x₁ changes ŷ by b₁ (units).”
  3. FitR² as a percentage in a sentence; adjusted R² if comparing models.
  4. Overall F testH₀: all β = 0. Significance F vs α → decision.
  5. t testsH₀: βⱼ = 0 for each X. P-value vs α → keep or drop.
  6. PredictSubstitute values inside the data range; give units; quote the standard error as the typical miss.

Write-up template: a chi-square test

  1. HypothesesH₀: the two variables are independent. H₁: they are associated.
  2. Expected countsE = row total × column total / n; check every E ≥ 5.
  3. Statisticχ² = Σ(O − E)²/E over every cell.
  4. df and critical valuedf = (r − 1)(c − 1); read χ² from the table at α.
  5. DecisionReject H₀ if χ² > critical (or p < α).
  6. Conclusion in contextWhich way the dependence runs, using row percentages and the biggest cells.
