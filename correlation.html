Correlation

Do two numbers move together, how tightly, and what does that prove? Scatter diagrams, covariance, Pearson r and Spearman rank correlation.

The manager's question

The manager's question

Your sales head walks in with a chart. “In the six months we spent more on ads, sales were higher. The correlation is 0.97. Let's double the ad budget for Diwali.”

Is 0.97 as strong as it sounds? What exactly does it measure? And does it prove that the ads caused the sales? By the end of this page you'll be able to answer all three, and compute the number yourself with a pen.

Correlation measures how tightly two numeric variables move together in a straight line, on a scale from −1 to +1. It describes a pattern. It does not explain it.

Level 1 · Basics

The idea in plain words

Two variables are correlated when knowing one helps you guess the other. Higher ad spend, higher sales: positive. Higher price, fewer units sold: negative. Shoe size and monthly salary: nothing.

Think of it like

Two cricket fans watching the same India match on TV. When one cheers, the other usually cheers too: that's a positive correlation. Now seat an India fan next to an Australia fan. When one cheers, the other groans: negative. Put two people in different cities watching different shows, and their cheering has nothing to do with each other: zero. The correlation coefficient is just a number for how reliably the two react together.

Before you compute anything, you draw. A scatter diagram puts one variable on the horizontal axis (x) and the other on the vertical axis (y), with one dot per observation: one dot per month, per store, per customer. Then you read four things off it.

Direction
Do the dots rise (positive) or fall (negative) from left to right?
Form
Is the pattern roughly a straight line, a curve, or clusters?
Strength
Are the dots packed tightly around a line, or a loose cloud?
Outliers
Is there a dot far from the rest that could be steering everything?
Scatter diagram
A chart of paired data: one dot per observation at the point (x, y).
Covariance, sxys_{xy}sxy​
A raw measure of whether x and y move together. Its sign gives the direction, but its size depends on the units, so it's hard to read.
Correlation coefficient, r
Covariance rescaled to run from −1 to +1. Unit-free, so you can compare it across any two datasets. Also called Pearson's r.
Spearman's rank correlation, rsr_srs​
The same idea computed on the ranks (1st, 2nd, 3rd…) instead of the raw values. Used for ratings and rankings, or when outliers or curves upset Pearson.
Linear
Following a straight line. Pearson's r only measures straight-line patterns.
Outlier
A point far away from the general pattern. One outlier can make r look strong or weak on its own.

So how do you read a value of r? The class deck uses a simple scale. We'll add the rule-of-thumb words most business reports use.

One warning before we start

A strong correlation never proves that one thing causes the other. Ice-cream sales and drowning deaths rise together because summer drives both. We come back to this in Pro, because it's the most common mistake managers make with this number.

Level 2 · Theory

Formulas and how they work

Step 1 · Covariance: do the dots lean up or down?

Here's the trick behind every correlation formula. Draw a vertical line at the mean of x and a horizontal line at the mean of y. That cuts the scatter into four boxes. For each point, measure how far it sits from the means: xi−xˉx_i-\bar{x}xi​−xˉ across and yi−yˉy_i-\bar{y}yi​−yˉ​ up. Multiply the two.

  • Points up-right of the centre (above average on both) give (+)(+) = positive.
  • Points down-left (below average on both) give (−)(−) = positive too.
  • Points in the other two boxes give a negative product.

Add up all the products. A big positive total means most points lie in the “agree” boxes, so x and y rise together. A big negative total means they move in opposite directions. Near zero means no lean either way. Each product is literally the area of a rectangle from the centre to the point, as Figure 3 shows.

Why covariance is hard to read. 18.8 what? The unit is “₹ lakh of ad spend × ₹ lakh of sales”, which means nothing to anyone. Worse, change the units and the number jumps. Record sales in ₹ thousand instead of ₹ lakh and the covariance becomes 1,880. Record both in ₹ thousand and it's 1,88,000. Same months, same relationship, three wildly different numbers. You can't say whether 18.8 is “big”. So we standardise.

Properties you should be able to state in the exam

  1. Range: −1≤r≤+1-1 \le r \le +1−1≤r≤+1. r=+1r = +1r=+1 or −1-1−1 only if every point lies exactly on a straight line.
  2. Unit-free: rupees, lakh, dollars, kilos: r doesn't care. Adding a constant to x or multiplying it by a positive number leaves r unchanged.
  3. Symmetric: the correlation of x with y equals the correlation of y with x. It has no “cause” side and “effect” side.
  4. Linear only: r measures straight-line patterns. A perfect U-shape can give r ≈ 0 (Figure 1, panel 7).
  5. Sensitive to outliers: because it's built on means and squared deviations, one extreme point can swing it (Figure 1, panel 8).
  6. Needs numeric data: interval or ratio scale for both variables. For ranks or ratings, use Spearman.

A preview of r² (the coefficient of determination)

Square r and you get the share of the variation in y that a straight line in x can account for. With r=0.969r = 0.969r=0.969, r2=0.939r^2 = 0.939r2=0.939: about 94% of the month-to-month variation in sales lines up with ad spend. With the class housing data, r=0.44r = 0.44r=0.44 gives r2=0.19r^2 = 0.19r2=0.19, so 81% of the variation in delinquent loans has nothing to do with the jobless rate. That's a sobering way to read a “moderate” correlation. You'll meet r² properly as R-squared on the Regression page.

Quick sense check

r = 0.5 sounds like “halfway”. It isn't: r² = 0.25, a quarter. Correlations need to be large before they explain much.

Spearman's rank correlation Light coverage

Sometimes the data aren't proper numbers. Customer-satisfaction ratings (1 to 5 stars), class ranks, a judge's ordering of business-plan pitches: these are ordinal. The gaps between them aren't equal, so means and standard deviations are shaky. Spearman's fix is simple: replace each value by its rank within its own column, then measure how well the two rankings agree.

Number check: 8 café outlets, ranks disagree a little, ∑d2=14\sum d^2 = 14∑d2=14. Then rs=1−6(14)/(8×63)=1−84/504=0.833r_s = 1 - 6(14)/(8 \times 63) = 1 - 84/504 = 0.833rs​=1−6(14)/(8×63)=1−84/504=0.833. Full working is in Worked example 2.

Ties. If two values are equal, give each the average of the ranks they would share. Two values tied for 3rd and 4th both get 3.5. With ties the shortcut formula is only approximate; the exact Spearman value is Pearson's r applied to the ranks (Excel: RANK.AVG then CORREL). With only a few ties they usually agree to two or three decimals (in Worked example 3: 0.9714 vs 0.9710).

When to prefer Spearman: (1) ordinal data such as ratings or rankings; (2) outliers you can't remove, because a rank can only be 1 place more extreme, never 10 times; (3) a curve that keeps going one way (always rising, but bending), called monotonic. Panel 8 of Figure 1 makes the point: Pearson says +0.73, Spearman says −0.01. The ranks ignore the single extreme dot.

Why does the formula have a 6 and n(n2−1)n(n^2-1)n(n2−1) in it?

If you feed ranks 1 to n into Pearson's formula, the means and standard deviations of the ranks are fixed numbers that depend only on n. After the algebra, everything collapses to 1−6∑d2/[n(n2−1)]1 - 6\sum d^2/[n(n^2-1)]1−6∑d2/[n(n2−1)]. So Spearman isn't a new idea; it's Pearson on ranks, written in a shortcut that only needs the differences. Ties break the “fixed numbers” part, which is why the shortcut becomes approximate.

Level 3 · Application

Where managers use it

Marketing

Spend vs response

Ad spend vs sales by month, discount depth vs units sold, brand awareness vs market share across regions. A first screen before anyone builds a marketing-mix model.

Finance

Diversification

Portfolio risk depends on how asset returns correlate. Two stocks with r = 0.2 cut risk far more than two with r = 0.9, even if each is equally risky alone.

HR

Engagement and attrition

Team engagement score vs resignations per quarter; training hours vs productivity. Survey scores are ordinal, so Spearman is often the honest choice.

Operations

Wear and downtime

Machine age vs breakdown hours, delivery distance vs delivery time. A strong r flags where a maintenance or routing rule could pay off.

Banking & credit

Macro risk signals

The class housing data: jobless rate vs delinquent loans across 27 markets (r = 0.44). Risk teams watch such links to set regional lending caps.

E-commerce

Engagement vs basket size

Time on site and pages viewed vs order value. This is exactly tasks 4–6 of the Heavenly Chocolates case.

Indian context

Suppose a tractor-financing NBFC finds r = 0.7 between district monsoon rainfall and loan disbursals over ten years. Useful for planning branch staffing before the kharif season. But rainfall drives crop income, which drives tractor demand, which drives loans. The correlation is real; the lever isn't rainfall. A manager who reads it as “rain causes loans” learns nothing they can act on, while one who asks “through which channel?” finds the real driver: farm income.

Level 4 · Pro

Nuance, pitfalls and depth

Correlation is not causation Examined

Every strong r has four possible explanations. The class deck lists them on one slide (slide 19). Here they are with business examples.

So how do you get closer to cause? Run an experiment if you can (an A/B test: raise ad spend in randomly chosen cities only). If you can't, at least check time order (did x change first?), look for a plausible mechanism, and control for the obvious confounders with multiple regression. The deck's closing line says it well: turning “r is high” into “if we raise spend 10%, sales rise by X” needs regression and a proper research design.

Is the correlation real or chance? Testing H₀: ρ = 0 Light coverage

Your sample r is an estimate of the population correlation ρ. With small samples, a respectable-looking r can appear from pure noise. The t-test checks that.

Anscombe's quartet: why you always plot first

In 1973 the statistician Frank Anscombe built four tiny datasets, 11 points each, to make one point. All four have the same mean of x (9), variance of x (11), mean of y (7.50), variance of y (4.12 to 4.13), the same r (about 0.82) and the same best-fit line, ŷ = 3.00 + 0.50x. Here they are.

The same thing happens in the class problem. In the bowl-games data, one game (the Sugar Bowl, predicted +14, actual +27) supplies 176 of the 201 total in ∑(x−xˉ)(y−yˉ)\sum (x-\bar{x})(y-\bar{y})∑(x−xˉ)(y−yˉ​). Drop it and r falls from 0.57 to 0.03. A report that said “Las Vegas odds predict margins well (r = 0.57)” would really be reporting one game.

Restricted range: when selection hides a real link

This is why admissions teams sometimes conclude “test scores don't predict performance”. They're only looking at the top slice. The same trap shows up in HR (“interview scores don't predict appraisal ratings”, measured only on people you hired) and in credit (“income doesn't predict default”, measured only on approved loans). Correlation computed on a narrow range understates the real relationship.

Spurious correlations in business dashboards

Anything that grows over time correlates with anything else that grows over time. Revenue, headcount, app downloads, the price of coffee: all trend up, so a dashboard that correlates monthly levels will light up green everywhere. Three habits protect you:

  1. Correlate the changes, not the levels. If this month's jump in x doesn't line up with this month's jump in y, the “link” was just the shared trend.
  2. Count your comparisons. A dashboard with 20 KPIs has 190 pairs. At α = 0.05 you expect about 9 or 10 “significant” pairs from pure noise (Figure 4, panel 4).
  3. Ask for the mechanism. If nobody can explain how x would move y, treat the correlation as a lead to investigate, not a finding.

Pearson r

Uses actual values. Best for numeric data with a straight-line pattern. Pulled around by outliers. Feeds straight into regression.

vs

Spearman rsr_srs​

Uses ranks. Works for ratings and rankings, any always-rising or always-falling curve, and data with outliers. Says nothing about the slope.

How a consultant would present it

One chart, one sentence, one caveat. “Across 27 metro markets, delinquent-loan rates were higher where unemployment was higher (r = 0.44, p = 0.02; scatter attached). The link is moderate: unemployment lines up with about a fifth of the variation. Three Sun Belt markets with housing bubbles sit well above the trend, so a regional factor matters as much as joblessness.” Never present r without the scatter and the n.

Interview-style question

“Our data show r = 0 between price and sales. So price doesn't matter, right?” A strong answer: r = 0 rules out a straight-line link only. Sales might rise with price up to a point (a premium signal) and then fall, giving an inverted U and r near 0. Or price changes might always come with promotions that hide the effect. Plot it, then think about confounders.

Common mistakes

WrongRight
“r = 0.8, so 80% of sales is explained by ad spend.”The share is r² = 0.64. And “explained” only means “lines up with”, not “caused by”.
“r = 0, so there's no relationship.”There's no straight-line relationship. A U-shape or a curve can hide behind r ≈ 0. Plot it.
Computing r straight from the spreadsheet without a chart.Scatter first, always. Anscombe's four datasets share r = 0.816 and look nothing alike.
“High correlation proves the ads work.”Consider reverse causality, a confounder (season, store size) and coincidence. Only an experiment proves cause.
Comparing covariances across datasets (“18.8 here vs 1,880 there, so the second is stronger”).Covariance depends on units. Compare r, which is unit-free.
Dividing by n for a sample covariance (or trusting the ToolPak Covariance tool).Sample covariance divides by n − 1: use COVARIANCE.S.
Ranking one variable high-to-low and the other low-to-high in Spearman.Rank both in the same direction, or the sign of rsr_srs​ flips.
Using the no-ties Spearman formula and giving tied values different ranks.Tied values share the average rank (two values tied for 3rd and 4th get 3.5 each).

Quick check

A retail chain finds r = −0.85 between a store's distance from the nearest metro station and its monthly sales. Which statement is correct?

  1. Moving a store 1 km closer to the metro will raise its sales by 85%.
  2. 85% of the variation in sales is explained by distance.
  3. Stores further from the metro tend to have lower sales, and the straight-line pattern is strong.
  4. The relationship is weak because r is negative.
r gives direction (negative: further away, lower sales) and strength (|r| = 0.85 is strong). The share of variation is r² = 0.72, not 0.85, so (b) is wrong. (a) claims cause and a size of effect that r can't give. A negative sign says nothing about weakness.

A brand finds r = 0.02 between the number of times a customer saw an ad and their recall score. The scatter shows recall rising up to about 6 exposures and then falling. What should the analyst conclude?

  1. Ad exposure has no effect on recall.
  2. The data must contain an error, because r should be positive.
  3. Use Spearman instead, which will show a strong correlation.
  4. There is a clear curved relationship that a linear r cannot capture.
r only measures straight-line patterns. An inverted U gives r near 0 even when the relationship is strong. Spearman wouldn't rescue it either, because the curve isn't monotonic (it goes up, then down). Describe the curve; ad fatigue after about 6 exposures is the real finding.

With sales in ₹ lakh, the covariance between ad spend and sales is 18.8 and r = 0.97. You re-record sales in ₹ thousand (every value × 100). What happens?

  1. Both the covariance and r are multiplied by 100.
  2. The covariance becomes 1,880 but r stays 0.97.
  3. The covariance stays 18.8 but r becomes 97.
  4. Neither changes.
Covariance carries the units, so it scales by 100. r divides by both standard deviations, the units cancel, and it can never leave the −1 to +1 range.
