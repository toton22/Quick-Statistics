Errors & p-value

Every test can be wrong in two ways. Learn what α, β, power and the p-value really mean, and how a manager decides which mistake to risk.

The manager's question

The manager's question

Your marketing team ran a one-month pilot of a new loyalty programme with 36 customers at the Dehradun store. Average monthly spend came in at ₹2,180, against the usual ₹2,000. The analyst says “p = 0.036, significant at 5%.”

The CFO is not impressed by jargon. She asks two blunt questions. One: if this programme actually does nothing, how often would a pilot like ours still fool us into rolling it out? Two: if it really works, how often would a pilot like ours have missed it?

Those two questions have names. The first is α, the risk of a Type I error. The second is β, the risk of a Type II error. The p-value is the analyst's way of saying how surprising ₹2,180 would be if nothing had changed. By the end of this page you can answer the CFO in plain words, with numbers.

A hypothesis test never removes risk. It lets you choose which mistake you are more willing to make, and how often.

Level 1 · Basics

Two ways to be wrong

A test ends in one of two decisions: reject H₀ or do not reject H₀. Reality is also one of two things: H₀ is true, or it is false. Two decisions times two realities gives four boxes. Two are right. Two are mistakes.

Quick reminder of the cast (the Hypothesis tests page sets them up properly). The null hypothesis H₀ is the boring, status-quo story: “the programme changes nothing”, “the batch is fine”, “this transaction is genuine”. The alternative H₁ (some books write Hₐ) is what you would need evidence to believe.

Think of it like

A courtroom. H₀ is “the accused is innocent”. The prosecution has to prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt, which is a deliberately small α. A Type I error is jailing an innocent person. A Type II error is letting a guilty person walk free. And look at the verdict the court gives: “not guilty”, never “innocent”. A test does the same thing when it says “do not reject H₀”.

Type I error = false alarm

Rejecting H₀ when it is actually true. The smoke alarm goes off and there's no fire. Also called a false positive. Its probability is α.

vs

Type II error = a miss

Not rejecting H₀ when it is actually false. There's a fire and the alarm stays quiet. Also called a false negative. Its probability is β.

Three business stories, same table

Product launch

Does the new feature lift sales?

H₀: no lift.
Type I: launch a product that doesn't work. You pay for marketing, stock and training, and get nothing back.
Type II: kill a product that would have worked. A competitor launches it next year.

Quality control

Is this batch within spec?

H₀: the batch is fine.
Type I: recall or scrap a good batch. Lost stock, a stopped line, an angry plant head.
Type II: ship a bad batch. Complaints, returns, and in food or pharma, real harm.

Fraud detection

Is this payment genuine?

H₀: the transaction is genuine.
Type I: block an honest customer's payment. Annoyance, a support call, maybe a lost customer.
Type II: let a fraud through. A refund, an investigation, a hit to trust.

Type I error
Reject H₀ when H₀ is true. A false alarm.
α (level of significance)
The probability of a Type I error that you agree to accept, chosen before you see the data. Usually 0.05.
Type II error
Fail to reject H₀ when H₀ is false. A miss.
β
The probability of a Type II error. It depends on how far the truth is from H₀.
Power = 1 − β
The probability the test catches a real effect of a given size.
p-value
If H₀ were true, the chance of a sample result at least as extreme as yours.
“Significant at 5%”
p ≤ 0.05, so H₀ is rejected when α = 0.05.

Notice the “given that” parts. α is worked out assuming H₀ is true. β is worked out assuming H₀ is false. They live in different columns of Figure 1, so they do not add up to 1. That's a very common exam slip.

Why we control α, and say “do not reject”

α is easy to control. H₀ gives one exact value (μ = ₹2,000), so you can draw the sampling distribution under H₀ and cut off a 5% tail. Done.

β is harder. “H₀ is false” covers a whole range of possibilities: the true mean could be ₹2,050, ₹2,250 or ₹3,000. Each gives a different β. Unless you pick one specific alternative and compute, β is unknown. So most tests are significance tests: they control α and leave β uncontrolled.

That's why statisticians say “do not reject H₀” rather than “accept H₀”. Saying “accept” would claim you are confident H₀ is true. But with β unknown you have no idea how often a test like yours misses real effects. “Do not reject” means: the evidence wasn't strong enough, so hold your judgement. (Anderson et al. make exactly this point in §9.2.)

Seeing α and β: two overlapping curves

Back to the loyalty pilot. Spend per customer has σ = ₹600, the pilot has n = 36, so the standard error is ₹600/√36 = ₹100. The test is right-tailed at α = 0.05: H₀: μ ≤ ₹2,000 vs H₁: μ > ₹2,000. Reject H₀ if the sample mean is above ₹2,000 + 1.645 × ₹100 = ₹2,164.5.

The one sentence to remember

Cutting α raises β, unless you also increase n. The only way to cut both risks at once is more (or cleaner) data.

The p-value in plain English

The p-value answers one question: if H₀ were true, how surprising is our sample? More exactly, it's the probability, computed assuming H₀ is true, of getting a test statistic at least as extreme as the one we got.

For the pilot: z = (2,180 − 2,000)/100 = 1.80. The p-value is the area beyond 1.80 under the H₀ curve.

The decision rule: compare p with α

Fix α before collecting data (say 0.05)
Compute the test statistic and its p-value
Is p ≤ α?
Yes
Reject H₀. “Significant at α.” Evidence supports H₁.
No
Do not reject H₀. “Not significant at α.” Not enough evidence.

The p-value rule and the critical-value rule always agree: p ≤ α exactly when the test statistic is beyond the critical value. Use whichever the question asks for.

What a p-value is NOT

✗ Not the chance H₀ is true

p = 0.036 does not mean “3.6% chance the programme is useless”. The p-value is calculated assuming H₀ is true, so it can't also be the probability that H₀ is true.

✗ Not the size of the effect

A tiny, worthless effect can have a tiny p-value if the sample is huge. A big, valuable effect can have a large p-value if the sample is small.

✗ Not “1 − p = chance H₁ is true”

0.964 is not the probability the programme works. Nothing in the calculation gives you that.

✗ Not the chance of a fluke

“p = 0.036 means a 3.6% chance the result was luck” sounds right but isn't. It's the chance of data this extreme if luck (H₀) were the only thing going on.

Statistical vs practical significance

Statistically significant means “probably not zero”. Practically significant means “big enough to matter for the business”. They are different questions. A sample of 40,000 food-delivery orders found average delivery time of 30.1 minutes against a target of 30, with s = 6. That gives z = 0.1/(6/√40,000) = 3.33 and a two-tailed p of 0.0009. Highly significant. And the difference is six seconds. Nobody should reorganise a fleet over six seconds.

Explore: feel the trade-off

H₀: mean monthly spend ≤ ₹2,000. The programme may truly lift it to ₹2,250. Move α, n and the true mean.
  • Set α to 0.01 and watch β jump to 0.43. Now raise n until β is back below 0.20. How many customers did you need? (About 58.)
  • Drag the true mean down towards ₹2,000. What happens to power? Why can no test reliably catch a tiny effect with a small sample?
  • Keep α = 0.05 and find the n that also makes β about 0.05 (it's about 63). That's the kind of pilot you'd want before a ₹1-crore roll-out.
Start at the pilot's z = 1.80. Drag it and read the verdict at 10%, 5% and 1%.
  • Find the z where the verdict flips at 5%. It should be 1.645 for a right-tailed test. Where does it flip at 1%? (2.326.)
  • Drag z to 1.28, then to 1.30. The p-value barely moves, yet one is “significant at 10%” and one isn't. Is the evidence really that different?
  • Switch H₁ to two-tailed. The same z = 1.80 now gives p = 2 × 0.036 = 0.072, no longer significant at 5%. That's why the tail must be fixed before you look at the data.
  • Switch the distribution to t (df = 24) and set the statistic to 2.00, two-tailed. You should get p = 0.057, as in Worked example 3(b).
Level 3 · Application

Choosing α in business: price the mistakes

There's nothing magic about 0.05. It's a convention. A good manager asks: which mistake costs more here, and by how much? If a false alarm is expensive, use a small α. If a miss is expensive, use a larger α (or a bigger sample).

Marketing

A/B testing a campaign

Type I: scale up a creative that doesn't beat the old one. Type II: kill a winner. For cheap, reversible changes (a subject line), α = 0.10 is fine. For a national TV spend, go stricter.

Operations

Batch acceptance

H₀: the batch meets spec. Type I: reject a good batch from a supplier. Type II: accept a bad one. If the part goes into car brakes, β matters far more than α. Increase sampling.

Banking

Fraud and credit screening

H₀: the customer is genuine (or creditworthy). Type I annoys good customers. Type II lets losses through. The threshold is a business choice, priced in rupees (see the mini-case below).

HR

Screening tests in hiring

H₀: the candidate is suitable. A high cut-off keeps β (hiring someone unsuitable) small, but raises α: some good people get rejected. Campus drives with hundreds of applicants often accept that trade-off.

Finance & audit

Flagging a misstatement

H₀: the accounts are fine. Type I: an unnecessary deep audit (cost, delay). Type II: a missed fraud (a scandal). Auditors deliberately keep β small for high-risk accounts.

Food & pharma

Safety and label claims

H₀ is often “the product is safe / meets its label”. Shipping something unsafe (Type II) is the error regulators fear most, so they demand large samples and strict checks.

Indian context

Suppose a UPI payments app scores every transaction for fraud risk. With lakhs of payments a day, even a 2% false-alarm rate means tens of thousands of honest people seeing “payment declined”. That's why the cut-off is tuned with rupee costs, not picked from a textbook.

Mini-case: setting a fraud-alert threshold

Situation (hypothetical). A payments app handles 10 lakh transactions a day; about 0.1% (1,000) are fraudulent. Its model gives each transaction a risk score. Genuine transactions score roughly Normal(mean 30, SD 10). Frauds score roughly Normal(mean 60, SD 10). Any transaction scoring above a threshold is blocked.

Here H₀ is “this transaction is genuine”. So α = P(genuine one scores above the threshold) and β = P(fraud scores below it). Suppose a false alarm costs about ₹50 (a support call and an irritated customer) and a missed fraud costs about ₹20,000.

The lesson. Pushing α very low (threshold 55) looks “strict” but costs the most, because misses are 400 times dearer than false alarms. The cheapest threshold is around 48. Your α should come from the cost of each mistake, not from habit.

Multiple testing and p-hacking

Run one test at α = 0.05 and a false alarm has a 5% chance. Run twenty tests on pure noise and the chance that at least one comes up “significant” is 64%.

p-hacking is any habit that quietly inflates false alarms: trying several tests and reporting the one that “worked”, stopping data collection the moment p dips below 0.05, dropping “outliers” only when it helps, or switching from two tails to one tail after seeing the data (which halves the p-value). It isn't always deliberate. It's still wrong.

Good practice

Write down H₀, H₁, α, the tail and the sample size before collecting data. If you must run k tests, the simple Bonferroni fix is to use α/k for each one (0.05/10 = 0.005), which keeps the overall false-alarm risk under 5%. Report every test you ran, not just the winners.

Report a confidence interval, not just p

A p-value tells you whether the effect is probably not zero. A confidence interval tells you how big it plausibly is. Managers need the second. For the loyalty pilot, a one-tailed test at 5% matches a two-sided 90% interval: ₹2,180 ± 1.645 × 100 = ₹2,015.5 to ₹2,344.5. So the lift is somewhere between about ₹16 and ₹345 per customer per month. That range is the real decision input. (Same logic links tests and intervals on the Estimation page.)

For the call centre (two-tailed, 5%): 6.4 ± 2.064 × 0.2 = 5.99 to 6.81 minutes. The interval contains 6, which matches “not significant at 5%”. But it also contains 6.8. So “no significant difference” doesn't mean “it's 6 minutes”.

Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence

Suppose the loyalty pilot had used only 9 customers and found ₹2,150. Then SE = 600/3 = 200, z = 0.75 and p = 0.227. Not significant. Is the programme useless? We can't say. The power of a 9-customer test to detect a real ₹250 lift is only 0.35. It would miss a genuine winner almost two times in three. That's result C in Figure 6: a wide interval stretching from “nothing” to “very good”. Compare result D, whose interval is narrow and sits near zero. Only D is evidence that the effect is small.

The classic boardroom error

“The test was not significant, so the new process makes no difference.” Always ask: what was the power, and what does the interval say?

The ASA statement on p-values

In 2016 the American Statistical Association published its Statement on Statistical Significance and P-Values, because misuse had become so common. Its six principles, in our words:

  1. A p-value measures how incompatible the data are with a stated model (such as H₀).
  2. It is not the probability that the hypothesis is true, and not the probability that the data came from chance alone.
  3. Business, policy and scientific decisions should not rest only on whether p crosses a line like 0.05.
  4. Honest inference needs full reporting: every test, every choice made along the way.
  5. A p-value does not measure the size or importance of an effect.
  6. On its own, a p-value is not a good measure of evidence. Pair it with estimates, intervals and context.

How a consultant would present it

Slide headline: “The loyalty pilot lifted monthly spend by ₹180 per customer.”

Evidence line: 36 customers; 90% CI for the lift ₹16 to ₹345; p = 0.036 (one-tailed), significant at 5%. The test had about 80% power to catch a ₹250 lift.

So what: The lift is real, but its size is uncertain (₹16 to ₹345). If the programme costs, say, ₹40 per customer per month (hypothetical), the central estimate clears that easily and the low end does not. Recommend extending to 3 stores with 50 tracked customers each, to narrow the interval before a chain-wide launch.

Interview question

“Which is worse, a Type I or a Type II error?” The strong answer: it depends on the cost of each in this decision. Then give one example where Type I is worse (launching an expensive dud) and one where Type II is worse (missing a safety defect).

How to think about all eight

One rule cracks every set-up: what you need evidence for goes in Hₐ; the claim, the status quo or the label goes in H₀; and the “=” sign always sits in H₀. Then the errors write themselves: Type I = acting on Hₐ when H₀ was true; Type II = sticking with H₀ when Hₐ was true. The solutions say “accepting H₀” for a Type II error. That's the same error; on this page we say “not rejecting H₀” for the reason explained under Theory.

Common mistakes

WrongRight
“p = 0.03, so there's a 3% chance H₀ is true.”If H₀ were true, data this extreme would turn up 3% of the time.
“We accept H₀.”“We do not reject H₀”: the evidence isn't strong enough. β is usually unknown.
“Not significant, so it has no effect.”Maybe the sample was too small. Check the power and the confidence interval.
“p is tiny, so the effect is big.”p depends on sample size. Judge size from the estimate and its interval.
“α + β = 1.”They are probabilities under different realities. No fixed sum. For a fixed n, lowering α raises β.
“Power = 1 − α.”Power = 1 − β. (1 − α is the chance of correctly not rejecting a true H₀.)
Choosing one tail after seeing which way the data went.Fix the tail and α before collecting data. Switching halves p and is p-hacking.
Testing 20 segments and reporting the one with p < 0.05.Report all tests, or use α/k (Bonferroni) for each one.

Quick check

A bank's fraud system treats H₀ as “this transaction is genuine”. The system blocks a payment from an honest customer. This is:

  1. A Type II error
  2. A correct decision
  3. A Type I error
  4. The power of the test
Blocking = rejecting H₀. H₀ (genuine) was actually true. Rejecting a true H₀ is a Type I error, a false alarm.

H₀: μ ≤ 2,000 vs H₁: μ > 2,000 with σ = 600, n = 36, α = 0.05. If the true mean is 2,250, the power of the test is closest to:

  1. 0.80
  2. 0.20
  3. 0.95
  4. 0.05
SE = 100; reject if x̄ > 2,000 + 1.645(100) = 2,164.5. β = P(Z < (2,164.5 − 2,250)/100) = P(Z < −0.855) ≈ 0.196, so power ≈ 0.804. (0.20 is β, and 0.05 is α.)

A test of a new checkout design gives p = 0.036. Which statement is correct?

  1. There is a 3.6% chance the new design makes no difference.
  2. There is a 96.4% chance the new design works.
  3. The improvement must be small, because p is small.
  4. If the design made no difference, a result at least this extreme would occur about 3.6% of the time.
The p-value is computed assuming H₀ is true. It is not the probability of H₀ or H₁, and it says nothing about the size of the effect.

Without changing α, which of these increases the power of a test?

  1. Using a smaller sample
  2. Using a larger sample
  3. Measuring the outcome less precisely (bigger σ)
  4. Looking for a smaller true effect
A larger n shrinks the standard error σ/√n, so the H₀ and H₁ curves overlap less and β falls. The other three all raise β.

A two-tailed t-test gives p = 0.057. How should you report it?

  1. Significant at 10% but not at 5%
  2. Significant at 5% but not at 1%
  3. Significant at 1%
  4. It proves H₀ is true
0.057 ≤ 0.10, so reject at 10%; 0.057 > 0.05, so do not reject at 5% (or 1%). Not rejecting never proves H₀.

One-glance summary

Errors & p-value in 60 seconds

  • Type I = reject a true H₀ (false alarm), probability α, chosen by you. Type II = fail to reject a false H₀ (a miss), probability β. Power = 1 − β.
  • For a fixed n, lowering α raises β. Bigger n, bigger effects and less noise all lower β.
  • p-value = if H₀ were true, the chance of a result at least as extreme as yours. Right tail: P(Z≥z)P(Z \ge z)P(Z≥z); two tails: 2P(Z≥∣z∣)2P(Z \ge |z|)2P(Z≥∣z∣).
  • Rule: p ≤ α → reject H₀, “significant at α”. p > α → do not reject H₀ (never “accept”).
  • p is not P(H₀ true) and not the size of the effect. Report the estimate and a confidence interval too.
  • Choose α from the cost of each error. Plan n for the power you need: n=(zα+zβ)2σ2/(μ1−μ0)2n = (z_\alpha + z_\beta)^2\sigma^2/(\mu_1-\mu_0)^2n=(zα​+zβ​)2σ2/(μ1​−μ0​)2.
  • Excel: =1-NORM.S.DIST(z,TRUE) · =T.DIST.2T(t,df) · =T.DIST.RT(t,df) · =NORM.S.INV(1-α) · β: =NORM.DIST(crit,μ1,SE,TRUE)
  • Write it like this: “The p-value is 0.036. At the 5% level we reject H₀ and conclude that mean monthly spend has risen above ₹2,000; the estimated lift is ₹180 (90% CI ₹16 to ₹345).”
