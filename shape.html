Shape

Which way does the tail run, how heavy is it, and who is sitting far out on it? Shape decides which average, which spread and which test you can trust.

The manager's question

The manager's question

You are pricing a new enterprise software plan for America's 50 largest corporations (the class dataset LargeCorp). Your analyst says: “The average customer has $96.5 billion in revenue, so let's design the standard plan for a firm about that size.”

But 35 of the 50 firms earn less than $100 billion, and the firm in the middle of the list earns $70.5 billion. Who is right? And could you have spotted the problem from two numbers on the Excel output, without staring at all fifty rows?

Shape comes before summary. Check which way the tail runs and how heavy it is, and only then pick your average, your spread and your test.

Level 1 · Basics

The idea in plain words

The shape of a distribution is its silhouette: what you see if you squint at a histogram. Three questions describe it.

Is it lopsided?Skewness: does one tail stretch further than the other?
How heavy are the tails?Kurtosis: are extreme values rare, or surprisingly common?
Is anyone far out on their own?Outliers: single values that sit well away from the rest.
Think of it like

A kite. The body is the hump where most values sit; the tail streams off to one side. Skew is named after the tail, not the body. Tail flying off to the right (towards bigger numbers) means right-skewed, also called positively skewed. Tail to the left means left-skewed, or negatively skewed.

Why does the mean move and the median doesn't? The mean is a balance point. Put one very large value far out on the right and the balance point has to slide right to stay level. The median just counts heads: it's the value with half the data on each side, so a single giant only counts as “one more person on the right”, however giant it is.

That gives you a quick test you can do from any summary table: mean bigger than median → probably right-skewed; mean smaller than median → probably left-skewed; about equal → roughly symmetric. In LargeCorp, $96.5 bn is well above $70.5 bn. Right skew, before you've drawn a single chart.

Symmetric
The left half is a mirror image of the right half. Mean ≈ median ≈ mode. Example: weights of machine-filled 1 kg sugar packs.
Right-skewed (positive)
Long tail towards high values. Mean > median. The business default: incomes, house prices, claim sizes, order values.
Left-skewed (negative)
Long tail towards low values. Mean < median. Example: scores on an easy test, where most students are near the top.
Tail
The thin stretch at either end of a distribution where only a few values live.
Skewness coefficient
One number for lopsidedness: 0 for symmetric, positive for right skew, negative for left skew. Excel: SKEW.
Kurtosis
One number for tail-heaviness compared with a bell curve. Excel's KURT is 0 for a normal curve.
Outlier
A value unusually far from the rest: more than 3 standard deviations from the mean, or outside the box-plot fences.
Mode (peak)
The most common value, or the tallest bar of the histogram.

Spot the wall

Here is a trick that predicts the shape of most business variables before you collect any data. Look for a wall: a limit the values cannot cross. Nobody earns less than zero. A food order can't arrive faster than the time it takes to cook and ride. A test can't score above 100. Values pile up against the wall, and the tail runs off the other way.

Right-skewed (wall on the left)

Incomes and salaries · house and flat prices · delivery and waiting times · insurance claim sizes · customer spend per order · company revenues · number of complaints per day.

Left-skewed (wall on the right)

Scores on an easy exam · lifetimes of well-made products that wear out (tyres, batteries) · age at retirement · “percent of target achieved” when most staff hit close to 100%.

Why the two numbers disagree

Pearson's coefficient only looks at one thing: the gap between mean and median, measured in SDs. SKEW looks at every value, and because it cubes each z-score, a value 4 SDs out counts 4³ = 64 times as much as a value 1 SD out. So in data with a couple of giants, SKEW explodes while Pearson's stays modest.

Don't mix the scales

Rules of thumb such as “beyond ±1 is substantial” were built for SKEW. Pearson's coefficient runs smaller on heavy-tailed data, so a Pearson value of 0.97 is already a strong signal. Always say which measure you used.

Kurtosis: how heavy are the tails? Light coverage

Two datasets can have the same mean, the same SD and zero skew, and still behave very differently at the edges. Kurtosis measures how much of the spread comes from rare, extreme values rather than from the many ordinary ones.

Leptokurtic · KURT > 0

Heavy tails. Extreme values turn up more often than a bell curve predicts. Daily stock returns, insurance claims, LargeCorp revenue (10.54).

Mesokurtic · KURT ≈ 0

Tails like a normal curve. Measurement errors, fill weights. PovertyLevel (−0.29) is close.

Platykurtic · KURT < 0

Light tails, flat top. Values spread evenly with few extremes: e.g. a quantity that is capped at both ends.

Exam tip

Excel's KURT already subtracts 3. A normal curve therefore gives KURT = 0, not 3. If a book quotes “kurtosis = 3 for a normal distribution”, it is using raw (non-excess) kurtosis, which is roughly KURT + 3 in large samples.

Properties worth remembering

  • The sign gives the direction, the size gives the strength. 0 means symmetric, not “no data”.
  • Unit-free. Measure revenue in $ billion or ₹ crore and SKEW is identical, because it's built from z-scores.
  • Very sensitive to single values. Drop IBM from the 30 DJIA prices and SKEW falls from 1.11 to 0.41.
  • Noisy in small samples. With 10 observations a SKEW of 0.8 could easily be luck. See the Pro section.
  • Excel returns #DIV/0! if n<3n \lt 3n<3 for SKEW or n<4n \lt 4n<4 for KURT, or if all values are equal.

Level 3 · Application

Where managers use it

HR · Compensation

Salary benchmarking

Pay is right-skewed: a few senior packages pull the mean up. Benchmark grades on the median and the quartiles, and use the mean only for budgeting the total wage bill (total = mean × headcount).

Operations · Logistics

Delivery promises

Delivery times have a hard floor and a long right tail. Promise a time from the 90th percentile, not the mean: with a typical right-skewed spread, 30–40% of orders take longer than the mean.

Finance · Risk

Fat-tailed returns

Daily returns have positive kurtosis: crashes happen far more often than a bell curve allows. Risk teams check KURT before trusting any normal-based loss estimate.

Marketing · CRM

Customer value

Spend per customer is right-skewed with a few “whales”. Report the median basket, and segment the top 5% separately instead of letting them inflate the average.

Manufacturing · Quality

Fill weights

A healthy filling line gives a symmetric spread around the target. If skew appears, something is pushing against a limit: a stuck valve, a machine running against its stop.

Insurance · Banking

Claims and loan sizes

A handful of very large claims or loans dominate the totals. Reserves and capital are set from the tail (high percentiles), never from the mean alone.

Indian context

Every placement season, B-schools announce an “average CTC” and a “highest CTC”. Salaries are right-skewed, so one or two international offers can lift the average well above what most graduates receive. That's why India's NIRF ranking framework asks institutions for the median salary. When you read a placement report, look for the median first.

  2. Investigate the outliersIs ₹3,200 one customer buying gifts for a wedding, or a reseller? If it's a reseller, it belongs to a different population and should be analysed separately. Without it the mean falls to ₹585.
  3. Anchor on the medianA threshold set 25% above the mean (₹949) leaves 12 of 15 customers below it, and only one of them within ₹200 of it. Set 25% above the median (₹650): 10 customers are below, and 5 of them are within ₹200, which is the nudge zone.
  4. Decide and testGo with roughly ₹649 and A/B test it against the current policy. The mean was never a sensible anchor here because it describes almost nobody.
Level 4 · Pro

How shape decides mean vs median, and which tests are safe later

Most of the inferential tools you'll meet later (confidence intervals, t-tests, regression inference) assume something is normal. Shape tells you whether that assumption is safe.

  • For means, sample size rescues you, up to a point. Anderson et al. (Ch. 7–9) give the working rule: with n≥30n \ge 30n≥30 the sampling distribution of xˉ\bar{x}xˉ is close enough to normal in most applications; if the population is highly skewed or has outliers, aim for about 50; with n<15n \lt 15n<15 you need the data themselves to look roughly normal. See Sampling and Estimation.
  • Small, skewed samples: switch to the median and a non-parametric test (Mann–Whitney U, Wilcoxon), covered under Hypothesis tests.
  • Large n doesn't fix everything. The CLT helps the mean. If you need a percentile, a prediction for one customer, or a risk limit, the shape of the raw data still matters however big n is.
  • Regression assumes the residuals are roughly normal, not the raw variables. Skewed residuals are often cured by logging Y. See Regression.
Is the skew real or just noise?

A rough standard error for SKEW is 6/n\sqrt{6/n}6/n​. For n=50n = 50n=50 that's 0.35, so PovertyLevel's 0.57 is only about 1.6 standard errors from zero: mild, and possibly chance. LargeCorp's 3.14 is about 9 standard errors out: very real. For KURT the rough standard error is 24/n\sqrt{24/n}24/n​, 0.69 at n=50n = 50n=50. This is why nobody should read much into the SKEW of ten observations.

Transformations: taming a long tail with logs

When a variable is money, a size or a count, taking logarithms often turns a right-skewed mess into something close to symmetric. Logs turn multiplication into addition: 10 → 1, 100 → 2, 1,000 → 3. Big values get squeezed together; small ones get spread apart.

  • Back-transform carefully. The mean of the logs is 1.9060, and 101.9060=80.5310^{1.9060} = 80.53101.9060=80.53: the geometric mean, $80.5 bn. It sits between the median ($70.5 bn) and the arithmetic mean ($96.5 bn).
  • Logs need positive values. LN(0) is an error. For counts with zeros, analysts use LN(x+1).
  • Ranks don't change, so medians and percentiles simply transform across: the median of the logs is the log of the median.
  • Natural log (LN) or base 10 (LOG10) give the same SKEW; pick whichever reads better.

The fat-tails story in finance Light coverage

Slide 15 of the class deck makes the key point: when excess kurtosis is positive, extreme events are more common than a bell curve predicts, so risk models built on the normal curve understate losses. Here is how big the gap can be.

Under the normal model a 5-SD day should come along roughly once in 6,900 years. Under a fat-tailed model with the same SD it's every three years or so. Real markets have behaved much more like the second: on 19 October 1987 the Dow Jones fell 22.6% in a single day, a move a normal model treats as essentially impossible. So risk teams check KURT, stress-test with historical crashes and avoid assuming normality for the tails.

Kurtosis is about tails, not peaks

Older textbooks (including Levine et al.) describe kurtosis as “peakedness”. The modern view is that it's driven almost entirely by the tails: because of the fourth power, values more than 1 SD from the mean supply about 96% of the total even for a normal curve. A distribution can have a flat top and high kurtosis if its tails are heavy. When you report KURT, talk about extremes, not about pointy peaks.

What to do with an outlier

  • Error: a salary of ₹12,50,000 a month typed instead of ₹1,25,000; revenue entered in ₹ instead of ₹ crore. Fix it from the source document.
  • Different population: a ₹4 lakh bulk corporate order in a file of retail baskets. Analyse it as its own segment.
  • Real and relevant: Exxon Mobil and Walmart really are that big. Keep them. Report the median and IQR, and show the effect: LargeCorp's mean is $96.52 bn with all 50 firms and $75.72 bn without the four fence outliers; the median moves only from $70.5 bn to $64.5 bn.

Resistant alternatives if you must summarise with one number: the median, the IQR, or a trimmed mean such as =TRIMMEAN(B2:B51,0.1), which trims 10% of the values in total, split between the two ends, before averaging (Excel rounds the count down to an even number, so with 50 values it drops 2 from each end).

How a consultant would present it

“Revenue among America's 50 largest corporations is heavily right-skewed (skewness 3.1). Two firms, Exxon Mobil ($443 bn) and Walmart ($406 bn), together earn nearly as much as the 17 smallest firms combined. So we describe the typical firm by the median, $70.5 bn, with the middle half between $54.8 bn and $105 bn, and we price the enterprise plan around that band, with a separate offer for the four giants.”

Interview-style insight

Asked “mean or median for house prices?”, everyone says median. The strong answer says why (a wall at zero and a few mansions create right skew, so the mean overstates the typical home) and what else you'd show (the IQR, a histogram, and the number of listings above ₹5 crore).

Common mistakes

WrongRight
“The peak is on the left, so it's left-skewed.”Skew is named after the tail. Peak on the left with a tail to the right is right-skewed.
Judging a Pearson coefficient of 0.9 against the “±1 is substantial” rule and calling it mild.The ±1 rule is for SKEW. Pearson's coefficient runs smaller; 0.9 is already strong. Name the measure you used.
“KURT = 0, so the data have no kurtosis.”Excel reports excess kurtosis. 0 means tails like a normal curve; a normal curve's raw kurtosis is 3.
Deleting outliers to “clean” the data before analysis.Investigate first. Fix errors, separate different populations, keep genuine values and report with and without.
Using only |z| > 3 to find outliers in very skewed data.The outliers inflate the SD and hide themselves (Chevron has z = 2.07). Use the 1.5 × IQR fences as well.
Reading a lot into SKEW from 10 observations.SKEW is noisy for small n (standard error about 6/n\sqrt{6/n}6/n​). Look at the histogram and the mean–median gap too.
Reporting the mean of logged data as if it were the ordinary mean.10mean of log1010^{\text{mean of log}_{10}}10mean of log10​ is the geometric mean ($80.5 bn for LargeCorp), not the arithmetic mean ($96.5 bn).
“n is 200, so shape doesn't matter.”Large n protects tests about the mean. Percentiles, single predictions and risk limits still depend on the shape.

Quick check

A histogram of customer spend per order has a tall hump at the low end and a long tail stretching to the right. Which statement is most likely true?

  1. Mean < median, SKEW negative
  2. Mean > median, SKEW positive
  3. Mean = median, SKEW = 0
  4. Mean > median, SKEW negative
The tail is on the right, so the data are right (positively) skewed. The few big orders pull the mean above the median, and SKEW is positive.

A sample of monthly mobile bills has mean ₹520, median ₹470 and standard deviation ₹125. What is Pearson's coefficient of skewness?

  1. 0.40
  2. 0.12
  3. 1.20
  4. 3.60
SkP=3(520−470)/125=150/125=1.20Sk_P = 3(520 - 470)/125 = 150/125 = 1.20SkP​=3(520−470)/125=150/125=1.20. Option (a) forgets the 3, (b) slips a decimal place, and (d) multiplies by 3 twice. Positive, so right-skewed, and strongly.

Excel gives KURT = 4.2 for a fund's daily returns. What does this tell the risk manager?

  1. Returns are left-skewed.
  2. Returns are almost exactly normal, because kurtosis near 3 is normal.
  3. The fund has a very low standard deviation.
  4. Extreme days happen more often than a normal curve predicts.
KURT is excess kurtosis, so a normal curve gives 0. A value of 4.2 means heavy tails (leptokurtic): big gains and losses are more frequent than the bell curve says. It says nothing about direction (that's skewness) or the size of the SD.

For a set of loan amounts (₹ lakh), Q1 = 40 and Q3 = 60. Which of these loans is an outlier by the 1.5 × IQR rule?

  1. ₹96 lakh
  2. ₹89 lakh
  3. ₹90 lakh
  4. ₹12 lakh
IQR = 20, so the fences are 40 − 30 = 10 and 60 + 30 = 90. Only ₹96 lakh lies outside (₹90 lakh is exactly on the fence, and ₹12 lakh is above the lower fence of 10).

House prices in a city have SKEW = 2.6 and two mansions far above everything else. What should a property portal do?

  1. Delete the two mansions and report the mean.
  2. Report the mean and SD; with SKEW above 2 they are still fine.
  3. Lead with the median and IQR, keep the mansions, and mention them.
  4. Report the mode, because skewed data have no meaningful median.
High skew with genuine extreme values calls for resistant summaries (median, IQR). The mansions are real, so they stay in the data and get a footnote rather than a silent delete.

One-glance summary

Shape in 60 seconds

  • Skew is named after the tail. Right (positive): mean > median. Left (negative): mean < median. Symmetric: mean ≈ median.
  • Look for the wall. A floor (zero, minimum time) gives right skew; a ceiling (100 marks, design life) gives left skew.
  • Hand formula: SkP=3(xˉ−Md)/sSk_P = 3(\bar{x} - M_d)/sSkP​=3(xˉ−Md​)/s, within ±3. Excel: =SKEW(range); |SKEW| < 0.5 symmetric, 0.5–1 moderate, > 1 high.
  • Kurtosis (light): =KURT(range) is excess kurtosis. 0 = normal tails, > 0 heavy tails (leptokurtic), < 0 light tails (platykurtic).
  • Outliers: |z| > 3 (STANDARDIZE) or outside Q1−1.5 IQRQ_1 - 1.5\,\text{IQR}Q1​−1.5IQR, Q3+1.5 IQRQ_3 + 1.5\,\text{IQR}Q3​+1.5IQR. Investigate; never silently delete.
  • Decision: skewed or outliers → median and IQR, check n ≥ 50 before mean-based tests, consider a log transform.
  • Write it like this: “The data are [right/left]-skewed (SKEW = __): the mean (__) is [above/below] the median (__) because of [the tail]. We therefore report the median.”
