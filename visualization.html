The manager's question

The manager's question

Your customer-experience head opens the Monday review with a 3D pie chart: seven coloured slices showing why 1,000 online orders came back last month. “Fit issues are a big problem,” she says.

The CEO asks three things. How big? Compared with what? And what do we fix first? The pie can't answer any of them quickly. A sorted bar chart with a running-total line (Figure 4 on this page) answers all three in about five seconds: two causes explain 61% of all returns.

The chart follows the data type and the question, not your taste. Get those two right and the picture does the talking.

Level 1 · Basics

The idea in plain words

Every chart on this page starts life as a table of counts. You sort the data into groups, count each group, and then draw the counts so the eye can compare them.

Think of it like

Sorting a shoebox of receipts into labelled trays. The trays and the count in each tray are the frequency distribution. The chart is a photograph of the trays, taken from the angle that makes the important difference obvious.

Before you draw anything, ask two questions. What kind of data is it? Categories (labels like brand, region, rating) or quantities (numbers on a scale like price or time)? And what is the question? Compare, show a share, rank causes, show a distribution, compare groups, show a relationship, or show change over time.

Frequency distribution
A table listing each category (or class) and how many observations fall in it.
Relative frequency
The fraction of all observations in a group: frequency ÷ n. The relative frequencies add to 1.
Percent frequency
Relative frequency × 100. Adds to 100%.
Class (bin)
For numbers: an interval such as 20–29 into which values are grouped.
Class width
The size of each interval (10 for 20–29, 30–39, …). Keep it equal for all classes.
Class limits · midpoint
The smallest and largest values a class can hold (20 and 29), and the point halfway between them (24.5).
Cumulative frequency
The running total: how many observations are in this class or any lower one.
Histogram · ogive
A bar chart of a frequency distribution for numbers (no gaps), and a line chart of its cumulative percentages.
Box plot
A picture of the five-number summary (min, Q1, median, Q3, max) with outliers marked.
Crosstabulation
A two-way table of counts for two variables at once, e.g. region × poverty band. Excel builds it with a PivotTable.

Bar (column) chart

One bar per category, gaps between bars, axis starting at zero. Nominal data (brands, reasons): sort bars from tallest to shortest. Ordinal data (ratings): keep the scale order, Excellent → Poor.

Pie chart

Only for parts of one whole, ideally five slices or fewer with clearly different sizes. Never 3D. If people must compare slices, use a bar chart instead.

Pareto chart

A bar chart sorted from largest to smallest, plus a line of cumulative percentages. It turns a list of problems into a priority list: “the vital few”.

Quantitative data: building classes

For numbers you first have to invent the categories, called classes. Three decisions, in this order:

1 · How many classes?Usually 5 to 20 (Anderson et al.); the class deck says 5 to 15. A quick rule: the smallest k with 2k ≥ n.
2 · How wide?Approximate width = (largest − smallest) ÷ k, rounded up to a convenient number. Same width for every class.
3 · Which limits?Start at or just below the smallest value. Classes must not overlap and every value must fall in exactly one class.

Five ways to draw one column of numbers

Histogram

Bars over the classes, touching each other. Shows shape, centre, spread and gaps at a glance. Bar area represents frequency, which matters if widths ever differ (see Pro).

Frequency polygon

Join the tops of the histogram bars at the class midpoints, and close the shape with a zero at each end. Handy for overlaying two groups on one chart.

Ogive

Cumulative percentages plotted at the upper class limits. Read off “what share is below X?” or “below what value is half the data?”.

Dot plot and stem-and-leaf

For small datasets: every value stays visible. A stem-and-leaf display also keeps the digits, so you can read the actual data back.

Box plots: five numbers, one picture

A box plot draws the five-number summary. The box runs from Q1 to Q3 (the middle half of the data), a line inside marks the median, whiskers reach out to the most extreme values that are not outliers, and dots mark outliers beyond the fences Q1−1.5 IQRQ_1 - 1.5\,\text{IQR}Q1​−1.5IQR and Q3+1.5 IQRQ_3 + 1.5\,\text{IQR}Q3​+1.5IQR.

  1. Find the centreThe median line: $32 for the DJIA.
  2. Read the spreadThe box width is the IQR ($30.25); whisker to whisker shows the range of typical values.
  3. Read the shapeMedian off-centre inside the box, or one whisker much longer than the other, means skew. Here the right side is longer: right skew.
  4. Spot the outliersDots beyond the fences. IBM at $107 is past the upper fence of $99.125.

Box plots really earn their keep when you compare groups. Five histograms would need five charts; five box plots fit on one.

Worked example 3

Row or column percentages in a crosstab?

Using the region × poverty-band crosstab above (class dataset PovertyLevel): (a) What percentage of Southeast states have child poverty of 20% or more? (b) What percentage of the high-poverty (20%+) states are in the Southeast? Why are the answers different?

Given

Interpret

Different denominators answer different questions. Row %: “if a state is in the Southeast, how likely is high poverty?” Column %: “of all high-poverty states, how many are Southeastern?” Always say which one you computed. Explore it

The 30 DJIA prices from Problem 3. Start at $10 with width $10 to match the class solution, then experiment.
  • Change the width to 20 (the 2k rule's suggestion). The second bump at $50–59 merges into its neighbour. Which picture would you show a manager, and why?
  • Try width 5. Do you learn anything new, or is it just noise?
  • Move the start from 10 to 5 with width 10. The same data, a slightly different story: that's why you always state your classes.

Level 3 · Application

Where managers use it

Marketing · CX

Complaint and NPS dashboards

A Pareto of complaint reasons each month shows whether the “vital few” are shrinking. A bar chart of NPS categories (promoters, passives, detractors) by region shows where to send the team.

Finance

Returns and fund comparison

A histogram of daily returns reveals fat tails; side-by-side box plots compare mutual-fund categories on median return and spread in one picture.

HR

Pay equity and attrition

Box plots of salary by grade and gender show overlaps and outliers better than average-pay tables. A Pareto of exit-interview reasons ranks what to fix.

Operations

Process times vs the SLA

A histogram of turnaround times with the service-level limit drawn on it shows how many cases breach and whether the problem is a long tail or a shifted centre.

Retail · E-commerce

Region × category crosstabs

A PivotTable of sales by region and category, shown as row percentages, reveals each region's product mix even when total sales differ hugely.

Banking

Loan-size ogive

The ogive of loan amounts answers policy questions directly: what share of loans is below ₹10 lakh, or which loan size covers 90% of applications.

Indian context

Label money the way your readers count it. Axes that read 10,000,000 make an Indian board pause; “₹1 crore” or “₹100 lakh” does not. In Excel, either show values in ₹ lakh or ₹ crore (divide by 1,00,000 or 1,00,00,000 in a helper column), or use the custom number format [>=10000000]##\,##\,##\,##0;[>=100000]##\,##\,##0;##,##0 to get Indian digit grouping such as 12,34,567.

Mini-case: one page for the monthly review

Suppose a retail chain's leadership wants a single page for its monthly review. The analyst asks what they decide each month (where to push, what is trending, what is going wrong) and gives each question exactly one chart.

  1. Lead with three or four KPIsRevenue ₹4.82 crore (up 4.8% on September's ₹4.60 crore), 38,420 orders, average order value ₹4.82 crore ÷ 38,420 = ₹1,255, return rate 6.1%.
  2. One question, one chartWhere? Sorted bars by region. Trend? A line (a line chart may zoom its axis; bars may not). Problems? A Pareto. Customers? Box plots by store format, which show that online orders have the lowest median (₹775) but by far the longest tail of big orders.
  3. Same colour, same meaningThe accent colour marks the one thing to look at in each panel; everything else is neutral.
  4. Build it from PivotTablesPivotCharts plus a Slicer (PivotTable Analyze › Insert Slicer) let the reader filter every chart by month or region at once.

Nuance, pitfalls and depth

Honest and misleading charts

The Module 1 deck has a slide on exactly this: the same numbers on two axes give two completely different board meetings.

TrickWhy it misleadsHonest fix
Truncated axis on barsBar length is the message; cutting the axis exaggerates differences.Bars start at zero. Line charts may zoom in, but label the axis clearly.
3D pies and 3D barsPerspective makes front slices look bigger and hides the true heights.Flat 2D charts, always.
Dual y-axesYou can pick two scales that make any two series look related, or unrelated.Two panels side by side, or index both series to 100 at the start.
Cherry-picked time windowStarting at a low point manufactures a “recovery”.Show a full cycle, and say why the window was chosen.
Raw counts across unequal groupsA big region always has more of everything.Percentages or rates (per 1,000 customers), as on slide 27.
Pictograms scaled in two directionsDoubling height and width makes the icon four times bigger.Scale one dimension only, or use bars.
Unequal class widths drawn as countsA wide class looks huge just because it is wide.Equal widths, or plot frequency density (below).

Unequal class widths and frequency density

A histogram's message is in the bar areas. If one class is wider, its bar must be drawn lower. Suppose someone groups LargeCorp into 0–49, 50–99 and 100–449. The last class holds 15 firms but is 350 wide, against 50 for the others.

Frequency densityLight
frequency density=fclass width\text{frequency density} = \frac{f}{\text{class width}}frequency density=class widthf​
Example
0–49: 6/50 = 0.12 · 50–99: 29/50 = 0.58 · 100–449: 15/350 = 0.043 firms per $ billion

Drawn as plain counts, the 100–449 bar (15) would look half as tall as the 50–99 bar (29) and suggest lots of firms up there. Drawn as densities, it is a long, low slab, which is the truth: those 15 firms are spread thinly over a huge range.

Storytelling with data

The syllabus lists Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic's Storytelling with Data as a supplementary reference. Its core advice fits on one figure.

  • Action title. Write the finding (“Only 12% rated the Fed's handling good or excellent”), not the topic (“Chart 1: Ratings”).
  • Declutter. Remove gridlines, borders, legends and 3D effects that carry no information. Label bars directly.
  • Highlight one thing. Grey for context, one accent colour for the message.
  • Order on purpose. Scale order for ordinal data, largest-first for nominal data, time order for time.
  • One chart, one message. If you need two messages, draw two charts.

Histogram or box plot?

Histogram

Shows the full shape: peaks, gaps, a second bump (the DJIA's $50–59 cluster), the exact tail. Best for one group, or a few at most.

vs

Box plot

Shows centre, spread, skew and outliers compactly, but hides peaks and gaps. Best for comparing many groups on one axis.

The number of classes changes the story too. With width 20 the DJIA frequencies become 15, 5, 8, 1, 1: the second peak survives, but it is now smeared across the whole 50–69 class, so you can no longer see that it sits in the $50s. Neither picture is wrong, but report your classes and check that your conclusion survives a different choice (try it in the explorer above).

How a consultant would present it

“Public confidence in the Fed's handling of the credit crisis is low. In a poll of 1,015 adults, only 12% rated it good or excellent, while 36% said bad or terrible; the most common verdict, from just over half, was ‘fair’. Our recommendation should assume a sceptical public.” One chart (the “after” version above), one sentence, one implication.

Exam tip

For any “construct a chart” question in a pen-and-paper exam: label both axes with units, give the chart a title, start bar axes at zero, keep ordinal categories in order, leave no gaps in a histogram, and write two sentences of interpretation under it. Those are the marks most students drop.

(d) How to do it

This part needs today's prices, which you collect yourself; we don't make them up here. A good answer follows these steps:

  1. Collect. On one date, record the closing price of each of the 30 companies from a financial newspaper or a market-data website, and note the date and source.
  2. Check the list. The DJIA has changed several times since 2009, so some of these 30 companies have left the index. Track the same 30 companies (the question is about them), and note which ones are no longer in the index.
  3. Adjust for splits. Many companies have split their shares since 2009, which cuts the price per share without anyone losing money. Use split-adjusted prices (the “adjusted close” on historical-price pages) or the comparison is meaningless.
  4. Rebuild the table and histogram. Prices will have moved, so you may need a wider class width (say $25 or $50) to keep 5–15 classes. Use the same steps and the Histogram tool as above.
  5. Compare and rank. Add a column for the change, current − 2009, and one for % change, (current − 2009)/2009 × 100. Sort to find the largest increase and the largest decrease, and say whether you ranked on $ or % change (they can differ).
  6. Discuss. Has the centre moved? Is the shape still right-skewed? Are there new outliers?

Common mistakes

WrongRight
Gaps between the bars of a histogram.Histograms have no gaps (the scale is continuous). Bar charts of categories do have gaps.
Overlapping classes like 10–20, 20–30.Every value must fit exactly one class: 10–19, 20–29 (or “10 to under 20”).
Unequal class widths drawn as plain counts.Use equal widths; if you can't, plot frequency density = f ÷ width.
Sorting ordinal categories by frequency (Very Good, Excellent, Good…).Keep the scale order for ordinal data; sort largest-first only for nominal data.
A pie chart of quantitative data, or a pie with 12 slices.Pies only for parts of one whole, ≤ 5 slices. Numbers on a scale get a histogram.
Bar chart axis starting at 31% “to show the change better”.Bars start at zero. If the change is small, say so, or show the change itself.
Comparing raw counts across groups of different size.Use row or column percentages, and say which.
A chart with no title, units or source.Title stating the finding, labelled axes with units, data source underneath.

Quick check

A bank wants to show how 2,400 loan applications are distributed by loan amount (₹). Which chart fits?

  1. Pie chart
  2. Pareto chart
  3. Histogram
  4. Line chart
Loan amount is quantitative and the question is about its distribution, so group it into classes and draw a histogram. Pie and Pareto charts are for categories; a line chart is for change over time.

A dataset has 60 observations, smallest 12 and largest 95. Using the 2k ≥ n rule, what is the approximate class width?

  1. About 14, so use 15
  2. About 17, so use 20
  3. About 8, so use 10
  4. About 1.4, so use 2

In a box plot, the median line sits close to the left end of the box and the right whisker is much longer than the left. What does this suggest?

  1. The data are symmetric.
  2. The data are left-skewed.
  3. There are no outliers.
  4. The data are right-skewed.
More stretch on the high side (inside the box and in the whisker) means a long right tail: right skew. The plot says nothing definite about outliers unless dots appear beyond the fences.

In a Pareto analysis of complaints, cumulative percentages are 38%, 64%, 79%, 88%, 95%, 100%. What share of complaints do the top three causes explain?

  1. 64%
  2. 79%
  3. 38% + 64% + 79% = 181%
  4. 21%
The cumulative % already adds the causes up: the third value, 79%, is the share explained by the first three causes together. Adding cumulative percentages again double-counts.

A chart shows quarterly market share of 31.2%, 31.5%, 31.9% and 32.4% as bars, with the axis starting at 31%. What is the honest summary?

  1. Share has grown about sevenfold over the year.
  2. Share has stayed exactly flat.
  3. Share rose by 1.2 percentage points, about 3.8%, in the year.
  4. Share rose by 32.4%.
32.4 − 31.2 = 1.2 percentage points, and 1.2/31.2 = 3.8% relative growth. The truncated axis makes the Q4 bar look 7 times taller than Q1's, which is the illusion in option (a).

One-glance summary

Visualization in 60 seconds

  • Data type first. Categories → frequency table, bar (sorted if nominal, in order if ordinal), pie (≤ 5 parts of a whole), Pareto (priorities). Numbers → classes, histogram, polygon, ogive, dot plot, box plot.
  • Frequencies: relative = f/n, percent = 100 f/n, cumulative = running total; the last cumulative value equals n.
  • Classes: 5–20 classes (smallest k with 2k ≥ n); width ≈ (max − min)/k rounded up; equal widths; no overlaps.
  • Two variables: crosstab / PivotTable (two categoricals; use row or column %), scatter diagram (two numbers), box plots (number by group).
  • Excel: COUNTIF, PivotTable, FREQUENCY with upper-limit bins, Data Analysis › Histogram, Insert › Statistical › Histogram / Pareto / Box and Whisker.
  • Honesty: bars start at zero, no 3D, no dual axes, percentages for unequal groups, title + units + source on every chart.
  • Write it like this: “The [chart] shows that [finding with a number]. Most values lie in [class]; the distribution is [shape]; [implication for the manager].”
