The manager's question

The manager's question

Your agency sends a campaign plan with a confident line in it: “Half of your shoppers are women, and 15.4% say social media influences what they buy. So a campaign aimed at women or social-media-influenced shoppers reaches 50% + 15.4% = 65.4% of your base.”

Your finance head reads it and frowns. “Aren't some of those women also influenced by social media? Are we paying for the same people twice?” Who is right, and what is the real number?

Probability is counting done carefully: list everything that can happen, count the ways your event can happen, and never count anyone twice.

Every manager already talks in probabilities, even without the word. “Most orders arrive on time.” “This client will probably renew.” “Defaults are rare in this segment.” This page gives you the grammar behind those sentences, so that “probably” becomes a number you can defend in a meeting and write on an exam sheet.

Level 1 · Basics

The idea in plain words

A probability is a number between 0 and 1 that says how likely something is. 0 means it cannot happen. 1 means it is certain. Everything interesting lives in between.

Managers face uncertainty every single day. Will tomorrow's demand cross 500 units? What share of new loans will default? If we send this offer to 10,000 customers, how many will buy? None of these can be known for sure in advance. But each can be given a probability, and once you have that, you can plan stock, price risk and set targets.

Think of it like

The win-probability bar you see during a cricket broadcast. At the start of a chase it might read 50–50. After three quick wickets it swings to 20–80. Nobody knows the result yet, but the number tells you how worried to be. A probability is exactly that: a measure of how much you should expect something, before you know.

The vocabulary

Six words do almost all the work in this topic. Learn them once and the rest of the pillar reads easily.

Experiment
Any process with a well-defined set of possible results, where you can't say in advance which one you'll get. A sales call, a loan application, a customer rating a delivery.
Outcome (sample point)
One possible result of the experiment. “Purchase” or “no purchase”. Each time you run the experiment, exactly one outcome happens.
Sample space, S
The full list of outcomes. For a 1–5 star rating, S = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5}.
Event
A collection of outcomes you care about. “Customer is a promoter” = {4, 5}. An event can contain one outcome or many.
Complement, AcA^cAc
Everything in S that is not in A. The complement of “promoter” is {1, 2, 3}.
Probability, P(A)
A number from 0 to 1 measuring how likely event A is. Found by adding the probabilities of the outcomes inside A.

Notice what happened in panel 3. To get the probability of an event, you simply add up the probabilities of the outcomes inside it. That one idea, plus careful counting, is most of this topic.

Step 2: assign the probabilities

Anderson et al. describe three legitimate ways to put a number on an outcome. Managers use all three, often in the same meeting.

Classical

Outcomes are equally likely, so each gets 1/n. Drawing one of 12 branches at random for an audit: each has probability 1/12.

Use only when “equally likely” is genuinely true.

Relative frequency

Use data: how often did it happen before? 579 of 746 analytics professionals rated data analysis critical, so P = 579/746 = 0.78.

Needs plenty of data from a stable process.

Subjective

Use informed judgment when there is no clean data: “I'd put the chance of winning this tender at 50–50.”

Still must obey the rules below. Combine with data when you can.

Worked example 3

Overlapping payment habits (addition law from percentages)

A survey of a food-delivery app's users (hypothetical) finds that 40% have paid by UPI in the last month, 30% by credit card, and 18% by both. What share used UPI or a card? What share used neither? What share used UPI only?

Given
P(U)=0.40, P(C)=0.30, P(U∩C)=0.18P(U) = 0.40,\ P(C) = 0.30,\ P(U \cap C) = 0.18P(U)=0.40, P(C)=0.30, P(U∩C)=0.18

Interpret

Just over half the users pay digitally with UPI or a card. Adding 40% and 30% would have claimed 70%, overstating the reach by 18 points because the “both” group was counted twice. Figure 4 is this exact example.

Why can't I just add 40% and 30%?

Because the 18% who use both are inside the 40% and inside the 30%. Adding counts them twice. Draw the Venn diagram, write the overlap in the middle first, then fill in “UPI only” (0.40 − 0.18) and “card only” (0.30 − 0.18). The union is the sum of the three regions: 0.22 + 0.18 + 0.12 = 0.52. Same answer, no formula needed.

Level 3 · Application

Where managers use it

Marketing

Campaign reach without double counting

Audiences overlap across Instagram, YouTube and email lists. Total reach is a union, so the overlap must be subtracted once. Agencies that add raw audience sizes overstate reach.

Banking & credit

Default rates by segment

If 1,200 of 40,000 personal-loan accounts defaulted last year, the relative-frequency estimate is 1,200/40,000 = 0.03. Credit policy and loan pricing start from numbers like this.

Operations

Stock-out risk

Demand exceeded shelf stock on 22 of the last 365 days, so P(stock-out on a given day) ≈ 22/365 = 0.060. Is 6% acceptable for this product? That is a business decision, but now it's an informed one.

HR

Attrition cross-tabs

Cross-tabulate exits by department and tenure band. Joint cells show where the leavers sit; margins show overall attrition. The same table feeds the conditional questions on the next page.

E-commerce

Returns and the complement

If 15% of fashion orders come back, the complement (85% kept) is what revenue planning really needs. Complements turn “bad news” rates into planning numbers.

Hospitality

No-shows and overbooking

If 8% of bookings are no-shows, a 200-room hotel expects about 200 × 0.08 = 16 empty rooms on a full night. That relative frequency is the starting point of every overbooking policy.

Indian context

Cash on delivery is still common in Indian e-commerce, and it brings refused deliveries. Suppose a Dehradun D2C brand (hypothetical) had 2,400 orders in its Diwali month: 900 COD and 1,500 prepaid. 180 COD orders and 90 prepaid orders came back. Then P(COD) = 900/2,400 = 0.375, P(returned) = 270/2,400 = 0.1125, P(COD and returned) = 180/2,400 = 0.075, and P(COD or returned) = (900 + 270 − 180)/2,400 = 990/2,400 = 0.4125. So only 1 − 0.4125 = 0.5875 of orders were the easy kind: prepaid and kept. Is a returned order more likely if it was COD? That's a “given” question, and the next page answers it (0.20 vs 0.06).

How you'd say it in the meeting

“About 55 in every 100 shoppers are women or say social media sways them. The 65% figure double-counts the 10 in 100 who are both.” Natural frequencies (“55 in 100”) land better with a leadership team than “0.552”.

Level 4 · Pro

Nuance, pitfalls and depth

Relative frequency needs volume (and a stable process)

A relative frequency is only an estimate. With a handful of observations it jumps around; with thousands it settles near the true value. This is the law of large numbers, and it's why a conversion rate from the first 20 visitors means very little.

Two cautions. First, the process must be stable: last year's default rate is a poor guide if lending rules or the economy changed. Second, the data must come from the population you care about. A survey of app users tells you about app users, not about all customers.

“At least one” is easiest through the complement

A quality inspector takes 4 items from a lot of 20 that secretly contains 3 defectives. What is the chance the sample catches at least one defective? Counting “one or two or three defectives” is painful. Count the opposite instead: no defectives at all means all 4 come from the 17 good items.

P(none)=(174)(204)=23804845=0.4912,P(at least one)=1−0.4912=0.5088P(\text{none}) = \frac{\binom{17}{4}}{\binom{20}{4}} = \frac{2380}{4845} = 0.4912, \qquad P(\text{at least one}) = 1 - 0.4912 = 0.5088P(none)=(420​)(417​)​=48452380​=0.4912,P(at least one)=1−0.4912=0.5088

So this inspection plan misses a bad lot about half the time. For a manager that's the real insight: a 4-item sample is a weak filter. The same trick answers the audit question from Worked example 1: P(at least one Dehradun branch) = 1 − (83)/(123)\binom{8}{3}/\binom{12}{3}(38​)/(312​) = 1 − 56/220 = 0.7455.

Subjective probabilities are allowed, but test them

Consultants, investors and bid managers use subjective probabilities all the time (“60% chance the client signs”). They are legitimate, but they must still obey the two basic requirements, and they should be checked against outcomes. A well-calibrated manager's “70% sure” calls come true about 7 times in 10. Keep a simple log of your forecasts and you'll learn your own bias quickly.

Interview-style insight

“What's the probability our new café on Rajpur Road survives two years?” A strong answer starts with a reference class (what share of similar cafés in similar locations survive two years: a relative frequency), then adjusts for what is special about this one (a subjective step). Starting from your own gut, with no base rate, is the classic error.

Odds are not probabilities Beyond the syllabus

Betting sites and some risk teams quote odds: odds = p / (1 − p). A conversion probability of 0.2 is odds of 0.2/0.8 = 0.25, or “1 to 4”. Odds reappear in logistic regression in the Relationships pillar. In this course, always answer in probabilities unless asked.

Mutually exclusive is not the same as independent

Students mix these up every year. Mutually exclusive means the events cannot happen together. Independent means one happening tells you nothing about the other. In fact, two mutually exclusive events (each with positive probability) are always dependent: if you know the customer holds a Gold card, you know for certain they don't hold Platinum. The full story is on the conditional probability page.

Rounding trap

In class Problem 1, using rounded parts gives 0.78 + 0.45 − 0.34 = 0.89, but the exact answer is 660/746 = 0.8847, which rounds to 0.88. Keep fractions (or at least 4 decimals) until the last step, then round once.

Exam tip

For every part, write three things: the event in words, the event in symbols, then the fraction. “P(male or name-brand) = P(M∪N)P(M \cup N)P(M∪N) = (1,138 + 660 − 319)/2,276 = 1,479/2,276 = 0.650.” Examiners award marks for the set-up even if the arithmetic slips.

(c)

(d)

Common mistakes

WrongRight
P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B), always.Subtract the overlap: P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B). Plain adding works only for mutually exclusive events.
P(female and influenced) = 173/850.That divides by the female total, which answers a “given female” question. A joint probability divides by the grand total: 173/1,700.
“Either it happens or it doesn't, so it's 50–50.”The classical method needs equally likely outcomes. A loan defaulting and not defaulting are two outcomes, but nowhere near equally likely.
Adding multi-select survey shares: “60% use Instagram + 55% use YouTube = 115%”.Multi-select answers overlap, so they aren't mutually exclusive and can sum past 100%. You need the overlap to find the union.
Using permutations to count committees or samples.If order doesn't matter, use combinations. Ask: “Does swapping two people give a different result?”
Treating “mutually exclusive” and “independent” as the same idea.Mutually exclusive = can't co-occur. Independent = one tells you nothing about the other. Mutually exclusive events (with positive probabilities) are always dependent.
Rounding each piece first: 0.78 + 0.45 − 0.34 = 0.89.Work with fractions and round once at the end: 660/746 = 0.88.
Reporting a probability of 1.15 or −0.05 and moving on.Every probability sits between 0 and 1, and outcome probabilities add to 1. If not, stop and recheck.

Quick check

Every order on a shopping app is paid by exactly one method: UPI, card or cash on delivery. If P(UPI) = 0.55 and P(card) = 0.25, what is P(cash on delivery)?

  1. 0.80
  2. 0.20
  3. 0.1375
  4. It can't be found without P(UPI and card)
The three methods are mutually exclusive and cover every order, so their probabilities add to 1: 1 − 0.55 − 0.25 = 0.20. P(UPI and card) is 0 here because one order uses one method. 0.80 is P(UPI or card), and 0.1375 wrongly multiplies the two.

A brand will pick 4 of its 9 influencers for a Diwali campaign. Order doesn't matter. How many different teams of 4 are possible?

  1. 3,024
  2. 36
  3. 126
  4. 6,561
A team is a combination: (94)=9×8×7×64×3×2×1=126\binom{9}{4} = \frac{9 \times 8 \times 7 \times 6}{4 \times 3 \times 2 \times 1} = 126(49​)=4×3×2×19×8×7×6​=126, or =COMBIN(9,4). 3,024 is PERMUT(9,4), which counts ordered line-ups; 36 is (92)\binom{9}{2}(29​); 6,561 is 949^494.

Among a fintech app's users, P(uses UPI) = 0.70, P(uses a wallet) = 0.25 and P(uses both) = 0.15. What is P(uses UPI or a wallet)?

  1. 0.80
  2. 0.95
  3. 0.10
  4. 0.175
Addition law: 0.70 + 0.25 − 0.15 = 0.80. Answer b double-counts the 15% who use both; 0.175 is 0.70 × 0.25, which is not the rule for “or”.

In class Problem 3, what does 173/1,700 = 0.102 represent?

  1. The probability a respondent is influenced, given she is female
  2. The marginal probability that a respondent is influenced
  3. The probability a respondent is female or influenced
  4. The joint probability that a respondent is female and influenced
One inner cell divided by the grand total is a joint (“and”) probability. “Given female” would divide by 850 (0.204); the marginal for influenced is 262/1,700 = 0.154; “female or influenced” is 939/1,700 = 0.552.

A start-up founder tells investors: “I'm 70% confident our next funding round closes by March.” Which method of assigning probability is this?

  1. Classical
  2. Subjective
  3. Relative frequency
  4. Complement rule
It's an informed judgment about a one-off event with no equally likely outcomes and no long history of repeats, so it's subjective. The complement rule isn't a method of assigning probabilities; it's a relationship between them.

One-glance summary

Probability basics in 60 seconds

  • Vocabulary: experiment → outcomes → sample space S → events (sets of outcomes). P(event) = sum of its outcome probabilities.
  • Counting: multi-step n1×n2×⋯n_1 \times n_2 \times \cdotsn1​×n2​×⋯; combinations (Nn)=N!n!(N−n)!\binom{N}{n} = \frac{N!}{n!(N-n)!}(nN​)=n!(N−n)!N!​ (order irrelevant); permutations N!(N−n)!\frac{N!}{(N-n)!}(N−n)!N!​ (order matters).
  • Assigning: classical (equally likely), relative frequency (data), subjective (judgment). Always 0≤P≤10 \le P \le 10≤P≤1 and the outcome probabilities add to 1.
  • Complement: P(Ac)=1−P(A)P(A^c) = 1 - P(A)P(Ac)=1−P(A). Great for “at least one”.
  • Addition law: P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B)P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B); if mutually exclusive, just add.
  • Cross-tab: joint = inner cell ÷ grand total; marginal = margin total ÷ grand total.
  • Excel: COMBIN, PERMUT, FACT, COUNTIFS, PivotTable › Show Values As › % of Grand Total.
