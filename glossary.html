A ↑ Search and A–Z

Addition law
The rule for “A or B”: add the two probabilities, then subtract the overlap so it isn’t counted twice. If 30% of customers pay by UPI, 25% by card and 10% use both, then 45% use at least one. See: Probability basics
Adjusted R²
R² with a penalty for every extra predictor. Use it to compare models with different numbers of x variables, because unlike R² it can fall when you add a useless one. See: Regression
Aggregate price index
One index for a whole basket of items, built from the total cost of the basket now versus the base period. The simple version just adds prices; weighted versions (Laspeyres, Paasche) allow for quantities bought. See: Advanced techniques
Alpha (α)
The level of significance: the risk of a Type I error you agree to accept before running a test, usually 0.05. In exponential smoothing the same letter means the smoothing constant. See: Errors & p-value
Alternative hypothesis (H₁)
The statement you are looking for evidence for: the mean has changed, the new ad works better. It always uses ≠, < or >. See: Hypothesis tests
Analysis ToolPak
Excel’s free statistics add-in: descriptive statistics, histograms, regression, t-tests, ANOVA and more, under Data › Data Analysis. Switch it on once under File › Options › Add-ins. See: Central tendency · Formula sheet
ANOVA (analysis of variance)
A test of whether three or more group means are equal, done by comparing the variation between groups with the variation within them. Example: do average sales differ across four store layouts? See: ANOVA
Assignable (special) cause
A specific, fixable reason for unusual variation in a process, such as a worn machine part or a new, untrained operator. It often shows up as a point outside the control limits. See: Advanced techniques

B ↑ Search and A–Z

Bar chart
Bars whose lengths show the count or value for each category. The default chart for categorical data, such as complaints by type. See: Visualization
Base period
The period an index number is compared against. Its index is set to 100. See: Advanced techniques
Bayes’ theorem
A way to update a probability when new evidence arrives. It turns P(evidence | cause) into P(cause | evidence). Example: given that a part is defective, how likely is it that supplier B made it? See: Conditional probability
Best-subsets regression
A model-building approach that fits every combination of the candidate x variables and compares them on adjusted R² or a similar measure, so you can pick a small model that still predicts well. See: Regression
Beta (β)
The probability of a Type II error, that is, missing a real effect. Power is 1 − β. See: Errors & p-value
Bias
A systematic error that pushes results in one direction, unlike random error, which tends to average out. Surveying only app users about app satisfaction builds in bias. See: Sampling · Research foundations
Bimodal
A distribution with two peaks. It often means two different groups are mixed together, such as weekday and weekend shoppers. See: Shape
Binomial distribution
The probabilities of getting 0, 1, 2, … successes in n independent tries when each try has the same chance of success p. Example: how many of 10 sales calls end in a sale? See: Distributions
Box plot
A chart of the five-number summary: a box from Q1 to Q3 with a line at the median, whiskers to the smallest and largest non-outlier values, and dots for outliers. Great for comparing groups side by side. See: Visualization

C ↑ Search and A–Z

Categorical (qualitative) data
Data that are labels rather than amounts: city, payment mode, brand. You count them; you don’t average them. See: Research foundations
Census
Collecting data from every member of the population instead of a sample. Accurate in principle, but slow and expensive. See: Sampling
Central limit theorem
With a large enough sample (rule of thumb: n ≥ 30) the sample mean is roughly normally distributed, even when the data themselves are not. This is why z and t methods work so widely. See: Sampling
Centred moving average
A moving average lined up with the middle of its window. For quarterly data you average two neighbouring four-quarter averages. It is the first step in finding seasonal indexes. See: Time series
Centroid
The centre of a cluster: the mean of every variable across the cases in that cluster. k-means keeps recomputing centroids until cases stop switching clusters. See: Advanced techniques
Chebyshev’s theorem
For any data set, whatever its shape, at least 1 − 1/z² of the values lie within z standard deviations of the mean. So at least 75% lie within 2 SDs and at least 88.9% within 3 SDs. See: Dispersion
Chi-square distribution
A right-skewed distribution of non-negative values whose shape depends on its degrees of freedom. It supplies the p-values for chi-square tests. See: Chi-square
Chi-square goodness-of-fit test
Checks whether the observed counts in categories match a claimed pattern, for example whether store visits are spread evenly across the seven days of the week. See: Chi-square
Chi-square test of independence
Checks whether two categorical variables are related by comparing the observed counts in a cross-tab with the counts you would expect if they were unrelated. Example: is preferred payment mode linked to age group? See: Chi-square
Class (bin)
One of the intervals that a histogram or frequency table groups values into, such as ₹0–499 and ₹500–999. Class width is the size of each interval. See: Visualization
Cluster analysis
A family of methods that group cases so that members of a group are similar and the groups differ from each other. Marketers use it to find customer segments. See: Advanced techniques
Cluster sampling
Divide the population into groups (clusters) such as branches or towns, pick some clusters at random, then survey everyone in the chosen clusters. Cheaper when the population is spread out. See: Sampling
Coding (survey responses)
Turning answers into numbers or short labels that can be analysed, such as 1 = Yes, 0 = No, or grouping open-ended comments into themes. Keep a codebook so others can follow it. See: Advanced techniques
Coefficient of determination (r², R²)
The share of the variation in y that the regression explains. R² = 0.64 means the model accounts for 64% of the ups and downs in sales. See: Regression
Coefficient of variation (CV)
The standard deviation as a percentage of the mean. It lets you compare variability across things on different scales, such as the price swings of a ₹100 share and a ₹2,000 share. See: Dispersion
Combination
A selection where order does not matter, such as choosing 3 of 10 applicants for interview. Count them with C(N, n) or =COMBIN. See: Probability basics
Common cause variation
The normal background variation built into a stable process. Reacting to it as if it were a problem usually makes things worse. See: Advanced techniques
Communality
In factor analysis, the share of a variable’s variance that the retained factors explain. A low communality means the variable doesn’t fit the factors well. See: Advanced techniques
Complement
Everything that is not the event. P(not A) = 1 − P(A). Very handy for “at least one” questions. See: Probability basics
Conditional probability
The probability of A once you know B has happened, written P(A | B). Example: the chance a shopper buys, given that they added an item to the cart. See: Conditional probability
Confidence interval
A range of plausible values for a population parameter: estimate ± margin of error. With a sample mean of 12.5 minutes, s = 3.2 and n = 25, the 95% interval for the mean wait is 11.18 to 13.82 minutes. See: Estimation
Confidence level
How often the interval method would capture the true value if you repeated the sampling many times, such as 95%. Higher confidence gives a wider interval. See: Estimation
Confounding variable
A third variable that drives both of the variables you are studying, creating a link that isn’t causal. Hot weather drives both ice-cream sales and air-conditioner sales. See: Correlation
Consumer Price Index (CPI)
An index of the prices of a fixed basket of goods and services that households buy. It is the usual measure of inflation and the usual deflator for wages and sales. See: Advanced techniques
Contingency table (cross-tab)
A table of counts for two categorical variables at once, such as age group by preferred brand. It is the starting point for joint probabilities and the chi-square test. See: Chi-square · Conditional probability
Continuity correction
Adding or subtracting 0.5 when a normal curve approximates a count (binomial) distribution, so that a whole number like 12 becomes the interval 11.5 to 12.5. See: Distributions
Continuous random variable
A variable that can take any value in a range, such as delivery time or weight. Probabilities are areas under a curve, so the chance of one exact value is 0. See: Random variables
Control chart
A time plot of a process measure with a centre line and upper and lower control limits (usually 3 standard errors away). Points outside the limits signal special causes. See: Advanced techniques
Control limits (UCL, LCL)
The upper and lower lines on a control chart, set from the process’s own variation. Not the same as specification limits, which come from the customer. See: Advanced techniques
Convenience sampling
Picking whoever is easiest to reach, such as friends or mall visitors. Quick and cheap, but the results may not represent the population. See: Sampling
Correlation coefficient (Pearson r)
A number from −1 to +1 that measures how closely two numeric variables follow a straight line. r = 0.8 is strong and positive; r = −0.2 is weak and negative. See: Correlation
Covariance
A measure of whether two variables move together (+) or in opposite directions (−). Its size depends on the units, which is why we usually standardise it into r. See: Correlation
Critical value
The cut-off for a test statistic that separates “reject H₀” from “do not reject H₀”. For a two-tailed z test at α = 0.05 it is ±1.96. See: Hypothesis tests
Cronbach’s alpha
A measure of how consistently the items of a scale measure the same thing. A value of 0.7 or more is usually acceptable for a survey scale. See: Advanced techniques
Cross-sectional data
Data collected at one point in time across many units, such as this month’s sales for 50 branches. Compare with time series data. See: Research foundations
Cumulative frequency
The running total of frequencies up to and including each class. Plotted, it gives an ogive. See: Visualization
Cyclical component
Long, wave-like swings in a time series that last more than a year, often tied to the business cycle. See: Time series

D ↑ Search and A–Z

Dashboard
One screen that pulls together the key charts and numbers for a decision, often built in Excel from PivotTables and PivotCharts. Good dashboards answer a few questions clearly rather than showing everything. See: Advanced techniques · Visualization
Data privacy (GDPR)
Rules on collecting and using personal data: collect only what you need, get consent, keep it secure. The EU’s GDPR is the best-known law; India’s Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 follows similar ideas. See: Research foundations
Data validation
An Excel feature (Data › Data Validation) that limits what can be typed into a cell, such as a drop-down list of cities or a number from 1 to 5. It stops errors at the point of entry. See: Research foundations
Deflating
Dividing a money series by a price index to strip out inflation, so you compare real rather than nominal values. Real salary = nominal salary ÷ (CPI/100). See: Advanced techniques
Degrees of freedom (df)
The number of values that are free to vary once your estimates are fixed. It picks the right row of the t, χ² or F table. For a one-sample t test, df = n − 1. See: Estimation
Dendrogram
The tree diagram from hierarchical clustering. It shows which cases merge and at what distance; cutting it at a chosen height gives the clusters. See: Advanced techniques
Dependent variable
The variable you want to explain or predict (y), such as sales. Also called the response or outcome variable. See: Regression
Descriptive research
Research that describes who, what, where, when and how much, such as a customer profile survey. It describes; it doesn’t test cause and effect. See: Research foundations
Descriptive statistics
Numbers and charts that summarise the data you have: averages, spreads, tables and graphs. They describe the data without generalising beyond it. See: Central tendency
Discrete random variable
A variable that takes countable values, such as the number of customers arriving per minute or defects per batch. See: Random variables
Dispersion
How spread out the data are. The range, IQR, variance and standard deviation all measure it. See: Dispersion
DPMO (defects per million opportunities)
Defects found ÷ (units × opportunities for a defect in each unit) × 1,000,000. Six Sigma quality means about 3.4 DPMO. See: Advanced techniques
Dummy variable
A 0/1 variable that brings a category into a regression, such as 1 = online order, 0 = store order. A category with k levels needs k − 1 dummies. See: Regression
Duplicate records
The same case entered more than once, which inflates counts and totals. In Excel, Data › Remove Duplicates finds and deletes them (keep a copy of the raw data first). See: Research foundations

E ↑ Search and A–Z

Eigenvalue
In PCA and factor analysis, the amount of variance a component explains. With standardised data, components with an eigenvalue above 1 are usually kept (the Kaiser rule). See: Advanced techniques
Empirical rule
For bell-shaped data, about 68% of values lie within 1 standard deviation of the mean, about 95% within 2 and about 99.7% within 3. See: Dispersion
Ethics in research
Treating participants and data fairly: informed consent, privacy, no harm, and honest analysis and reporting. Cherry-picking results or hiding a biased sample is an ethical problem, not just a technical one. See: Research foundations
Euclidean distance
The straight-line distance between two cases measured across several variables: the square root of the sum of squared differences. Standardise the variables first so one in rupees doesn’t swamp one on a 1–5 scale. See: Advanced techniques
Event
One or more outcomes of an experiment that you are interested in, such as “the customer pays by UPI”. See: Probability basics
Expected frequency
The count you would expect in a category or cell if H₀ were true. In a cross-tab: row total × column total ÷ grand total. See: Chi-square
Expected value
The long-run average of a random variable: each value times its probability, added up. If a policy has a 2% chance of a ₹50,000 claim and no other claims, the expected claim is ₹1,000. See: Random variables
Experiment (probability)
Any process with uncertain outcomes, such as a customer visit, a quality check or the toss of a coin. See: Probability basics
Experimental (causal) research
Research that changes one thing on purpose (the price, the ad) and measures the effect while holding other factors steady. The design that can show cause and effect. See: Research foundations
Exploratory research
Early, open-ended research to understand a problem and generate ideas, using interviews, focus groups or secondary data. See: Research foundations
Exponential smoothing
A forecasting method in which the next forecast is a weighted average of the latest actual value and the latest forecast, so older data fade out gradually. See: Time series

F ↑ Search and A–Z

F statistic (F distribution)
A ratio of two variance estimates. A big F says the “signal” (differences between groups, or variation explained by a model) is large compared with the “noise”. Used in ANOVA and regression. See: ANOVA · Regression
Factor analysis
A method that finds a few hidden factors behind many correlated survey items. Ten questions about staff, speed and ambience might boil down to two factors: service and atmosphere. See: Advanced techniques
Factor loading
The correlation between a variable and a factor. The items that load highly on a factor are what you use to name it. See: Advanced techniques
Finite population correction
A factor, √((N − n)/(N − 1)), that shrinks the standard error when the sample is more than 5% of the population. See: Sampling
Fisher’s ideal index
The geometric mean of the Laspeyres and Paasche price indexes, √(L × P). It always lies between the two. See: Advanced techniques
Five-number summary
Minimum, Q1, median, Q3 and maximum: the five numbers a box plot draws. See: Visualization
Forecast error
Actual value minus forecast. A positive error means the forecast was too low. See: Time series
Frequency distribution
A table showing how many observations fall in each category or class. The first step before a bar chart or histogram. See: Visualization

G ↑ Search and A–Z

Geometric mean
The nth root of the product of n values. The right average for growth rates: returns of +10% and then −10% average about −0.5% a year, not 0%. See: Central tendency
Grand mean
The mean of all observations pooled together across groups (often written as x with two bars). ANOVA measures how far each group mean sits from it. See: ANOVA

H ↑ Search and A–Z

Heteroscedasticity
When the spread of the residuals changes with x, often fanning out like a funnel. It breaks a regression assumption and makes the standard errors unreliable. See: Regression
Hierarchical clustering
Clustering that starts with every case on its own and keeps merging the two closest groups until one group is left, producing a dendrogram. See: Advanced techniques
Histogram
Touching bars that show how many values fall in each class of a numeric variable. It reveals the shape: symmetric or skewed, one peak or two. See: Visualization
Holdout sample
Data kept aside while you build a model and then used to test it. A model that predicts the holdout well is less likely to be overfitted. See: Regression
Hypothesis test
A formal way to choose between two competing statements about a population using sample data, by asking how surprising the sample would be if H₀ were true. See: Hypothesis tests

I ↑ Search and A–Z

Independent events
Events where knowing that one happened doesn’t change the chance of the other: P(A | B) = P(A), or equivalently P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). See: Conditional probability
Independent samples
Two samples with no link between their members, such as customers of two different branches. Compare with paired samples. See: Hypothesis tests
Independent variable
A variable used to explain or predict y in a regression (an x), such as ad spend. Also called a predictor or explanatory variable. See: Regression
Index number
A number that shows the change in a price, quantity or value compared with a base period set at 100. An index of 118 means 18% higher than the base. See: Advanced techniques
Inferential statistics
Using a sample to draw conclusions about a population, with the uncertainty stated: estimates, confidence intervals and hypothesis tests. See: Sampling · Estimation
Informed consent
Agreement to take part in research given freely by someone who knows what the study involves, what data are collected and how they will be used. See: Research foundations
Interaction term
The product of two predictors (x₁ × x₂) in a regression. It lets the effect of one depend on the level of the other, such as a discount that works better in festive months. See: Regression
Interquartile range (IQR)
Q3 minus Q1: the spread of the middle 50% of the data. Outliers don’t affect it. See: Dispersion
Interval scale
Numeric data with equal steps but no true zero, such as temperature in °C or calendar years. Differences make sense; ratios don’t. See: Research foundations
Irregular component
The random, unpredictable part of a time series that is left after the trend, seasonal and cyclical effects are taken out. See: Time series

J ↑ Search and A–Z

Joint probability
The probability that two events happen together, P(A and B). In a cross-tab it is a cell count divided by the grand total. See: Conditional probability

K ↑ Search and A–Z

k-means clustering
Clustering that splits cases into k groups by repeatedly assigning each case to the nearest centroid and then recomputing the centroids. See: Advanced techniques
Kaiser rule
Keep only the components or factors with an eigenvalue greater than 1, because each of them explains more than one original variable’s worth of variance. See: Advanced techniques
Kurtosis
A measure of how heavy a distribution’s tails are. Excel’s KURT reports excess kurtosis: about 0 for a normal curve, positive when extreme values are more common. See: Shape

L ↑ Search and A–Z

Laspeyres index
A weighted price index that uses base-period quantities as weights. It answers: what would the base-period basket cost at today’s prices? See: Advanced techniques
Least squares method
The rule for fitting a regression line: choose the line that makes the sum of squared residuals as small as possible. See: Regression
Level of significance
α, the probability of rejecting H₀ when it is actually true, chosen before the test. Common choices are 0.05, 0.01 and 0.10. See: Errors & p-value
Likert scale
A rating scale for agreement, such as 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree. Technically ordinal, though it is often analysed as if it were numeric. See: Research foundations · Advanced techniques
Line chart
Points joined by lines, used to show how a value changes over time. See: Visualization
Logistic regression
Regression for a yes/no outcome, such as buys or doesn’t, defaults or doesn’t. It predicts a probability between 0 and 1. Beyond the syllabus. See: Regression

M ↑ Search and A–Z

Mann–Whitney U test
A non-parametric test that compares two independent groups using ranks instead of the raw values. Useful when the data are ordinal or badly skewed. See: Hypothesis tests
Margin of error
The ± part of a confidence interval: how far the estimate could reasonably be from the truth. A larger sample shrinks it. See: Estimation
Marginal probability
The probability of a single event on its own, read from the margins (row or column totals) of a contingency table. See: Conditional probability
Mean
The sum of the values divided by how many there are. It is the balance point of the data and gets pulled towards outliers. See: Central tendency
Mean absolute deviation (MAD)
In forecasting, the average size of the forecast errors, ignoring their sign. Anderson calls it MAE, the mean absolute error. See: Time series
Mean absolute percentage error (MAPE)
The average forecast error as a percentage of the actual values. A MAPE of 8% means the forecasts miss by 8% on average. See: Time series
Mean square
A sum of squares divided by its degrees of freedom, which makes it a variance estimate. ANOVA compares MSB with MSW; regression compares MSR with MSE. See: ANOVA · Regression
Mean squared error (MSE)
The average of the squared errors. In forecasting it punishes big misses heavily; in regression it is SSE ÷ (n − p − 1). See: Time series · Regression
Median
The middle value once the data are sorted. Half the values lie below it and half above, so outliers barely move it. See: Central tendency
Missing values
Blank or unknown entries in a data set. Find them (COUNTBLANK), decide how to handle them (drop, fill or flag), and record what you did. See: Research foundations
Mode
The value that appears most often. The only average that works for categories, such as the best-selling pack size. See: Central tendency
Moving average
The average of the most recent k periods, used to smooth a time series or to forecast the next period. See: Time series
Multicollinearity
When the x variables in a regression are strongly correlated with each other. The coefficients become unstable and hard to interpret; check with the VIF. See: Regression
Multiple regression
Regression with two or more x variables, such as predicting sales from price, ad spend and store size together. See: Regression
Multiplication law
The rule for “A and B”: P(A and B) = P(B) × P(A | B). If the events are independent it is simply P(A) × P(B). See: Conditional probability
Mutually exclusive events
Events that cannot happen at the same time, such as “paid in cash” and “paid by card” for a single payment. See: Probability basics

N ↑ Search and A–Z

Nominal scale
Labels with no order, such as city, gender or product category. Only counts, percentages and the mode make sense. See: Research foundations
Non-parametric test
A test that doesn’t assume a particular distribution such as the normal, and often works with ranks. Examples: Mann–Whitney U, Wilcoxon signed-rank, Spearman’s rank correlation. See: Hypothesis tests
Non-probability sampling
Any sampling method where chance doesn’t decide who is chosen: convenience, judgement, quota or snowball sampling. Quicker, but you can’t measure sampling error properly. See: Sampling
Non-response bias
Bias that appears when the people who don’t respond differ from those who do, such as busy senior managers ignoring a survey. See: Sampling
Normal distribution
The symmetric, bell-shaped curve fixed by its mean μ and standard deviation σ. Many measurements, and most sampling distributions, follow it closely. See: Distributions
Normal probability plot
A plot of the sorted residuals (or data) against the values a normal distribution would give. Points close to a straight line suggest the data are roughly normal. See: Regression
Null hypothesis (H₀)
The default “nothing has changed” statement that a test looks for evidence against. It always contains the equals sign (=, ≤ or ≥). See: Hypothesis tests

O ↑ Search and A–Z

Observed frequency
The actual count recorded in a category or a table cell. See: Chi-square
Odds and odds ratio
Odds are p ÷ (1 − p): a 0.8 chance of buying means odds of 4 to 1. An odds ratio compares the odds for two groups; logistic regression reports them. See: Regression
Ogive
A line graph of cumulative frequencies or cumulative percentages. Use it to read how many values fall below any level. See: Visualization
One-tailed test
A test where H₁ points in one direction only (μ < μ₀ or μ > μ₀), so the whole rejection region sits in one tail. See: Hypothesis tests
Ordinal scale
Categories with a meaningful order but unequal or unknown gaps, such as satisfaction ratings or education level. See: Research foundations
Outlier
A value far from the rest of the data. Flag it if |z| > 3, or if it lies below Q1 − 1.5 × IQR or above Q3 + 1.5 × IQR, then investigate before deleting it. See: Shape · Visualization

P ↑ Search and A–Z

p chart
A control chart for the proportion of defective items in each sample. See: Advanced techniques
p-value
The probability of getting a sample result at least as extreme as yours if H₀ were true. A small p-value is strong evidence against H₀: reject H₀ when p ≤ α. See: Errors & p-value
Paasche index
A weighted price index that uses current-period quantities as weights. It needs fresh quantity data every period. See: Advanced techniques
Paired samples
Two measurements on the same units, such as each salesperson’s sales before and after training. You analyse the differences. See: Hypothesis tests
Parameter
A number that describes a whole population, such as μ, σ or p. Usually unknown, so we estimate it with a statistic. See: Sampling
Pareto chart
A bar chart sorted from largest to smallest with a cumulative-percentage line. It shows the “vital few” causes behind most of the problems. See: Visualization
Partial F test
A test of whether a group of x variables adds anything to a regression, by comparing the error sum of squares with and without them. See: Regression
Percentile
The value below which a given percentage of the data falls. Scoring at the 90th percentile means you did better than about 90% of test-takers. See: Dispersion · Formula sheet
Permutation
An arrangement where order matters, such as ranking 3 of 10 products as 1st, 2nd and 3rd. Count them with =PERMUT. See: Probability basics
Pie chart
A circle cut into slices showing each category’s share of one whole. Best with five slices or fewer. See: Visualization
PivotTable
Excel’s drag-and-drop summary tool. It counts, sums or averages data by category and builds cross-tabs in seconds (Insert › PivotTable). See: Visualization
Point estimate
A single number used as the best guess for a parameter, such as the sample proportion for the population proportion. See: Estimation
Poisson distribution
The probabilities of 0, 1, 2, … events in a fixed interval when events happen independently at a steady average rate, such as calls per hour at a help desk. See: Distributions
Pooled variance
A combined estimate of a variance that two groups are assumed to share, used in the equal-variances two-sample t test. See: Hypothesis tests
Population
The entire group you want to know about: all customers, all invoices this year, all households in Dehradun. See: Sampling
Posterior probability
A probability revised after new information arrives; the output of Bayes’ theorem. See: Conditional probability
Power of a test
The probability that a test correctly rejects a false H₀, which is 1 − β. Bigger samples give more power. See: Errors & p-value
Prediction interval
A range for one new y value at a given x. It is always wider than the confidence interval for the mean of y at that x. See: Regression
Price relative
The current price as a percentage of the base-period price: (current price ÷ base price) × 100. See: Advanced techniques
Primary data
Data you collect yourself for the study in hand, through a survey, interview or experiment. See: Research foundations
Principal component analysis (PCA)
A method that turns many correlated variables into a few new, uncorrelated ones (components) that keep most of the information. See: Advanced techniques
Prior probability
Your probability for an event before new evidence arrives; the starting point for Bayes’ theorem. See: Conditional probability
Probability
A number from 0 to 1 that measures how likely an event is: 0 is impossible, 1 is certain. It can come from equally likely outcomes, past relative frequencies or informed judgement. See: Probability basics
Probability density function
The curve of a continuous random variable. Its height is not a probability; the area under it between two values is. See: Random variables · Distributions
Probability distribution
A table, graph or formula giving the probability of every possible value of a random variable. The probabilities add up to 1. See: Random variables
Probability sampling
Sampling in which every member has a known, non-zero chance of selection: simple random, systematic, stratified and cluster sampling. It lets you measure sampling error. See: Sampling
Process capability (Cp, Cpk)
How well a stable process fits inside the customer’s specification limits. Cp compares the spreads; Cpk also allows for whether the process is centred. 1.33 or more is a common target. See: Advanced techniques
Proportion
The fraction of a sample or population with some feature, such as the share of customers who buy again. Written p̄ for a sample and p for a population. See: Estimation

Q ↑ Search and A–Z

Quantitative data
Numbers that measure an amount, such as sales, age or time, so averaging makes sense. They are discrete (counts) or continuous (measurements). See: Research foundations
Quartiles
The three values that cut sorted data into four equal parts: Q1 (25th percentile), Q2 (the median) and Q3 (75th percentile). See: Dispersion
Questionnaire
The set of questions used in a survey. Good ones are short, clear and neutral, and are tried out on a few people first. See: Advanced techniques · Research foundations
Quota sampling
Non-random sampling that fills set quotas, such as 50 men and 50 women, choosing anyone convenient within each quota. See: Sampling

R ↑ Search and A–Z

R chart
A control chart for the range of each sample. It tracks process variation and should be checked before the x̄ chart. See: Advanced techniques
Random variable
A numeric outcome of a chance process, such as the number of orders in an hour or the time a delivery takes. See: Random variables
Range
The largest value minus the smallest. Quick, but it depends entirely on the two most extreme values. See: Dispersion
Ratio scale
Numeric data with a true zero, so ratios make sense: sales, weight, income, time. ₹20 lakh really is twice ₹10 lakh. See: Research foundations
Regression equation
The fitted line ŷ = b₀ + b₁x that predicts y from x. The slope b₁ is the change in predicted y for each one-unit increase in x. See: Regression
Rejection region
The set of test-statistic values that lead you to reject H₀. It begins at the critical value. See: Hypothesis tests
Relative frequency
A count expressed as a fraction or percentage of the total. Past relative frequencies are a common way to assign probabilities. See: Visualization · Probability basics
Reliability
Consistency: a reliable measure gives similar results when it is repeated. Cronbach’s alpha measures it for multi-item scales. See: Research foundations · Advanced techniques
Research design
The overall plan for answering a research question: exploratory, descriptive or causal, plus what data you need, from whom, how and when. See: Research foundations
Research process
The steps of a study: define the problem, plan the design, collect the data, analyse it and report the findings. See: Research foundations
Residual
Actual y minus predicted y. It is the part of each observation the regression line doesn’t explain. See: Regression
Residual plot
A scatter of residuals against x or against predicted y. A random band is good; a curve suggests the wrong model form, and a funnel suggests heteroscedasticity. See: Regression

S ↑ Search and A–Z

Sample
The part of the population you actually observe and measure. See: Sampling
Sample size
The number of observations, n. Bigger samples give narrower intervals and more powerful tests; the size you need follows from the margin of error you can accept. See: Estimation
Sample space
The list of all possible outcomes of an experiment. See: Probability basics
Sampling distribution
The distribution of a statistic, such as the sample mean, across all possible samples of the same size. Its standard deviation is the standard error. See: Sampling
Sampling error
The natural gap between a sample statistic and the population parameter that arises just because you looked at a sample. Larger samples reduce it. See: Sampling
Sampling frame
The actual list you draw a sample from, such as a customer database. If the frame misses people, so will your sample. See: Sampling
Scatter diagram
A plot of paired numeric values with one variable on each axis. It shows the direction, form and strength of a relationship, and any outliers. See: Visualization · Correlation
Scree plot
A line chart of eigenvalues from largest to smallest. Keep the components before the point where the line flattens out (the “elbow”). See: Advanced techniques
Seasonal index
A number showing how a season compares with the trend. An index of 1.15 means sales in that quarter run 15% above trend. See: Time series
Secondary data
Data someone else collected for another purpose: government statistics, industry reports, company records. Cheap and fast, but check that they fit your question and are accurate. See: Research foundations
Semantic differential scale
A rating scale between two opposite words, such as “expensive … affordable” or “slow … fast”, often with seven points. See: Research foundations · Advanced techniques
Simple random sampling
Every possible sample of size n has the same chance of being chosen, like drawing names from a hat or using RAND() in Excel. See: Sampling
Six Sigma
A quality approach that aims for no more than about 3.4 defects per million opportunities, using the DMAIC cycle: Define, Measure, Analyse, Improve, Control. See: Advanced techniques
Skewness
A measure of lopsidedness. Positive (right) skew has a long tail of high values, like incomes; negative (left) skew has a long tail of low values. See: Shape
Smoothing constant (α)
The weight exponential smoothing gives to the newest actual value, between 0 and 1. A higher α reacts faster but jumps around more. See: Time series
Spearman rank correlation
Pearson’s r applied to ranks. It measures any steady upward or downward relationship, and it works for ordinal data and data with outliers. See: Correlation
Specification limits (USL, LSL)
The limits a product must meet, set by the customer or the design. Compare with control limits, which come from the process itself. See: Advanced techniques
Standard deviation
The typical distance of the values from the mean, in the original units. It is the square root of the variance. See: Dispersion
Standard error
The standard deviation of a statistic from sample to sample, such as σ/√n for the sample mean. It tells you how much the estimate would wobble if you sampled again. See: Sampling
Standard error of the estimate
The typical size of a regression’s prediction errors, s = √MSE, in the units of y. Smaller is better. See: Regression
Standard normal distribution
The normal distribution with mean 0 and standard deviation 1. Any normal value converts to it with z = (x − μ)/σ, which is how the z-table is used. See: Distributions
Standardising
Converting values to z-scores by subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation, so that variables on different scales can be compared or combined. See: Dispersion · Advanced techniques
Statistic
A number calculated from a sample, such as the sample mean, s or p̄. It estimates a parameter. See: Sampling
Statistical quality control (SQC)
Using statistics, mainly control charts and capability indexes, to keep a process stable and within specification. See: Advanced techniques
Statistical significance
A result is statistically significant when its p-value is at or below α, so it is unlikely to be just sampling noise. Significant doesn’t automatically mean large or important. See: Errors & p-value
Stepwise regression
An automatic way of choosing predictors by adding or dropping one variable at a time based on its significance. Convenient, but check that the final model makes business sense. See: Regression
Stratified sampling
Split the population into groups that share a feature (strata), such as region or income band, then sample at random within each. Every group is guaranteed a place in the sample. See: Sampling
Sum of squares (SST, SSR, SSE)
Totals of squared deviations. SST is all the variation in y, SSR is the part the model explains, and SSE is the part left over: SST = SSR + SSE. See: Regression
Survey research
Collecting data by asking a sample of people structured questions, then coding, cross-tabulating and reporting the answers. See: Advanced techniques
Systematic sampling
Pick a random starting point, then every k-th item on the list, such as every 20th invoice. See: Sampling

T ↑ Search and A–Z

t distribution
A bell-shaped distribution with fatter tails than the normal, used when σ is unknown and estimated by s. It gets closer to the normal as the degrees of freedom grow. See: Estimation
t test
A test for means that uses the t distribution because σ is estimated by s. It comes in one-sample, two independent samples and paired versions. See: Hypothesis tests
Test statistic
The number calculated from the sample (z, t, F or χ²) that measures how far the data sit from what H₀ predicts. See: Hypothesis tests
Time series
Data recorded at regular time intervals, such as monthly sales or daily footfall. See: Time series
Tree diagram
A branching picture of a multi-step experiment with probabilities on the branches. Multiply along a path to get a joint probability. See: Conditional probability
Trend
The long-run upward or downward movement in a time series. A linear trend is fitted as T = b₀ + b₁t. See: Time series
Trimmed mean
The mean after dropping a small percentage of the highest and lowest values. Less affected by outliers than the ordinary mean. See: Central tendency
Two-tailed test
A test where H₁ says “not equal” (≠), so the rejection region is split between both tails. See: Hypothesis tests
Type I error
Rejecting H₀ when it is actually true: a false alarm. Its probability is α. See: Errors & p-value
Type II error
Failing to reject H₀ when it is actually false: a missed signal. Its probability is β. See: Errors & p-value

U ↑ Search and A–Z

Unbiased estimator
An estimator whose average over all possible samples equals the parameter. The sample mean is unbiased for μ, and dividing by n − 1 makes s² unbiased for σ². See: Estimation

V ↑ Search and A–Z

Validity
Whether a measure actually measures what it claims to. A “loyalty” survey that only asks about price may not be valid. See: Research foundations
Variable
A characteristic that differs from one unit to the next, such as age, income or the brand bought. See: Research foundations
Variance
The average squared distance of the values from the mean. Its square root is the standard deviation. See: Dispersion
Variance inflation factor (VIF)
A check for multicollinearity: 1 ÷ (1 − R²) from regressing one x on the others. Above 5 deserves a look; above 10 signals a serious problem. See: Regression
Venn diagram
Overlapping circles that show events, their overlap (both) and their union (either). See: Probability basics

W ↑ Search and A–Z

Weighted mean
An average in which some values count more than others. The average price per kg across orders of different sizes is a weighted mean, with the quantities as weights. See: Central tendency
Weighted moving average
A moving average that gives more weight to recent periods, with the weights adding up to 1. See: Time series
Wilcoxon signed-rank test
A non-parametric test for paired data. It ranks the sizes of the differences and compares the totals of the positive and negative ranks. See: Hypothesis tests

X ↑ Search and A–Z

x̄ chart
A control chart of sample means over time. It tracks where the process is centred. See: Advanced techniques

Z ↑ Search and A–Z

z-score
How many standard deviations a value sits above or below the mean: z = (x − mean) ÷ SD. A z of 2 means two standard deviations above average. See: Dispersion · Shape
z test
A test for a mean when σ is known, or for a proportion, that uses the standard normal distribution. See: Hypothesis tests
