How to write each kind of answer

2/3 marks · about 5 lines

Definition in one sentence, then one business example. For a quick calculation: formula, substitution, answer with units. No essays here; the marks are for precision.

5 marks · about one page

Open with a one-line definition (1 mark), give 3–4 explained points with an example each (3 marks), close with what it means for a manager (1 mark). Numericals: show every step and end with a sentence of interpretation.

10 marks · 2–3 pages

Use headings. Add a small table or sketch. For numericals, lay out Given → Formula → Substitute → Result → Interpret. For cases, end with a clear recommendation and the risk attached to it.

The six-step layout for any hypothesis test

Examiners mark the steps, not just the final answer. Write all six, every time, even when the question looks easy.

HypothesesState H0H_0H0​ and H1H_1H1​ in symbols and words. The claim with “=”, “≤” or “≥” sits in H0H_0H0​.
Significance levelWrite α\alphaα (usually 0.05) and whether the test is one- or two-tailed.
Test and formulaName the test (z, t, χ²), why it fits, and the formula of the statistic.
ComputeSubstitute the numbers; show the standard error separately.

A bare “t = 2.95” earns little. Finish every numerical with a line like: “At the 5% level there is enough evidence that the average wait is longer than 4 minutes, so the chain's claim does not hold.” That one sentence is often worth a full mark.

Module 1 · Foundations of Business Research & Statistics

Scope: the research process and types of research, data types and measurement scales, sampling techniques, ethics (GDPR, DPDP Act, bias), and preparing data in Excel. All theory.

Q1.1 Define business research. Give one example of a managerial decision it can support.

Answer pointers
  • Definition (1 mark): a systematic and objective process of collecting, analysing and interpreting information so that a manager can take a specific decision. The key words are systematic, objective and decision-oriented.
  • Example (1 mark): a bank surveys 400 college students before deciding whether to launch a zero-balance student account, and at what fee.

Revise: Research foundations

Q1.2 Distinguish between primary and secondary data with one business example of each.

Answer pointers
  • Primary data (1 mark): collected first-hand by the researcher for the problem at hand. Example: a café chain's own exit survey of 300 customers about a new menu.
  • Secondary data (1 mark): collected earlier by someone else for another purpose. Example: RBI data on UPI transaction volumes, Census tables, a competitor's annual report.
  • Bonus line: primary data fits the question exactly but costs time and money; secondary data is quick and cheap but may be outdated or defined differently.

Q1.3 State the scale of measurement (nominal, ordinal, interval or ratio) of each variable: (a) PIN code of a customer's delivery address; (b) a hotel's star rating, 1★ to 5★; (c) temperature inside a cold-storage warehouse in °C; (d) monthly sales of a showroom in ₹ lakh; (e) an employee's ID number; (f) years of work experience.

Answer pointers

½ mark each.

  • (a) Nominal: the digits are labels for an area; adding two PIN codes means nothing.
  • (b) Ordinal: 5★ is better than 4★, but the gap between ranks is not fixed.
  • (c) Interval: equal differences, but 0 °C is not “no temperature”, so 20 °C is not twice as warm as 10 °C.
  • (d) Ratio: true zero; ₹20 lakh really is double ₹10 lakh.
  • (e) Nominal: a number used as a name.
  • (f) Ratio: zero years means no experience; ratios make sense.

The trap examiners like: “it is a number, so it must be quantitative”. PIN codes and IDs are numbers but nominal.

Q1.4 A Dehradun-based bank wants to survey its credit-card holders about a new rewards programme. Identify the target population, a suitable sampling frame and the sampling unit. Point out one way the frame could fail to match the population.

Answer pointers
  • Target population (1): all active credit-card holders of the bank (in the region covered by the programme).
  • Sampling frame (1): the bank's card-holder database as on a given date, the list from which names are actually drawn.
  • Sampling unit (½): one individual card-holder (or one primary card account).
  • Frame error (½): the list may still contain closed or dormant cards, miss add-on card users, or leave out customers who joined after the list was pulled. Any one, explained.

Q1.5 Name any four data-protection principles (GDPR / India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023) that a researcher collecting customer data must follow.

Answer pointers

½ mark each for any four, with a one-line meaning:

  • Lawfulness and consent: have a legal basis, usually informed consent, before collecting.
  • Purpose limitation: use the data only for the purpose you stated.
  • Data minimisation: collect only the fields the analysis needs.
  • Accuracy: keep records correct; fix them when asked.
  • Storage limitation: delete raw personal data when the retention period ends.
  • Integrity and security: protect files; anonymise or pseudonymise before sharing.

Q1.6 Explain the steps of the business research process. Illustrate each step with a running example of a supermarket chain whose average bill value has been falling.

Answer pointers

Steps and explanation (4 marks), a consistent example (1 mark).

  1. Define the problem: turn the symptom into a decision question. “Why has the average bill fallen from ₹1,450 to ₹1,280, and should we change promotions?”
  2. Design the study: choose exploratory, descriptive or causal design; decide variables (bill value, items, visit frequency, store, customer type) and the instrument (POS data plus a short survey).
  3. Sample and collect: define population and frame (loyalty-card customers), pick a technique (stratified by store), collect.
  4. Prepare the data: clean, code and validate in Excel; remove duplicates, handle missing values.
  5. Analyse: describe first (means, box plots by store), then test and model (does bill value differ by store format?).
  6. Report and act: a short report and dashboard for the regional manager, with a clear recommendation.

Strong answers add: most failures happen at step 1 (wrong question) or step 3 (biased sample), and no amount of analysis fixes those.

Revise: Research foundations

Q1.7 Distinguish between probability and non-probability sampling. Explain any three non-probability methods with a business example each, and say when a manager can reasonably accept them.

Answer pointers
  • Difference (2 marks): in probability sampling every unit has a known, non-zero chance of selection, so we can compute a margin of error and generalise. In non-probability sampling selection depends on the researcher's choice or convenience, so results cannot be generalised with a stated confidence.
  • Three methods (1 mark each):
    • Convenience: interview shoppers at the mall entrance on a Saturday.
    • Judgement (purposive): a fintech interviews 10 CFOs it believes are early adopters.
    • Quota: fill fixed numbers by age and gender (say 50 men and 50 women) but choose people freely within each quota.
    • Snowball: each respondent refers others; useful for rare groups like boutique-hotel owners.
  • When acceptable: exploratory research, pre-testing a questionnaire, hard-to-reach populations, or when time and budget are tight and the decision is low-stakes.

Revise: Sampling

Q1.8 Explain exploratory, descriptive and causal research with a business example of each. Why can an observational study rarely prove cause and effect?

Answer pointers
  • Exploratory (1.5): used when the problem is vague; flexible methods such as interviews, focus groups, secondary data. Example: an ed-tech start-up holds focus groups to learn why trial users do not convert.
  • Descriptive (1.5): describes who, what, how much, how often; structured surveys and existing records. Example: a telecom firm estimates average monthly data use by age group.
  • Causal (1.5): tests whether X changes Y using a controlled experiment. Example: an e-commerce site randomly shows half its visitors free delivery above ₹499 and compares conversion rates (an A/B test).
  • Observational vs experimental (½): in observational data the researcher does not control who gets the “treatment”, so other factors (season, income, location) may drive both X and Y. Only random assignment rules those out.

Q1.11 A (hypothetical) hotel chain with six properties in Uttarakhand has seen its repeat-booking rate fall from 34% to 26% in one year. The general manager asks you for a research plan. Cover: (a) the management decision problem and research objectives; (b) the research design with a reason; (c) data sources; (d) the sampling plan: population, frame, technique and an approximate size; (e) three key variables and their measurement scales; (f) ethical safeguards; (g) how you would analyse the data in Excel.

Answer pointers
  • (a) Problem and objectives (2): decision: what to change (price, service, loyalty offers) to win back repeat guests. Objectives: measure satisfaction by property and guest type; identify the drivers of intention to revisit; compare lapsed vs loyal guests.
  • (b) Design (1): start exploratory (a few guest and staff interviews, review analysis), then a descriptive survey. Optional causal step: test a loyalty offer on a random half of past guests.
  • (c) Sources (1): primary: guest survey; secondary: booking system (stay dates, channel, spend), online review ratings, competitor prices.
  • (d) Sampling plan (2): population: guests who stayed in the last 18 months; frame: the CRM list of guests with e-mail or phone and consent to be contacted; technique: stratified random by property (and by loyal / lapsed); size: around 400 responses gives a margin of error near ±5 percentage points for a proportion at 95% confidence; plan for non-response.
  • (e) Variables and scales (1.5): overall satisfaction on a 1–5 Likert item (ordinal); likelihood to revisit (ordinal); spend per stay in ₹ (ratio); purpose of trip (nominal). Any three, correctly scaled.
  • (f) Ethics (1): informed consent, purpose limitation, no unnecessary personal fields, anonymised analysis file, secure storage, honest reporting of non-response.
  • (g) Analysis (1.5): clean the data; PivotTables of satisfaction by property; descriptive statistics; chi-square test of lapsed/loyal vs purpose; regression of revisit intention on service scores; a one-page dashboard.

Q1.13 A (hypothetical) food-delivery app plans to combine customers' order histories, delivery locations and late-night ordering times into a “lifestyle health score” and sell it to health insurers. The marketing head says, “Customers clicked ‘I agree’ at sign-up, so we are covered.” Evaluate the ethical and legal issues in the light of GDPR-style principles and India's DPDP Act, 2023, and recommend what the company should do.

Answer pointers
  • Consent (2): a sign-up click is not informed, specific consent for selling health inferences to a third party. Consent must be free, specific to the purpose and as easy to withdraw as to give.
  • Purpose limitation (1.5): data collected to deliver food is being reused for insurance pricing, a different and unexpected purpose.
  • Data minimisation and re-identification (1.5): location plus timing can identify a person even without a name; “anonymised” scores may not stay anonymous.
  • Data-subject rights (1): customers can access, correct and erase their data under both laws; GDPR also gives a right to object to purely automated decisions that affect them (such as a premium hike), which the DPDP Act does not spell out.
  • Bias and harm (1.5): night-shift workers or people in some neighbourhoods would be scored “unhealthy” for reasons that have nothing to do with health; that is unfair and may discriminate.
  • Recommendation (2.5): do not sell individual-level scores. If any partnership goes ahead: separate opt-in consent, clear notice, aggregated and genuinely anonymised data only, a data-protection impact assessment, retention limits and an independent review. Note the reputational risk too.

Q1.14 (a) The chart below appeared in a logistics firm's monthly review. Critically evaluate it and describe a fair version. (4 marks) (b) Explain any three sources of bias in a business survey, with a way to reduce each. (6 marks)

Answer pointers

(a) The chart (4 marks)

  • The vertical axis starts at 88%, not 0. The Q4 bar looks about 17 times taller than Q1, yet the rate rose only from 88.2% to 91.4%: 3.2 percentage points, or about 3.6% in relative terms.
  • The headline “soaring” is a biased interpretation of a modest change.
  • Fair version: start a bar chart at zero, or use a line chart with a clearly labelled, honest axis and the actual change written on it (“+3.2 pp in a year”); add the number of deliveries and the data source.
  • Mention the ethics angle: misleading graphs are one of the classic forms of unethical statistical practice.

(b) Three sources of bias (2 marks each)

  • Selection / sampling bias: the frame or method leaves out part of the population (an online-only survey misses customers who don't use apps). Fix: a frame that covers the population, probability sampling.
  • Non-response bias: people who reply differ from those who don't (only angry customers answer). Fix: reminders, short questionnaires, small incentives, compare early and late responders.
  • Response bias (social desirability, leading questions): respondents give the answer that sounds good or that the wording pushes. Fix: neutral wording, anonymity, pre-test the questionnaire.
  • Also acceptable: interviewer bias, survivorship bias, confirmation bias in analysis.

Q2.3 Bill values at a premium restaurant are strongly right-skewed. Arrange the mean, median and mode in order, explain why, and say which one you would report as the “typical bill”.

Answer pointers

Q4.1 Define R² and adjusted R². Why can adjusted R² fall when a new variable is added to a regression?

Answer pointers
  • R² (½): the share of the variation in Y explained by the model, R2=SSR/SSTR^2 = SSR/SSTR2=SSR/SST, between 0 and 1.
  • Adjusted R² (½): R² corrected for the number of predictors: 1−(1−R2)n−1n−k−11 - (1-R^2)\dfrac{n-1}{n-k-1}1−(1−R2)n−k−1n−1​.
  • Why it can fall (1): R² never drops when you add a variable, even a useless one. Adjusted R² charges a penalty for each extra predictor, so it rises only if the new variable explains more than chance would. Use it to compare models with different numbers of X's.

Q4.9 An analyst regressed customers' monthly spend on their income and plotted the residuals against the fitted values (below). What problem does the plot show? Why does it matter, and how could the analyst fix it?

Answer pointers
  • Problem (1): a funnel shape: residuals are tightly packed for low fitted values and spread out as fitted values grow. This is heteroscedasticity (the error variance is not constant).
  • Assumption broken (1): least squares assumes the same spread of errors at every level of X.
  • Why it matters (1½): the coefficients are still unbiased, but their standard errors are wrong, so t-stats, p-values and confidence intervals can't be trusted, and prediction intervals are too narrow for big spenders and too wide for small ones.
  • Fixes (1½): model log(spend) instead of spend; use per-unit ratios (spend as % of income); weighted least squares or heteroscedasticity-consistent standard errors (both beyond Excel's ToolPak); check whether a missing variable creates the pattern.

Q5.4 In factor analysis, explain in plain words: (a) an eigenvalue; (b) a factor loading; (c) the Kaiser criterion.

Answer pointers
  • (a) Eigenvalue (1): how much of the total variance of all the items one factor explains, measured in “items' worth”. With standardised items each item contributes 1, so an eigenvalue of 2.4 means the factor carries as much information as 2.4 items.
  • (b) Factor loading (1): the correlation between an item and a factor. A loading of 0.86 means the item is a strong marker of that factor; loadings below about 0.4 are usually ignored.
  • (c) Kaiser criterion (1): keep only factors with eigenvalue >\gt> 1, i.e. those that explain more than a single item does. Usually checked with a scree plot and cumulative % variance.

Q5.6 Distinguish between factor analysis and principal component analysis (PCA). What conditions should a data set meet before you run a factor analysis on a customer survey?

Answer pointers
  • PCA (1): a data-reduction method that rewrites many correlated variables as a few uncorrelated components that keep as much of the total variance as possible. Purpose: summarise or compress (e.g. combine 20 financial ratios into 3 indexes).
  • Factor analysis (1): assumes a few hidden (latent) factors, such as “service quality”, cause the correlations among items. Purpose: understand the underlying structure and build scales.
  • Key difference (1): PCA explains total variance and is descriptive; factor analysis explains shared (common) variance and is a model of latent constructs. In practice PCA is often used as the extraction step and the two give similar pictures with clean data.
  • Conditions (2): interval-type data (Likert items are usually accepted); adequate sample size (100+, ideally 5–10 respondents per item); items actually correlated (many correlations above 0.3); KMO measure of sampling adequacy above about 0.6; Bartlett's test of sphericity significant; no extreme outliers.

Q5.7 Explain hierarchical and k-means cluster analysis with a market-segmentation example. Why must variables usually be standardised before clustering?

Answer pointers
  • Idea (1): group customers so that members of a cluster are similar to each other and different from other clusters, based on a distance measure (usually Euclidean: d=∑(xi−yi)2d = \sqrt{\sum (x_i - y_i)^2}d=∑(xi​−yi​)2​).
  • Hierarchical (1½): start with every customer as its own cluster and repeatedly merge the two closest (agglomerative); the result is a tree (dendrogram) that you cut at a chosen height. Good for small samples and for choosing the number of clusters.
  • k-means (1½): choose k, place k starting centres, assign each customer to the nearest centre, recompute centres, repeat until stable. Fast for large customer bases. Example: a telecom firm groups 50,000 subscribers by data use, call minutes, recharge value and tenure into k = 4 segments.
  • Standardise (1): distance is dominated by variables with big numbers. Annual spend in rupees (thousands) would swamp “orders per year” (single digits). Converting each variable to z-scores gives each one equal say.

When the table and Excel disagree

Tables round z to two decimals, so your hand answer can differ from Excel in the third or fourth decimal (0.0358 vs 0.0357 in Q3.15, for example). In the exam, the table answer is the right answer. The pointers on this page give the table value first and the exact Excel value in brackets where they differ.

Exam-day checklist

  • Read the instructions first: how many to attempt in each section.
  • Start with Section A for quick marks, then your strongest 10-mark questions.
  • For every test: hypotheses → α → test and formula → compute → table value → decision in words.
  • For every output question: say which number you read and what it means.
  • Theory: definition, points with examples, a managerial takeaway. Headings help the examiner find your marks.
