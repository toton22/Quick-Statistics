Sampling

How to pick a few hundred customers so that what they tell you is true of all of them, and how far a sample average can wander from the truth.

The manager's question

The manager's question

You run card marketing at a mid-sized bank (hypothetical) with 48,000 credit-card holders. Before the board signs off a cashback scheme, it wants one number: the average monthly spend per cardholder. Phoning all 48,000 would take months. Your analyst says, “Give me 400 customers, picked properly, and I'll get you within about ₹400 of the true figure.”

Can 400 people really speak for 48,000? Which 400? And what does “within ₹400” actually mean?

A sample picked by chance, not by convenience, lets a few hundred people speak for lakhs, and the maths of sampling tells you how far off they might be.

Level 1 · Basics

The idea in plain words

Sampling means studying a part to learn about the whole. The whole is the population. The part you actually look at is the sample.

Almost every number a manager sees is a sample number: customer satisfaction scores, TV ratings, election forecasts, quality-control reports, audit findings. So the real question is never “sample or not?” It is “was the sample picked in a way I can trust?”

Why not just ask everyone? (a census)

Cost

Surveying 48,000 customers costs a fortune. A sample of 400 costs a small fraction of that.

Time

By the time a census is finished, the decision has already been made, or the market has moved.

Destructive tests

To measure battery life you run the battery until it dies. Test every battery and you have nothing left to sell.

Accuracy

Odd but true: a small, carefully run sample often beats a huge, sloppy census with tired interviewers and typing errors.

Think of it like

Tasting dal. You don't drink the whole pot to check the salt. One spoonful is enough, if you stir first. Stirring is randomisation: it gives every part of the pot the same chance to land in your spoon. A spoonful skimmed off the unstirred top is a convenience sample. It tastes of whatever happened to float up.

Probability sampling: the four methods you must know

In a probability sample every unit in the population has a known, non-zero chance of being picked. That one property is what lets you calculate a margin of error later. Here are the four standard designs.

1. Simple random sampling (SRS)

Every possible group of n units is equally likely to be the sample. Practically: number the frame 1 to N, then pick n numbers at random (a random-number table, or Excel). In business we sample without replacement: once a customer is picked, they can't be picked again.

2. Systematic sampling

Work out the interval k=N/nk = N/nk=N/n. Pick a random start between 1 and k, then take every k-th unit. For 4,000 members and a sample of 200, k=20k = 20k=20; with a random start of 7 you take members 7, 27, 47, 67 and so on. It is fast for long lists, invoice files and production lines.

The catch is periodicity. If the list has a hidden rhythm that matches k (every 7th day is a Sunday, every 10th flat is a corner flat), you could sample only Sundays or only corner flats.

3. Stratified sampling

Split the population into strata: groups that don't overlap and are alike inside (regions, income bands, departments). Then draw a simple random sample inside each stratum. With proportional allocation each stratum gets the same share of the sample as it has of the population: nh=n×Nh/Nn_h = n \times N_h / Nnh​=n×Nh​/N.

Two payoffs. Every group is guaranteed a voice, and the estimate is usually more precise than an SRS of the same size (the Pro section shows by how much).

4. Cluster sampling

Split the population into clusters: natural groups like stores, branches, villages or housing societies. Pick a few whole clusters at random and survey everyone inside them. It is cheap, because your field team visits 5 stores instead of 140. But it needs each cluster to be a mini-version of the population.

Multi-stage sampling

Big surveys combine the methods in stages. For example:

Stage 1 · strataSplit the country into states or city tiers
Stage 2 · clustersRandomly pick districts, then stores or villages inside them
Stage 3 · unitsInside each chosen store, interview every k-th customer

Non-probability sampling: quick, cheap, and not for inference

Convenience

Whoever is easiest to reach: shoppers at one mall, your college WhatsApp group, people who click a pop-up survey.

Judgement (purposive)

An expert hand-picks “typical” units, like a sales head choosing three test-market cities he believes are representative.

Quota

Fill fixed quotas (say 50% women, 30% aged 18–25), but the interviewer chooses who fills them. It looks like stratified sampling. It isn't, because the final choice is not random.

Snowball

Each respondent recruits the next. Useful for hard-to-reach groups such as gig workers or HNI investors.

Why these can't support inference

With a non-probability sample, nobody knows each person's chance of being picked. No known chances means no sampling distribution, and no sampling distribution means no valid standard error, margin of error, confidence interval or p-value. Use them for pilots, questionnaire testing and early ideas. Don't put a “±3%” on them.

Probability sample

Known chance for every unit. Costs more, needs a frame. Supports confidence intervals and hypothesis tests.

vs

Non-probability sample

Unknown chances. Fast and cheap. Describes only the people you happened to reach.

Two kinds of error: chance and bias

  • Coverage error: a survey run only through the mobile app misses customers who still bank at the branch.
  • Non-response bias: only angry customers bother to fill in the feedback form, so satisfaction looks worse than it is.
  • Response bias: “Don't you agree our service is excellent?” pushes people towards yes. Sensitive questions (income, drinking, loans) get under-reported.
  • Survivorship bias: studying only the mutual funds that still exist makes past returns look great, because the funds that died are missing from the data.
  • Processing error: typing 12000 as 1200, coding “strongly agree” as 1 in one file and 5 in another.

And how big should a sample be? Hold that thought. The short version: precision grows with the square root of the sample size, so quadrupling the sample only halves the error. The next section shows why.

Level 2 · Theory

Sampling distributions and the CLT

Here is the big mental leap of this whole pillar. A sample mean is not a fixed number. Draw a different 36 customers and you get a different x̄. So x̄ itself has a distribution.

Picture drawing every possible sample of 36 cardholders, computing x̄ for each one and plotting all those x̄ values. That histogram is the sampling distribution of x̄. You never see it in real life (you only draw one sample), but theory tells you its centre, its spread and its shape. That is what makes a margin of error possible.

In plain words: whatever shape the population has (skewed, flat, lumpy), the average of a big enough random sample behaves like a normal variable. That's why the normal curve shows up everywhere in business statistics, even though hardly any business data are normal to begin with.

What the CLT promises

  • x̄ is approximately normal for large n, whatever the population shape.
  • Its centre is μ and its spread is σ/√n.
  • The same idea works for sample proportions and totals.

What it does not promise

  • That the data themselves become normal. They don't.
  • That n = 30 is always enough. Very skewed data (insurance claims, salaries with a few crorepatis) need 50–100 or more.
  • Anything at all for a biased or non-random sample.
  • Anything about medians or maximums.

Why do we always round the sample size up?

n = 271.06 means 271 customers fall slightly short of the 95% target. You can't interview 0.06 of a person, so you take the next whole number, 272. Rounding down would quietly break the promise.

  • Standard error, n = 36: =4200/SQRT(36) → 700
  • P(17,500 ≤ x̄ ≤ 18,500): =NORM.DIST(18500,18000,4200/SQRT(36),TRUE) - NORM.DIST(17500,18000,4200/SQRT(36),TRUE) → 0.5249

The trick is the third argument. For a single customer you'd use σ = 4200. For an average of 36 customers you use the standard error 4200/SQRT(36). Forgetting this is the number-one Excel slip on this topic.

Explore it yourself

100 customers in four regions and ten stores. Draw a sample of 20 each way and compare the sample's mix and average with the population.
  • Press Simple random five times. How often does the smallest region get only 0 or 1 customers?
  • Switch to Stratified. The regional mix now matches the population on every draw. Watch how little the sample mean moves.
  • Try Cluster a few times. The sample mean jumps around much more, because two whole stores can be unusual.
Start from a right-skewed population (like order values). Draw 1,000 samples and watch the averages pile up into a bell.
  • Set n = 1 and draw 1,000 samples: the “means” are just single values, so the histogram copies the skewed population.
  • Move to n = 5, then n = 30. Compare the SD of the means with σ/√n in the readout.
  • Switch the shape to bimodal. Does n = 30 still give a bell? (It does: that is the CLT.)
Level 3 · Application

Where managers use it

Marketing

Brand-tracking surveys

Stratify by city tier (metro, tier 2, tier 3) so small-town buyers are not drowned out by Mumbai and Delhi respondents.

Finance & audit

Voucher checks

An auditor checks a random or systematic sample of 120 vouchers out of 18,000. The error rate in the sample becomes the estimate for the year.

Operations

Destructive testing

Battery life, tyre wear, crash tests: you test 50 units from a lot of 5,000 because a tested unit can't be sold.

HR

Engagement pulse

Stratify by department and grade so shop-floor staff are heard, not just the head office that answers emails first.

Retail

Mystery shopping

Cluster sampling: randomly pick 12 of 140 stores and audit every counter in them. The travel budget decides the design.

Banking

Loan-file reviews

Stratify by loan size and sample big loans more heavily, because one bad ₹5 crore loan matters more than ten bad ₹50,000 ones.

Indian context

The Census of India tries to count every person: a census. The National Sample Survey (NSS), which produces much of India's data on household spending and employment, deliberately does not. It uses a multi-stage stratified design: villages and urban blocks are chosen first, then households within them. TV ratings work the same way. BARC measures viewing in a panel of tens of thousands of homes, not in every TV home, and advertising worth crores is bought on those sample numbers.

Mini-case: a café chain's satisfaction survey

A (hypothetical) chai-and-snacks chain has 40 outlets: Dehradun 16, Haridwar 10, Rishikesh 8, Roorkee 6. Each outlet serves about 300 customers a day. The budget covers 320 interviews over one day per outlet visited. Here is a defensible three-stage design.

  1. Stage 1: stratify by cityProportional allocation of 320: Dehradun 320 × 16/40 = 128, Haridwar 80, Rishikesh 64, Roorkee 48.
  2. Stage 2: clusters inside each cityRandomly pick half the outlets in each city: 8, 5, 4 and 3 outlets (20 in all). That works out to exactly 16 interviews per outlet (128/8, 80/5, 64/4, 48/3).
  3. Stage 3: systematic at the counter300 customers ÷ 16 interviews ≈ 18.75, so interview every 18th customer from a random start between 1 and 18. Rounding down to 18 gives about 16.7 interviews a day, a small cushion over 16.
  4. Guard against biasSpread interviews over the whole day (morning regulars differ from evening crowds), and record refusals so non-response can be reported.

Notice what each stage buys you. The strata guarantee every city is represented. The clusters cut travel. The systematic step stops staff from choosing friendly-looking customers.

Level 4 · Pro

Nuance, pitfalls and depth

Finite population correction (FPC)

The formula σ/√n assumes the population is huge compared with the sample. When you sample a big slice of a small population, each pick tells you more, and the standard error shrinks a little extra.

Interview insight

“Why does a poll of about 1,000 people work for a small state and for the whole of India?” Because the standard error depends on n, not on N, once the sample is a tiny fraction of the population. The FPC is essentially 1 in both cases. What changes the precision is the number of people you ask, not the number of people who exist.

Why stratifying pays: the numbers

Take a second (hypothetical) bank whose cardholders are 60% urban (mean spend ₹24,000, SD ₹3,000) and 40% semi-urban (mean ₹10,000, SD ₹2,000). The overall mean is ₹18,400, but the overall SD is ₹7,351, because most of the variation is between the two groups.

Cluster sampling's catch

If customers inside a store are alike (a premium mall outlet full of high spenders), every extra interview in that store adds little new information. Ten interviews in one store can be worth only three or four independent ones. So cluster samples need more units for the same precision. Good clusters are mixed inside, mini-populations of their own.

Disproportionate allocation Beyond the syllabus

Proportional allocation gives the East region only 30 interviews, too few for a separate East estimate. Researchers often oversample small strata (say 60 in the East) and then re-weight the results so the East counts only as 15% of the total. You'll see “weighted” in the footnotes of most professional surveys.

Bigger is not better if the design is biased

The classic warning is the Literary Digest poll of the 1936 US presidential election. The magazine got back about 2.4 million ballots and still predicted the wrong winner by a wide margin. Its lists came largely from telephone directories and car registrations (a richer slice of voters in the Depression), and the people who chose to reply differed from those who didn't. A random sample of a couple of thousand would have done far better. A huge biased sample just gives you the wrong answer very precisely.

How a consultant would present it

“We interviewed 400 cardholders chosen at random from the full list of 48,000. Average monthly spend was ₹18,240, accurate to within about ±₹412 at 95% confidence. 29% of those contacted did not respond; non-respondents were younger, so we checked the result by age group.” (Illustrative figures.) One sentence each on how the sample was drawn, the result with its margin, and the known weaknesses.

Exam-style questions on these slides (our own)

Q1 (5 marks). Distinguish stratified from cluster sampling, with one business example of each.
  • Stratified: groups are alike inside and differ from each other; sample randomly from every stratum. Goal: precision and representation. Example: a bank surveys customers stratified by account type (savings, current, NRI).
  • Cluster: groups are natural units (stores, branches), each mixed inside; randomly pick some clusters and survey everyone in them. Goal: lower cost. Example: an FMCG firm audits shelf display in 15 randomly chosen retail outlets out of 300.
  • One line to finish: stratified samples within all groups; cluster samples groups.
Q2 (5 marks). A start-up posts a survey link on Instagram and gets 2,000 replies. Can it report “72% of Indian millennials prefer our app, ±2%”?
  • No. This is a convenience (self-selected) sample: followers who chose to reply.
  • Selection probabilities are unknown, so there is no valid standard error and the “±2%” is meaningless.
  • Likely biases: coverage (only Instagram users who follow the brand), non-response (fans reply more), response bias.
  • Fix: draw a probability sample from a proper frame (say, a panel of millennials), or describe the result honestly as “72% of 2,000 followers who answered”.

Common mistakes

WrongRight
“Random” means haphazard: I just stopped whoever walked by.Random means a known chance for every unit, produced by a random-number mechanism, not by the interviewer's choice.
Using σ in NORM.DIST for a question about x̄.Use the standard error σ/√n for any question about a sample mean.
A bigger sample removes bias.A bigger sample only shrinks sampling error. Coverage, non-response and response bias stay.
The CLT makes the data normal.The CLT is about the distribution of x̄ across samples. The raw data keep their shape.
Quota sampling is the same as stratified sampling.Both fix the mix by group, but in quota sampling the interviewer picks who fills the quota, so it isn't a probability sample.
Doubling the sample halves the error.You must quadruple n to halve the standard error, because n sits under a square root.
Rounding a required sample size down (271.06 → 271).Always round up (272).
Confusing strata and clusters.Strata: alike inside, sample from every one. Clusters: mixed inside, take a few whole ones.

  3. The sample mean will equal the population mean
