What this pillar does for a manager

The manager’s question

Your boss forwards a spreadsheet: one column, 50 numbers, and a one-line email. “What does this tell us?” You have ten minutes before the call. What do you say?

Descriptive statistics answers four questions about the data you already hold: what’s typical, how much it varies, what shape it takes, and how best to show it.

That really is the whole pillar. “Descriptive” means you describe the data in front of you and stop there. You’re not yet claiming anything about customers you never surveyed; that jump is inferential statistics, in Module 3.

Honestly, most analysis in a real job is descriptive. A sales review, an HR pay audit, a delivery-time dashboard: each is a few well-chosen summaries and two or three honest charts. Get this pillar right and every later module gets easier, because they all reuse these same numbers.

Decision guide

Start with the data, not the chart menu. Two questions settle almost everything: is the variable a category or a number, and are you looking at one variable or two?

The two classic chart mistakes

A pie chart of a quantitative variable (200 loan amounts are not “parts of a whole”), and a line chart joining categories that have no order (a line from “Delhi” to “Pune” means nothing). Both lose marks in the exam and credibility in a meeting.

Rating scales

“Excellent … Poor” or 1–5 ratings are ordinal categories. Report counts and percentages (and the share in the top two boxes). A mean rating is common in business, but say it treats the gaps between ratings as equal, which is an assumption.

Pelican Stores

A clothing chain mails discount coupons to new shoppers. Who turned up, how much did they spend, and was the promotion worth it?

Syllabus case · Unit 1 Used again in Unit 2

The situation

Pelican Stores is a chain of women’s clothing stores owned by a larger apparel group, National Clothing. To win new customers, the group mailed discount coupons to people who shop at its other stores. On one day of the promotion, Pelican sampled 100 in-store credit-card transactions.

Each record notes whether the shopper used a coupon (a promotional customer) or not (a regular customer), how many items they bought, the net amount charged, how they paid (one option is the group’s own store card), and their gender, marital status and age. Management sees coupon sales as extra sales it would not otherwise have made, and it hopes these new shoppers come back.

What the manager wants

Part A · Chapter 2, tables and charts

  1. Percent frequency distributions for the key variables.
  2. A bar or pie chart of the number of purchases by method of payment.
  3. A crosstabulation of customer type (regular or promotional) against net sales, with comments on how the two groups compare.
  4. A scatter diagram of net sales against customer age.

Part B · Chapter 3, numerical measures

  1. Descriptive statistics for net sales, overall and for different groups of customers (by type, payment method, gender, marital status).
  2. Numerical measures of the relationship between age and net sales.

Data you need

File PelicanStores (Excel) from the textbook companion site, or ask your instructor. 100 rows, one per transaction. Variables: Customer (ID), Type of Customer (Regular / Promotional), Items (number bought), Net Sales ($ charged), Method of Payment (store card or one of several credit cards), Gender, Marital Status, Age.

Why it is also a Unit 1 case

Before any chart, sort the eight variables by type: four are categorical (type of customer, payment method, gender, marital status) and three are quantitative (items, net sales, age), with the ID being neither. That is the measurement-scales lesson from Research foundations, and it decides every tool below. Also check the file the way Unit 1 teaches: consistent labels, no blanks, no duplicate IDs.

How to crack it

  1. Classify, then cleanMark each column categorical or quantitative. Filter each categorical column to check the labels are spelt one way only.
  2. Percent frequency tables (task 1)For each categorical variable: Insert › PivotTable, the variable in Rows and again in Values (Count), then Show Values As › % of Grand Total. For net sales and age, pick sensible classes (for example $50 wide, 10 years wide) and use Data › Data Analysis › Histogram or FREQUENCY.
  3. Payment chart (task 2)A column chart of the payment PivotTable, sorted largest first. A pie is acceptable only if the number of payment methods stays small.
  4. Crosstab (task 3)PivotTable with Type of Customer in Rows and Net Sales in Columns; right-click a net-sales value › Group into $50 bands; show % of Row Total so the two customer types can be compared fairly.
  5. Scatter (task 4)Insert › Scatter with Age on the horizontal axis and Net Sales on the vertical. Add axis titles with units.
  6. Numerical summaries (task 5)Run Data › Data Analysis › Descriptive Statistics on Net Sales. For groups, use a PivotTable (Average, StdDev, Count of Net Sales) and add medians with =MEDIAN(IF(B2:B101="Promotional",D2:D101)). Compare medians as well as means, because spending data are usually right-skewed.
  7. Age and spending (task 6)=COVARIANCE.S(age,sales) and =CORREL(age,sales). Describe the strength in words and look back at your scatter.
What a strong managerial report includes
  • A customer profile in one paragraph: the gender and marital-status mix, typical age, typical number of items and typical spend (median, with the quartiles).
  • The promotion verdict: what share of transactions and of sales came from promotional customers; how their median and mean spend compare with regular customers; whether they paid with the store card (a hint they may return).
  • Side-by-side tables for regular vs promotional rather than two separate outputs, so the comparison is instant.
  • Clean charts: titles, axis labels with $ and units, bars sorted, no 3-D effects.
  • The age question answered plainly: “older shoppers spend a little more / less / about the same”, with r quoted and the scatter shown.
  • Limits stated: one day, 100 card transactions, no cash buyers, and no data on what the coupons cost. So the report describes this sample; it can’t prove the promotion pays.
  • One or two recommendations that follow from the numbers (for example, a follow-up offer to promotional shoppers to turn them into regulars).

Read the full case in the textbook, p. 84 (Chapter 2) and p. 157 (Chapter 3).

Anderson et al. (12e) · Ch. 3 · Case Problem 4 · p. 161

Heavenly Chocolates Website Transactions

A premium chocolate maker’s online shop is doing better than expected. What do its web shoppers look like, and what makes them spend?

Syllabus case · Unit 2

The situation

Heavenly Chocolates makes quality chocolates and sells them from a shop at its factory in upstate New York. Two years ago it opened a website, and online sales have beaten the plan. Management now wants ideas to grow them further, so it pulled a sample of 50 website transactions from last month.

For each transaction we know the day of the week, the browser the customer used, the minutes spent on the site, the number of pages viewed and the amount spent. Management’s hunch: shoppers who stay longer and look at more pages spend more. It also wants to know whether the day of the week and the browser make a difference.

What the manager wants

  1. Graphical and numerical summaries of time on the site, pages viewed and amount spent per transaction, and what they say about online shoppers.
  2. For each day of the week: the number of transactions, the total spent and the mean spent per transaction, with observations.
  3. The same three numbers for each type of browser, with observations.
  4. A scatter diagram and correlation for time on site (horizontal axis) against amount spent.
  5. A scatter diagram and correlation for pages viewed (horizontal axis) against amount spent.
  6. A scatter diagram and correlation for pages viewed (horizontal axis) against time on site.

Data you need

File Shoppers (Excel) from the textbook companion site, or ask your instructor. 50 rows. Variables: Customer, Day (Mon–Sun), Browser (Internet Explorer, Firefox, Other and so on), Time (minutes on site), Pages Viewed, Amount Spent ($).

How to crack it

  1. One summary table for the three numbers (task 1)Data › Data Analysis › Descriptive Statistics, select all three numeric columns with their headings, tick Labels in first row and Summary statistics. Add quartiles with QUARTILE.EXC.
  2. Pictures for the three numbersA histogram (or box plot) of each. Check the shape before you choose mean or median for the report.
  3. Day-of-week table (task 2)PivotTable: Day in Rows; Amount Spent in Values three times, set to Count, Sum and Average. Sort the days Monday to Sunday, not alphabetically.
  4. Browser table (task 3)Same PivotTable with Browser in Rows. Note how many transactions each browser has; tiny groups give shaky averages.
  5. Three scatters and three r’s (tasks 4–6)Insert › Scatter with the axes the case specifies. CORREL for each pair, or Data › Data Analysis › Correlation on the three numeric columns for a 3 × 3 matrix in one go.
  6. Turn numbers into sentencesFor each table and chart, write one line: what it shows and what the business could do about it.
What a strong managerial report includes
  • A portrait of the typical online visit: the usual time on site, pages viewed and spend (median and IQR if skewed), and how much they vary.
  • Day of week, read three ways: the busiest day by number of orders may not be the biggest by total revenue or by spend per order. Say which is which and why it matters for, say, timing an email campaign.
  • Browser differences with a caution about small groups; it may say more about device or customer type than about the browser itself.
  • Three correlations in words (“strong positive”, “weak”), each beside its scatter, and the honest warning that correlation is not causation: a long visit may signal a keen buyer rather than cause the spending.
  • Two or three concrete actions for the website team, each tied to a number in the report.
  • Limits: one month, 50 transactions; patterns are leads to test, not proof.

Read the full case in the textbook, p. 161. The scatter and correlation parts are taught on Correlation.

Anderson et al. (12e) · Ch. 2 Case Problem 2, p. 85 · Ch. 3 Case Problem 2, p. 158

Motion Picture Industry

A handful of blockbusters and a long line of ordinary releases. Which measures of a film’s early performance go with a big final box-office total?

The situation

Film studios release several hundred movies a year, and their earnings vary enormously. The industry tracks four yardsticks of success: the opening-weekend gross, the total gross, the number of theatres a film plays in, and the number of weeks it stays in release. The case gives these four numbers for the 100 top-grossing films released in 2011.

What the manager wants

Chapter 2 · tables and charts

  1. Tabular and graphical summaries of each of the four variables, and what each says about the industry.
  2. Scatter diagrams of total gross against opening-weekend gross, against number of theatres, and against weeks in release, each discussed.

Chapter 3 · numerical measures

  1. Descriptive statistics for the four variables, and what they tell you.
  2. Which films, if any, count as high-performance outliers, and why.
  3. Numerical measures of the relationship between total gross and each of the other three variables.

Data you need

File 2011Movies (Excel) from the textbook companion site, or ask your instructor. 100 rows. Variables: Motion Picture (title), Opening Gross Sales ($ million), Total Gross Sales ($ million), Number of Theaters, Weeks in Release.

How to crack it

  1. Summaries firstDescriptive Statistics on all four numeric columns at once; add QUARTILE.EXC for Q1 and Q3.
  2. A histogram per variableHistogram tool with tidy bins (for example $25 million for the gross variables, 500 for theatres, 2 for weeks). Write the shape of each in a word: symmetric, right-skewed, left-skewed.
  3. Find the outliersTwo ways, and compare: z-scores with =STANDARDIZE(x,AVERAGE(r),STDEV.S(r)) (flag |z| > 3), and box-plot fences Q3 + 1.5 × IQR. Sort by total gross and list the films past the fence.
  4. Three scattersTotal gross on the vertical axis each time; opening gross, theatres and weeks on the horizontal. Look for curves and clusters, not just direction.
  5. Three correlationsCORREL for each pair, or the Correlation tool for the full 4 × 4 matrix. Rank the three predictors by the size of r.
What a strong managerial report includes
  • An industry profile that respects the skew: medians and quartiles for the money variables, with the mean shown beside them, and one sentence on how far a few hits pull the mean.
  • The outlier list by name, with how far each sits beyond the fence or how many SDs above the mean, and a note on whether the two rules agree.
  • The relationship verdict: which early signal tracks total gross most closely (compare the r values), in plain words a studio executive could use.
  • Charts that tell one story each, with titles and units ($ million, theatres, weeks).
  • Limits: only the top 100 films are in the file (the flops are missing), only one year, and theatre counts have a natural ceiling. Correlation still isn’t causation; a big opening and a big total may both come from a big marketing budget.

Exam

How this pillar is examined

The end-semester exam is pen and paper: 100 marks, statistical tables provided, no Excel. So everything you did with AVERAGE and the ToolPak in class has to be done by hand, with the working on the page. A calculator with a statistics mode is a good check, but the examiner marks the working, not the calculator.

Descriptive questions reward method. The steps are fixed, so a well-laid-out answer collects most of the marks even if one subtraction slips. And they punish two things: missing interpretation, and sloppy presentation (unlabelled charts, overlapping classes, no units).

Examiner’s notes

Examiner’s note 1 · Frequency distributions and histograms

Full marks: equal, non-overlapping classes that cover every value (0–9, 10–19, … or “0 to under 10”); a tally or count that you check adds up to nnn; relative frequencies that sum to 1 (or 100%); a histogram with touching bars, a labelled axis with units and a frequency axis starting at zero; and a sentence on shape, the most frequent class and where the middle lies.

Common mark losses: classes like 10–20, 20–30 (where does 20 go?); unequal widths without comment; gaps between histogram bars; a cumulative column that doesn’t reach nnn; no comment on shape. A bar chart for categories, on the other hand, should have gaps.

Examiner’s note 2 · Mean, median, SD from a small dataset

Full marks: sort the data before the median and write the position (n+1)/2(n+1)/2(n+1)/2; a deviation table with x−xˉx - \bar{x}x−xˉ and (x−xˉ)2(x-\bar{x})^2(x−xˉ)2 and their column sums (the first should be 0, a free check); divide by n−1n-1n−1 for a sample; give the SD in the original units; round only at the end; then one line on which average is the honest summary and why.

Common mark losses: dividing by nnn for sample data; reporting the variance as if it were in rupees or minutes; taking the middle of the unsorted list; quoting the mean of skewed data with no mention of the median; forgetting units altogether.

Examiner’s note 3 · Quartiles and box plots

Full marks: state your quartile rule in one line (the class uses position p(n+1)p(n+1)p(n+1), as in QUARTILE.EXC); show the positions and any interpolation; compute IQR and both fences; draw the box from Q1 to Q3 with the median line, whiskers to the most extreme values inside the fences, and outliers as separate dots; then read the plot: centre, spread, skew and what the outliers might mean for the business.

Common mark losses: whiskers drawn out to an outlier; fences computed but outliers never marked; a box plot with no scale. Different books use slightly different quartile rules: for LargeCorp, Q1 is 54.75 with p(n+1)p(n+1)p(n+1), 55.75 with QUARTILE.INC, and 55 with the textbook’s np/100np/100np/100 round-up rule. Stating your rule lets the examiner follow your working.

  • Symmetric: mean ± SD. Skewed or outliers: median and IQR. Comparing different scales: CV.
