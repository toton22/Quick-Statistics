The manager's question

The manager's question

You head sales for a pharma distributor in Lucknow. HR is about to hire a rep with 9 years of experience and asks, “What annual sales should we pencil in for her?” Ten minutes later the marketing head walks in: “If I give a territory ₹1 lakh more for ads and one more rep, how much extra sales do I get? And which of the two is actually doing the work?”

Two different people, one question in disguise: when one number moves, how much does another number move?

Regression finds the straight line (or, with several drivers, the flat sheet) that sits closest to your data. That line tells you how much y changes when x changes, and it lets you predict y for an x you haven’t seen yet.

Level 1 · Basics

The idea in plain words

Regression writes down the relationship between a number you want to predict and the numbers you already know, as an equation you can actually use.

Think of it like

A cab fare. There is a fixed starting charge plus a rate per kilometre. Now imagine you only have a shoebox of old receipts: distance and fare for each trip. You could work backwards and estimate both the starting charge and the per-km rate. That is exactly what regression does. The starting charge is the intercept, the per-km rate is the slope, and whatever part of each fare the formula can’t explain (traffic, waiting, surge) is the residual.

Which variable is which?

Every regression has one dependent variable (y), the outcome you want to explain or predict, and one or more independent variables (x), the inputs you use to explain it. The y goes on the vertical axis. Honestly, the names confuse people: “independent” doesn’t mean “unrelated”. It just means “the input you feed in”.

Quick test

Ask yourself: “Which number do I want to predict?” That one is y. Everything you’ll know in advance is an x.

Correlation or regression?

You met correlation on the correlation page. It squeezes a scatter diagram into one number, r, that says how tightly two variables move together. Regression goes further. It gives you an equation with a slope in real units (rupees per year, minutes per km) and lets you predict.

Correlation

One number between −1 and +1. Symmetric: r between sales and experience equals r between experience and sales. No units, no prediction.

vs

Regression

An equation, ŷ = b₀ + b₁x. One-way: y is explained by x. Slope has units. Predicts y. Extends to many x’s at once.

Neither one proves cause

A strong line between ice-cream sales and sunburn cases doesn’t mean ice cream burns skin. Both follow the temperature. Regression measures association; cause needs an experiment or a very good argument.

2. Least squares, in plain words

Draw any line through the scatter. Each point now has a residual, its vertical distance from your line. Square each residual (so negatives don’t cancel out, and big misses count extra), then add them up. That total is the sum of squared errors, SSE. The least-squares line is simply the one line, out of all possible lines, with the smallest SSE.

Figure 3 makes the squares literal. On the left, the least-squares line; on the right, a line most people would draw by eye, joining the first and last salesperson. Same data. The tinted squares are the squared residuals. Add up the areas and the eyeballed line loses.

3. Reading the slope and the intercept in business words

Exam template

Slope: “For every one-[unit of x] increase in [x], [y] changes by [b₁ with units], on average.”
Intercept: “When [x] is zero, the predicted [y] is [b₀ with units],” followed by whether x = 0 makes sense.

Sales data: b₁ = 4 and y is in $1000s, so each extra year of experience is associated with $4,000 more annual sales, on average. Note the units. Writing “sales go up by 4” loses marks; “by $4,000” earns them.

The intercept, b₀ = 80, says a salesperson with zero experience is predicted to sell $80,000. Is that meaningful? Borderline. The least experienced person in the data has 1 year, so x = 0 is just outside the data. Contrast this with the house-price example on the class slides (slide 21): a house of 0 square feet doesn’t exist, so its intercept is just a mathematical anchor for the line and should not be interpreted.

When the intercept is meaningless

If x = 0 is impossible (a flat with 0 sq ft, a store with 0 footfall) or far outside your data, say so in one line and don’t interpret b₀. You still keep it in the equation. It positions the line.

4. Prediction: interpolation vs extrapolation

To predict, plug x into the equation. A salesperson with 9 years: ŷ = 80 + 4(9) = 116, i.e. $116,000. Because 9 sits inside the observed range (1 to 13 years), this is interpolation, and you can use it with reasonable confidence.

Predicting for 25 years gives ŷ = 180. That is extrapolation. Nobody in the sample has that much experience, so we have no evidence the straight line keeps going. Sales may well flatten out as people move into management.

5. How good is the fit? SST = SSR + SSE

Without any x, your best guess for every salesperson would be the average, ȳ = 108. The total squared distance of the points from that average is the total sum of squares, SST. The line explains part of it (SSR, regression sum of squares); the rest is left over (SSE, error sum of squares).

It depends on the field. Cost-and-volume relationships in operations often give r² above 0.9. Consumer behaviour is noisier, and an r² of 0.3 can still be commercially useful. Judge sₑ too: a typical miss of $4,600 on sales of around $108,000 is about 4%, which is pretty tight.

The two-second check

Turn your head and look at the residual plot as if it were static on a TV. If you can see any shape at all (a smile, a funnel, a wave, one lonely point far away), the model is missing something.

9. Dummy (indicator) variables

Regression needs numbers, but many drivers are categories: region, gender, MBA or not, promotion week or not. The trick is to code a two-level category as a dummy variable: 1 if the trait is present, 0 if not.

Take salaries (Problem M4): y = salary (₹ lakh p.a.), x₁ = years of experience, x₂ = MBA (1 = has an MBA, 0 = doesn’t). The fitted equation is ŷ = 4.31 + 0.731x₁ + 3.73x₂. Plug in the two values of the dummy and you get two lines:

  • No MBA (x₂ = 0): ŷ = 4.31 + 0.731x₁
  • MBA (x₂ = 1): ŷ = (4.31 + 3.73) + 0.731x₁ = 8.04 + 0.731x₁

So b₂ = 3.73 reads: at the same experience, employees with an MBA earn about ₹3.73 lakh a year more, on average. The comparison group (here “no MBA”) is called the base or reference category.

Rules for dummies
  • A category with m levels needs m − 1 dummies. Region with North, South, East, West → three dummies (South, East, West), and North becomes the base.
  • Every dummy’s coefficient is a difference from the base, holding the other x’s constant.
  • Never include all m dummies plus the intercept. They add up to 1 in every row, Excel can’t separate them, and you get nonsense (the “dummy variable trap”).

Why do the deviation columns always add up to zero?

Because the mean is the balance point. Σ(x − x̄) = Σx − n x̄ = Σx − Σx = 0. The same goes for y. If your Σ(x − x̄) column doesn’t total zero (allowing for rounding), you’ve made an arithmetic slip, so it’s a free check in the exam.

Where managers use regression

Wherever someone asks “how much?” and “what if?”, there’s usually a regression behind the answer.

Marketing

Marketing-mix models

Sales regressed on TV, digital and print spend (plus a festive-season dummy) to see which rupee works hardest. The slopes become return-per-rupee estimates for next year’s budget.

Finance

A stock’s beta

Regress a stock’s monthly return on the market’s (say, the Nifty 50). The slope is beta: 1.3 means the stock tends to move 1.3% for every 1% market move. Anderson’s Case Problem “Measuring Stock Market Risk” (Ch. 14) builds exactly this.

HR

Pay-equity audits

Salary regressed on experience, grade, performance rating and a gender dummy. A significant gender coefficient, with everything else held constant, is a red flag the company must explain.

Operations

Promise times

Delivery or turnaround time on distance, number of stops and order size. The equation sets customer promise windows and flags routes that run late (Problem M3).

Real estate & banking

Valuation and lending

Flat prices on carpet area, age, floor and locality dummies (a “hedonic” model, Problem M2). Banks use similar models to sanity-check valuations before sanctioning a home loan.

Outliers and influential points Light coverage

An outlier is a point with an unusually large residual. A rough rule: a standardised residual (e/sₑ) beyond ±2 deserves a look, beyond ±3 almost certainly. An influential point is one that moves the line a lot when you include or drop it. Points with extreme x values are the usual suspects, because they have the most pull on the slope.

What to do with an odd point

First check the data entry (a sales figure typed in rupees instead of lakh is the classic). If it’s genuine, don’t quietly delete it. Run the model with and without it, and report both if the story changes. Excel’s Standardized Residuals tick-box flags candidates; specialist software adds Cook’s distance for influence.

Curves and log transforms Beyond the syllabus

If the residual plot shows a curve (Figure 6, panel 2), a straight line is the wrong shape. Two common repairs:

  • Add x² (a quadratic term) for a hump or a U. In Excel, add a column =A2^2 and include it as a second x.
  • Take logs. Make columns with =LN(B2) and regress on those. In a log-log model, ln y = b₀ + b₁ ln x, the slope is an elasticity: a b₁ of −1.4 in a demand model means a 1% price rise cuts quantity by about 1.4%. In a log-y model, ln y = b₀ + b₁x, each extra unit of x changes y by about 100 × b₁ percent; for b₁ = 0.08 the exact figure is e0.08−1=0.083e^{0.08} - 1 = 0.083e0.08−1=0.083, i.e. 8.3% per unit.

Logs also tame right-skewed money variables (salaries, revenue, house prices) and often fix a funnel-shaped residual plot at the same time.

Heteroscedasticity (unequal spread)

The E in LINE. If residuals fan out as ŷ grows (Figure 6, panel 3), the errors don’t have constant variance. Fixes: model log y instead of y; model a ratio (sales per square foot rather than sales); or use weighted least squares or heteroscedasticity-consistent standard errors, which need software beyond the ToolPak.

Stepwise logic

Forward: start empty; add the x with the smallest P-value; repeat while it’s below 0.05. Backward: start with all x’s; drop the one with the largest P-value above 0.05; refit; repeat. Regression is not causation

Three traps
  • Omitted variables. In M6, demand regressed on price alone gives a positive slope (+1.96): higher prices, more scooters sold? No. Income rose at the same time and drove both. Leave out a driver and its effect leaks into whatever is left.
  • Reverse causality. Firms spend more on ads in the months they expect strong sales. The regression can’t tell which way the arrow points.
  • Selection. The MBA premium in M4 may partly reflect who chooses to do an MBA, not what the degree adds.

Two series that both trend over time (GDP and mobile subscriptions, say) give a huge R² even if unrelated. For data in time order see the time series page.

Excel’s ToolPak has no logistic tool. It can be fitted with Solver (by maximising the log-likelihood) or, far more easily, in Python, R or an AI data assistant. Anderson et al. (12e) Section 15.9 walks through a full example with software output.

How a consultant would present it

One slide, four lines. The finding in words with a range: “After allowing for headcount, each extra ₹1 lakh of advertising goes with ₹2.3–6.1 lakh more quarterly sales per territory (95% CI).” How much it explains: “Ad spend and reps together explain 88% of the variation (adjusted R²).” The base: “12 territories, one quarter.” The caveat: “An association, not a controlled test; we recommend a two-territory pilot.” Nobody in the room needs to see a P-value.

How to crack it in Excel

  1. Describe the data (report item 1)Run Data › Data Analysis › Descriptive Statistics on all three columns with Summary statistics ticked. Report mean, median, SD, minimum and maximum; compare mean with median to spot skew; use CV = SD/mean to compare spread. Draw two scatter charts (charges vs income, charges vs household size) and run Data › Data Analysis › Correlation for the 3 × 3 matrix. Say which x looks more closely related to charges, and whether income and household size are related to each other.
  2. Two simple regressions (item 2)Run Regression twice with Y = amount charged: first X = income, then X = household size. For each, write the equation and interpret the slope in dollars: “each extra $1,000 of income goes with $__ more charges a year”; “each additional household member goes with $__ more”. Compare R², the standard error and Significance F. Both models predict the same y, so the one with the higher R² and the lower standard error is the better single predictor.
  3. The multiple regression (item 3)Place income and household size in adjacent columns and run Regression with both as X. Check Significance F first, then each P-value. Interpret each slope “holding the other constant”. Compare adjusted R² and the standard error with the two simple models: does using both clearly beat either alone? Did the slopes change much from the simple regressions? (If they did, the two x’s overlap.) Tick Residual Plots and look for curves, funnels or lone outliers.
  4. The prediction (item 4)Plug household size = 3 and income = 40 into the multiple regression equation. Income is recorded in $1000s, so $40,000 enters as 40, not 40,000. This is the single most common slip in this case. Check that both values lie inside the data range, and give the answer in dollars with a rough range (about ± 2 standard errors for one household).
  5. Other variables (item 5)Suggest drivers with a business reason: age or life stage, credit limit and number of cards, existing loans or EMIs, credit score, occupation, education, home ownership (a dummy), city tier or region (dummies), online-shopping frequency, rewards-programme membership. For each, say why it might matter and how you would measure it, and flag likely multicollinearity (education and income usually travel together).

WrongRight
Regressing the wrong way round (experience on sales), or typing =SLOPE(A2:A11,B2:B11)y is the variable you want to predict. Excel’s functions take y first: =SLOPE(B2:B11,A2:A11).

Interpreting the intercept when x = 0 is impossible or far outside the dataSay “x = 0 is outside the data, so b₀ has no practical meaning” and move on.
Predicting for x far outside the observed rangeInterpolate. If you must extrapolate, say so and treat the number as a rough guess.

Quick check

In the class Sales model ŷ = 80 + 4x (x = years of experience, y = annual sales in $1000s), what does the 4 mean?

  1. Sales rise by 4% for each extra year of experience
  2. A salesperson with 4 years of experience sells $80,000
  3. Each extra year of experience is associated with $4,000 more annual sales, on average
  4. Experience explains 4% of the variation in sales
The slope is the change in ŷ for a one-unit rise in x, in y’s units. y is in $1000s, so 4 means $4,000 per extra year.

A simple regression has SST = 800 and SSE = 200. What is r²?

  1. 0.75
  2. 0.25
  3. 0.80
  4. 1.33
SSR = SST − SSE = 600, so r² = SSR/SST = 600/800 = 0.75. (0.25 is SSE/SST, the unexplained share.)

You add a fifth x variable to a model. R² rises from 0.812 to 0.815, but adjusted R² falls from 0.790 to 0.786. What should you conclude?

  1. The new variable is highly significant
  2. Keep it, because R² went up
  3. The whole model is now invalid
  4. The new variable doesn’t add enough to justify itself; leave it out
R² can only go up when you add a variable. Adjusted R² falling means the small gain doesn’t cover the lost degree of freedom.

In ŷ = 4.31 + 0.731 Exp + 3.73 MBA (salary in ₹ lakh, Exp in years, MBA = 1 if the person has an MBA), what does 3.73 tell you?

  1. The salary of an MBA with zero experience
  2. The average salary gap between MBAs and non-MBAs with the same experience
  3. The extra salary per year of experience for MBAs only
  4. The probability that an MBA earns more than a non-MBA
A dummy’s coefficient shifts the intercept: MBA holders sit ₹3.73 lakh above non-MBAs at every level of experience. (An MBA with zero experience is predicted at 4.31 + 3.73 = 8.04.)

A simple regression on 10 observations reports F = 106.92 in its ANOVA table. What is the t Stat for the slope (ignoring sign)?

  1. 53.46
  2. 106.92
  3. 10.34
  4. It can’t be found without the coefficients table
With one x, F = t², so |t| = √106.92 = 10.34. That is exactly the Sales data’s slope t.
