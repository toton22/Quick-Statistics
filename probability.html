Why managers need probability

Every business decision has an “it depends” hiding in it. Will the customer pay back? Will the new toy sell? Will the parcel arrive on time? Probability is how you turn that “it depends” into a number you can price, compare and plan around.

You don't need to love maths for this. Honestly, most of the pillar is careful counting, a few rules for combining chances, and two ready-made shapes (binomial and normal) that save you the counting. Here is what that buys a manager:

Insurance · Pricing

What should a policy cost?

Suppose 7% of gadget-protection buyers claim ₹5,000 and 3% claim ₹15,000. The expected claim is ₹800 per policy. Charge less than that and every sale loses money on average.

Retail · Inventory

How much stock to order?

If demand for a new toy is roughly normal with mean 20,000 and SD 5,102, ordering 24,000 units still leaves about a 22% chance of running out (the Specialty Toys case below).

Banking · Credit risk

Should we recall this card?

5% of cardholders default. Among those who miss a payment, the default chance jumps to about 21% (class problem 8). That one update decides whether the card is recalled.

Marketing · A/B tests

Is the new page really better?

The old landing page converts 10%. A new one gets 15 sign-ups from 100 visitors. Impressive? Even with no real change, 15 or more happens about 7% of the time (0.0726) purely by luck.

Probability turns “it might happen” into a number, and a number is something you can put in a budget.

The four topics in this pillar

Each card opens a full Basics → Pro page. The line in bold is the question that topic answers for you.

1 · Probability basics

“How likely is this event, and what if I combine two events with OR?”

Experiments, sample space, events, contingency tables, marginal and joint probability, the addition law, complements, counting with combinations. Class problems 1–3.

2 · Conditional probability

“Now that I know something new, how should my estimate change?”

Conditional probability, independence checks, the multiplication law, probability trees and Bayes' theorem (prior → posterior). Class problems 4–9.

3 · Random variables

“What is the average outcome, and how much risk sits around it?”

Discrete vs continuous, probability distribution tables, expected value, variance and SD of a profit or demand table. Workbook sheet “Expected Value”.

4 · Distributions

“Which ready-made shape fits, and what is the chance of a count or a measurement?”

Binomial (exactly, at least, more than, expected number), normal (areas, z-scores, cut-offs), Poisson (light). The discrete and continuous problem sheets.

Think of it like

A weather forecast. “70% chance of rain” is an event probability. “Given these clouds, 90%” is conditional. “How many rainy days this month?” is a random variable. And “rainy days follow a binomial pattern” is a distribution. Same sky, four questions.

Anderson et al. (12e) · Ch. 4 · Case Problem · p. 212

Hamilton County Judges

A newspaper wants to know which judges make too many mistakes. Appeals and reversals are the evidence; conditional probability is the tool.

The situation

A US county's courts disposed of roughly 183,000 cases over three years, handled by 38 judges in three courts: Common Pleas, Domestic Relations and Municipal. In most cases the verdict simply stands. Some cases are appealed to a higher court, and some of those appeals end with the original decision reversed.

A local newspaper collected, for every judge, how many cases they disposed of, how many were appealed and how many were reversed. Its reasoning: appeals and reversals often follow a judge's mistakes, so these counts say something about performance. You've been brought in to turn the counts into probabilities and a defensible ranking. (Two judges served in more than one court during the period.)

What the manager wants

  1. For each of the three courts, the probability that a case is appealed and the probability that it is reversed.
  2. For each judge, the probability that a case is appealed.
  3. For each judge, the probability that a case is reversed.
  4. For each judge, the probability of reversal given that the case was appealed.
  5. A ranking of judges within each court, with the criterion you chose and why.

Data you need

Table 4.8 in the textbook (data file Judge on the textbook companion site, or ask your instructor). One row per judge per court with the variables court, judge, total cases disposed, appealed cases and reversed cases, plus court totals.

How to crack it

  1. Name the eventsLet A = “the case is appealed” and R = “the case is reversed”. A case can only be reversed if it was appealed, so R sits inside A and P(A∩R)=P(R)P(A \cap R) = P(R)P(A∩R)=P(R). That makes task 4 easy: P(R∣A)=P(R)/P(A)P(R \mid A) = P(R)/P(A)P(R∣A)=P(R)/P(A), which is simply reversed ÷ appealed.
  2. Court-level probabilities (task 1)For each court, divide by its total cases disposed: P(A) = appealed ÷ disposed, P(R) = reversed ÷ disposed. Our worked numbers are below; check yours against them.
  3. Judge-level columns in Excel (tasks 2–4)With disposed in column C, appealed in D and reversed in E, add three columns: F =D2/C2 for P(A), G =E2/C2 for P(R) and H =E2/D2 for P(R | A). Format to 4 decimals and fill down. Keep each court in its own block of rows.
  4. Choose a ranking criterion (task 5)This is the judgement part, and the newspaper's report is only as fair as this choice. Compare the options below before you rank.
  5. Check for small numbersAny rate built on a handful of appeals can swing wildly. Flag those judges instead of ranking them at face value.
  6. Rank and write it upRank within each court on the P(R) column with =RANK.EQ(G2,G$2:G$17,1) (the 1 means smallest = rank 1 = best; adjust the range to each court's rows). Report the top and bottom few with their rates, not just rank numbers.

What a strong managerial report includes
  • A one-paragraph summary an editor can read: which court has the highest appeal and reversal rates, and the headline ranking.
  • The court table (P(appeal), P(reversal), P(reversal | appeal)) with a sentence on why courts differ.
  • A full judge table for each court with the three probabilities to 4 decimals and the rank, sorted by your criterion.
  • Your criterion stated in plain words (“reversed cases per case disposed”) and the reason you preferred it.
  • A bar chart of P(reversal) by judge within each court, so the spread is visible at a glance.
  • Caveats: different case mixes even within a court, small appeal counts for some judges, the two judges who moved courts, and the fact that appeals depend on who can afford them.
  • A fair recommendation: what the paper can responsibly say (“these judges have notably higher reversal rates”) and what it can't (“these judges are bad”).

Anderson et al. (12e) · Ch. 6 · Case Problem · p. 294 · Syllabus Unit 3 case

Specialty Toys

How many talking teddy bears should a toy retailer order, once, months before anyone knows whether the toy will be a hit?

The situation

Specialty Toys sells new and unusual children's toys and likes to launch them in October, just before the holiday gift season. The catch: to have stock in October, it must place a single order with its manufacturer in June or July. There's no second chance. Order too few and a hit toy sells out, with sales lost. Order too many and the leftovers are dumped in clearance sales.

This year's candidate is Weather Teddy, a talking bear with a built-in barometer that “predicts” the weather when a child presses its hand. Managers suggested order quantities of 15,000, 18,000, 24,000 and 28,000 units, which tells you how much they disagree. The bear sells for $24, costs $16, and any unsold stock after the holidays goes for $5. The senior forecaster expects demand of 20,000 units and puts a 0.95 probability on demand landing between 10,000 and 30,000.

What the manager wants

  1. Use the forecaster's statement to set up a normal distribution for demand: its mean and standard deviation, with a sketch.
  2. The probability of a stock-out for each of the four suggested order quantities.
  3. The profit for each suggested order quantity under three sales scenarios: worst case 10,000 units, most likely 20,000, best case 30,000.
  4. The order quantity that gives a 70% chance of meeting demand (30% chance of a stock-out), and its profit under the three scenarios.
  5. Your own recommended order quantity, its profit projections and your reasons.

Data you need

No data file. Everything comes from the case text: selling price, unit cost, salvage price, the forecaster's mean and 95% range, and the four suggested quantities.

How to crack it

  1. Turn the forecaster's sentence into μ and σ (task 1)95% of a normal curve lies within 1.96σ of the mean. So μ is the midpoint of the range and the half-width, 10,000 units, equals 1.96σ.
  2. Sketch the demand curve (task 1)A bell centred at 20,000 with 10,000 and 30,000 marked as the 95% band. Draw the four order quantities on it.
  3. Stock-out probability for each order (task 2)A stock-out means demand D exceeds the order Q: P(D>Q)=1−P(z≤(Q−μ)/σ)P(D \gt Q) = 1 - P(z \le (Q - \mu)/\sigma)P(D>Q)=1−P(z≤(Q−μ)/σ).
  4. Profit at three sales levels (task 3)Each unit sold earns $24 − $16 = $8; each unit left over loses $16 − $5 = $11. Apply that to every order and sales pair.
  5. The 70% order (task 4)Find the Q with P(D≤Q)=0.70P(D \le Q) = 0.70P(D≤Q)=0.70 using Q=μ+zσQ = \mu + z\sigmaQ=μ+zσ, then its three profits.
  6. Recommend (task 5)Weigh the $8 vs $11 asymmetry, the worst-case loss and the stock-out risk, then commit to a number.
  7. Build it in ExcelOne small sheet answers every task (shown at the end).

What the marker looks for
  • Events defined in words or letters. The class solutions always start with “S1 = successful, B = request received…”. Copy that habit.
  • The right denominator. Joint and marginal: grand total. Conditional: the total of the group you were “given”.
  • No double counting in OR. Subtract the shared cell once.
