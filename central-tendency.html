Central tendency

Mean, median and mode: which single number can honestly speak for a whole column of business data, and how to get it by hand and in Excel.

The manager's question

The manager's question

You run growth for a small skincare brand that sells online from Dehradun. Yesterday's dashboard says average order value: ₹1,989. Your marketing head wants free delivery on orders above ₹1,900, “just under what a typical customer spends”.

You pull the 15 orders behind that number. Is ₹1,989 really what a typical customer spends?

So the dashboard isn't lying. It is just answering a different question. The mean tells you revenue per order. It does not tell you what a typical customer does. That gap is what this page is about.

An “average” is a one-number summary of a whole column. Pick the one that fits the shape of the data and the decision in front of you.

Level 1 · Basics

The idea in plain words

A measure of central tendency is one value that tells you where the “middle” of your data sits. There are three everyday answers: the mean, the median and the mode.

Think of it like

Mean is the balance point of a see-saw loaded with your data. Median is the person standing in the middle of a queue sorted by height. Mode is the dish the canteen sells most of.

Mean (average)
Add all the values, divide by how many there are. Written xˉ\bar{x}xˉ for a sample, μ\muμ for a whole population.
Median
The middle value after sorting. Half the data sit below it, half above.
Mode
The value (or category) that occurs most often. A dataset can have one, several or none.
Outlier
A value far away from the rest, like one ₹9,900 bulk order among ₹1,000 orders.
Skewed data
Data with a long tail on one side. Salaries, house prices and order values usually have a long right tail.
Percentile
The value below which a given percentage of the data falls. The median is the 50th percentile.

Look at Figure 2 for a second. The mean has to sit at 9.54, far from the four staff, because the founder is so far out on the right. That is the mean's one big weakness: it gets pulled towards extreme values. The median doesn't care how far out the founder is. Make his salary ₹28 lakh or ₹2.8 crore, the median stays 5.1.

The mode

No formula, just counting. The mode is the most frequent value. It is the only average that works for categories like “preferred payment method” or a rating word such as “Very Good”. For numbers that rarely repeat (prices, revenues) the mode is often useless: the 30 Dow share prices in class have five modes.

What skew does to the three

In Figure 3 the mean is $26 billion above the median. Only 16 of the 50 firms have revenue above the mean. That gap is your skew alarm: when the mean and median part company, the data are skewed, and the tail is on the side of the mean.

Exam tip

Quartiles can differ slightly depending on the rule, and examiners know it. In the pen-and-paper exam, write the rule you used (“i=(p/100)ni = (p/100)ni=(p/100)n, round up”) and you are safe. In Excel, the class slides use QUARTILE.EXC, so that is what your lab answers should match.

Text categories trip up MODE.SNGL

MODE.SNGL only looks at numbers, so on the AirSurvey ratings (“Excellent”, “Very Good”…) it returns #N/A. For categories, build a frequency table with =COUNTIF(A2:A51,"Very Good") or a PivotTable (Rating in Rows, Count of Rating in Values), and read off the biggest count. One-cell version: =INDEX(A2:A51,MODE.SNGL(MATCH(A2:A51,A2:A51,0))) returns “Very Good” (older Excel needs Ctrl+Shift+Enter).

Level 3 · Application

Where managers use it

HR

Pay benchmarking

Salary surveys report the median (and quartiles) per grade. A few senior packages would push a mean above what most people earn.

E-commerce

Average order value

For revenue planning the mean is right: revenue = mean order value × number of orders. For “what does a typical buyer spend?” use the median.

Real estate

Property prices

Price per sq ft in a city is right-skewed by luxury projects. Property reports usually quote the median to describe the typical flat.

Marketing / CX

Ratings and surveys

For “Excellent … Poor” answers report the mode, the median category and the share in the top two boxes, not a decimal “4.16 out of 5”.

Procurement

Average cost of stock

Raw material bought in lots at different prices: the weighted mean (weights = quantity) is the true cost per unit.

Finance

Average yearly return

Returns compound, so the geometric mean (CAGR) is the honest yearly figure. The simple mean overstates it (see Pro).

Read Figure 6 like a manager. The airline's mode is “Very Good” (46%) and 86% of passengers chose one of the top two boxes. The FedBank mode is “Fair” (52%), but 36% said Bad or Terrible against only 12% Good or Excellent. Same tool, very different stories.

Level 4 · Pro

Nuance, pitfalls and depth

Geometric mean for growth rates Core

Suppose a mutual fund (hypothetical) returns +24%, −12%, +18%, +6% and −4% over five years. The simple average is (24−12+18+6−4)/5=6.4%(24 - 12 + 18 + 6 - 4)/5 = 6.4\%(24−12+18+6−4)/5=6.4% a year. But ₹1 lakh invested at the start actually ends at ₹1,31,028, and 6.4% compounded would have given ₹1,36,367. Returns multiply; they don't add. So average them by multiplying.

Trimmed mean Light coverage

A compromise: chop a small percentage off each end, then take the mean of what remains. Diving competitions score this way: the highest and lowest judges' marks are dropped. =TRIMMEAN(range, 0.2) removes 20% of the values in total (10% from each end; Excel rounds the number removed down to an even count). LargeCorp: mean 96.52, 20% trimmed mean 77.63, median 70.5. The trimmed mean keeps most of the data but loses the giants' pull.

Harmonic mean and your SIP Beyond the syllabus

Invest a fixed ₹10,000 a month for three months at NAVs of ₹50, ₹40 and ₹50. You buy 200 + 250 + 200 = 650 units for ₹30,000, so your average cost is ₹46.15 per unit, not the simple average NAV of ₹46.67. That ₹46.15 is the harmonic mean, 3/(150+140+150)3 / (\tfrac{1}{50} + \tfrac{1}{40} + \tfrac{1}{50})3/(501​+401​+501​). It's the right average whenever you spend a fixed amount at varying prices (or drive a fixed distance at varying speeds). Excel: =HARMEAN().

Means of ratings: handle with care

Code AirSurvey as Poor = 1 … Excellent = 5 and the mean is 4.16. It looks precise but it assumes the gap between “Fair” and “Good” equals the gap between “Very Good” and “Excellent”. Nobody measured that. Report “86% rated the flight Very Good or Excellent; mode Very Good”. If your boss insists on a mean score, show the distribution next to it.

Interview-style insight

Why do two “averages” exist at all? If you must guess every value in a dataset with one number, the median keeps your total distance from the data as small as possible, and the mean keeps your total squared distance as small as possible. Squaring punishes big misses hard, so the mean moves towards outliers to avoid missing them badly. That one sentence explains everything on this page.

How a consultant would present it

“The typical order is ₹1,150 (median). The reported average of ₹1,989 is lifted by three bulk orders that made up 57% of yesterday's revenue. If we want most customers to qualify for free delivery, set the bar around the 25th percentile, ₹910, not near the mean.”

Lead with the typical value, name the distortion, then make the decision. One number on its own is never the story.

Next steps: the mean is only half a summary until you know how spread out the data are (Dispersion), and the mean–median gap is formalised by the skewness coefficient (Shape). Box plots draw the median and quartiles in one picture (Visualization).

Quick check

House prices in Gurugram are strongly right-skewed. A property portal wants one number for the “typical” flat price. What should it report?

  1. The mean, because it uses every listing
  2. The mode, because it is the most common price
  3. The median
  4. The midpoint between the cheapest and dearest flat
A few luxury listings pull the mean up; the median is the price of the middle flat and is hardly affected by them. Prices rarely repeat exactly, so the mode is unreliable, and the mid-range is decided by the two most extreme listings.

A shop buys 30 kg of dal at ₹40/kg and 70 kg at ₹50/kg. What is its average cost per kg?

  1. ₹47
  2. ₹45
  3. ₹46
  4. ₹48
Weighted mean: (30×40+70×50)/(30+70)=4,700/100=47(30 \times 40 + 70 \times 50)/(30 + 70) = 4{,}700/100 = 47(30×40+70×50)/(30+70)=4,700/100=47. ₹45 is the simple mean of the two prices, which ignores that more dal was bought at ₹50.

Grouped data: 0–10 (f = 4), 10–20 (f = 10), 20–30 (f = 6). What is the estimated mean?

  3. 17
  4. 16

For LargeCorp revenues, mean = 96.52 and median = 70.5. Which statement is correct?

  1. The data are left-skewed, so the mean understates the typical firm
  2. The data are right-skewed; a few very large firms pull the mean above the typical firm
  3. The data are symmetric because both numbers are positive
  4. Half of the firms earn more than $96.52 billion
Mean well above median = long right tail (skewness 3.14). Half the firms earn more than the median (70.5), not the mean; in fact only 16 of 50 are above the mean.

A stock rises 50% in year 1 and falls 50% in year 2. What is its average annual return?

  1. 0%, the mean of +50% and −50%
  2. −25%
  3. About −13.4%
  4. +25%
Geometric mean: 1.5×0.5−1=0.75−1=−0.134\sqrt{1.5 \times 0.5} - 1 = \sqrt{0.75} - 1 = -0.1341.5×0.5​−1=0.75​−1=−0.134. ₹100 becomes ₹150 then ₹75, a real loss, which the arithmetic mean of 0% hides. −25% is the total two-year change, not the yearly rate.

One-glance summary

Central tendency in 60 seconds

  • Mean xˉ=∑x/n\bar{x} = \sum x / nxˉ=∑x/n (sample), μ=∑x/N\mu = \sum x / Nμ=∑x/N (population). Balance point; uses every value; pulled by outliers. =AVERAGE()
  • Median: sort, take position (n+1)/2(n+1)/2(n+1)/2. Middle value; resistant to outliers. =MEDIAN()
  • Mode: most frequent value; the only one for categories. =MODE.SNGL(), =MODE.MULT(), COUNTIF/PivotTable for text.
  • Weighted mean ∑wx/∑w\sum wx / \sum w∑wx/∑w. =SUMPRODUCT(w,x)/SUM(w)
  • Grouped mean ∑fM/∑f\sum fM / \sum f∑fM/∑f with class midpoints MMM.
  • Percentile (textbook): i=(p/100)ni = (p/100)ni=(p/100)n; not whole → round up; whole → average iii and i+1i+1i+1. Excel: PERCENTILE.EXC, QUARTILE.EXC.
