The inference leap: from a sample to the whole market

The manager's question

Your snack brand has 3.2 lakh customers on its app. The research team can only phone 400 of them. Of those 400, 248 (62%) say they would buy a new millet-chips range. Can you walk into the board meeting and say "most of our customers want it"? How sure are you, and what happens if you're wrong?

Inference is a disciplined leap: you measure a few, you speak about all, and you always attach an honest error band to what you say.

Descriptive statistics describe the data you have. Inferential statistics use that data to make claims about the data you don't have: every customer, every bulb in the shipment, every future order. Nobody can survey 3.2 lakh people. So you take a sample, and then you make a careful leap back to the population.

Why is the leap safe at all? Because sample results wobble in a predictable way. Take many random samples and their means pile up in a bell shape around the true mean, with a spread we can calculate (the standard error). Once you know how much a sample result can wobble, you can say two useful things: "the true value is probably in this range" (an interval estimate) and "this claim doesn't fit the data" (a hypothesis test).

Population
Everyone (or everything) you want to talk about: all customers, all bags in the batch.
Parameter
A number describing the population, like the true mean μ\muμ or true share ppp. Usually unknown.
Sample · statistic
The nnn items you actually measured, and a number computed from them, like xˉ\bar{x}xˉ, sss or p^\hat{p}p^​.
Sampling distribution
How a statistic (say xˉ\bar{x}xˉ) would vary if you repeated the sampling many times.
Standard error (SE)
The standard deviation of that sampling distribution: the typical size of the "wobble". For a mean, σ/n\sigma/\sqrt{n}σ/n​.
Significance level α\alphaα
The false-alarm risk you agree to before testing, usually 5%.

Guide

Which interval or test? A decision flowchart

Almost every problem in this pillar is settled by three questions: what kind of variable is it, how many groups are you comparing, and do you know the population standard deviation σ?

Answer them in order and you land on the right tool. Then ask one last thing: are you estimating a value (build an interval) or checking a claim (run a test)? Both use the same standard error, so once you have one you are halfway to the other.

The z-or-t trap

"σ known" is about the population standard deviation, not about sample size. If the question gives you a standard deviation calculated from the sample (sss), use t, even when n=100n = 100n=100. That's how the class solution to problem 8.15 does it (t = 1.9842 with df = 99). With a big sample t and z are close anyway (1.984 vs 1.960), so the habit costs nothing and is always right.

The "n large?" check

For a mean: with n≥30n \ge 30n≥30 the Central Limit Theorem makes xˉ\bar{x}xˉ roughly normal whatever the shape of the data. With a smaller sample you must be willing to assume the population itself is roughly normal (look at a dot plot or histogram for strong skew or outliers). For a proportion: you need np≥5np \ge 5np≥5 and n(1−p)≥5n(1-p) \ge 5n(1−p)≥5.

Interval and test: two views of one curve

This is the picture behind the whole pillar. Under H0H_0H0​, the sample mean xˉ\bar{x}xˉ follows a normal curve centred at the claimed value. A test asks "is my xˉ\bar{x}xˉ out in the tails?" An interval asks "which claimed values would my xˉ\bar{x}xˉ be comfortable with?" Same curve, same 1.96, so they always agree.

Name the sampling method in each situation, and give one risk the manager should watch for.

  1. A private bank in Dehradun wants satisfaction scores from its salaried, self-employed, pensioner and student account holders. It picks a random 2% from each group.
  2. A highway toll operator surveys every 25th vehicle through the booth, starting from a vehicle chosen at random among the first 25.
  3. A supermarket chain with 140 stores picks 8 stores at random and audits every billing counter in those 8.
  4. A fintech app emails a survey to all 6 lakh users and analyses whoever replies.
Worked solution
(a)
Stratified random sampling (proportional: 2% from every stratum). Risk: the strata must be defined so each customer falls in exactly one; small groups may need oversampling and weighting if you want to compare them.
(b)
Systematic sampling, k=25k = 25k=25 with a random start. Risk: periodicity. If traffic has a pattern that repeats every 25 vehicles (convoys of trucks, say), the sample is biased.
(c)
Cluster sampling: stores are the clusters and everything inside them is measured. Risk: counters within a store are alike, so 8 clusters carry less information than the same number of counters picked at random; the standard error is bigger.
(d)
Not a probability sample: a voluntary-response (self-selected) sample. Risk: non-response bias. Very happy or very angry users reply more, and no honest margin of error can be attached.
Result
(a) stratified · (b) systematic · (c) cluster · (d) voluntary response

In Excel: add =RAND() next to each name, sort by it and take the first k (simple random, or within each stratum); for (b) pick the start with =RANDBETWEEN(1,25).

  2. "With 40,000 customers in the sample, the new app layout raised mean order value by ₹3 (p < 0.001). This is a big win."

Worked solution
(a)
The tail has to be fixed before seeing the data. Switching afterwards doubles the real false-alarm risk in that direction. Report the two-tailed p = 0.08: not significant at 5%.
(b)
Statistically significant isn't the same as important. A huge sample detects tiny effects: ₹3 on, say, a ₹1,000 order is 0.3%. Compare the lift (with its confidence interval) against what the change costs.
(c)
Failing to reject H0H_0H0​ doesn't prove it. We simply lack evidence of a difference, and a small or noisy sample may have low power. A confidence interval for the difference shows how big a gap is still plausible.
(d)

Where the marks usually go (and where they leak)
  • Step 1 is about the population: μ\muμ, never xˉ\bar{x}xˉ. The tail must match the wording ("less often" → <).
  • Step 3: say why it's t (σ unknown). One phrase is enough.
  • Step 4: the df and the sign of the critical value. A left-tailed test needs −1.690, not +1.690.

  • Step 6: "reject H0H_0H0​" alone is half an answer. The business sentence is the other half.

  • Confidence interval: "We are 95% confident that the mean [variable] of all [population] lies between [L] and [U] [units]."
