The manager's question

The manager's question

Your plant near Haridwar packs 10 kg bags of atta. A weights-and-measures inspector pulls 36 bags off the line and finds they average 9.94 kg. Your production head shrugs: “Sixty grams on a ten-kilo bag? That’s just noise. The machine is fine.” The inspector isn’t convinced. One of them is about to cost the company money. Who is right, and how sure can either of them be?

A hypothesis test asks one blunt question: if nothing were wrong, how surprising would this sample be? If the answer is “very surprising”, we stop believing that nothing is wrong.

That’s the whole idea. Everything else on this page (the symbols, the tables, the Excel functions) is just a careful way of measuring “surprising”. You’ll settle the atta argument in six written steps in Worked example 1, and by the end you’ll be able to do the same for any claim about a mean or a proportion, by hand in the exam and in Excel at work.

Why this page matters for the exam

Module 3 is examined through the z-test, t-test and chi-square test. Almost every hypothesis-test question has the same skeleton: write H₀ and H₁, pick α, compute z or t, compare with a table value or a p-value, and conclude in words. Learn the skeleton once and you can answer all of them.

Level 1 · Basics

The idea in plain words

A hypothesis test is a structured way of deciding whether sample evidence is strong enough to overturn a default belief about a population.

Every test starts with two competing statements about a population number, such as the mean fill weight μ\muμ or the share of returned orders ppp. One statement is the default, the “nothing has changed, the claim is fine” story. The other is what someone suspects or wants to prove. We collect a sample and ask whether it is too far from the default to blame on chance.

Think of it like a courtroom

An Indian court starts from “innocent until proven guilty”. The judge doesn’t ask the accused to prove innocence. The prosecution has to bring evidence strong enough to convict. If it can’t, the verdict is “not guilty”, which is not the same as “innocent”. A hypothesis test works the same way. The null hypothesis is the presumption of innocence; the sample is the evidence; α is how much doubt we tolerate before we convict.

The vocabulary you need

Null hypothesis, H₀
The default claim about a population parameter: the status quo, the label claim, “no change”, “no effect”. It always contains the equality (=, ≤ or ≥).
Alternative hypothesis, H₁
What we accept if the evidence against H₀ is strong. It is the claim that needs proof. Anderson and your class slides write it as Hₐ; Levine writes H₁. Same thing.
Test statistic
One number (z or t) that measures how many standard errors the sample result sits away from what H₀ predicts.
Significance level, α
The risk you accept of rejecting a true H₀. Usually 0.05 (5%). Chosen before you look at the data.
Critical value
The cut-off from the z- or t-table. Beyond it lies the rejection region, the zone of “too surprising to be chance”.
p-value
If H₀ were true, the probability of getting a sample result at least as extreme as yours. Small p = strong evidence against H₀.
One- or two-tailed
One-tailed: H₁ points one way (< or >). Two-tailed: H₁ says “different” (≠), so surprises on either side count.
Reject / do not reject
The only two verdicts. We never “accept” H₀; we just say the evidence wasn’t strong enough to reject it.

Who goes into H₀, and who goes into H₁?

This is where most marks are lost, so here are the three rules your instructor keeps repeating:

  1. The equality always sits in H₀. Any of =, ≤, ≥ belongs to the null. H₁ only ever uses <, > or ≠.
  2. The claim that needs evidence goes in H₁. If someone wants to show something (a new method works, returns have gone up), that is H₁.
  3. The status quo is H₀. Last year’s figure, the label on the pack, the advertised promise: give it the benefit of the doubt until the data say otherwise.

Anderson et al. (Ch. 9.1) describe three common situations. Recognise which one you are in and the hypotheses almost write themselves:

Form 1

Research hypothesis

You hope to show something new is better. The research claim is H₁; H₀ says “no improvement”.

Class Q6: a coaching centre claims a new method raises the mean score above 62. H0 ⁣:μ≤62H_0\colon \mu \le 62H0​:μ≤62, H1 ⁣:μ>62H_1\colon \mu \gt 62H1​:μ>62.

Form 2

Testing a claim

A company states something about its product. Their claim is the assumption we challenge, so it is H₀; the suspicion is H₁.

Class Q4: a power bank “lasts at least 18 hours”. H0 ⁣:μ≥18H_0\colon \mu \ge 18H0​:μ≥18, H1 ⁣:μ<18H_1\colon \mu \lt 18H1​:μ<18.

Form 3

Testing for compliance

A process must hit a target and being off in either direction is a problem. H₀ says “on target”; H₁ says “off target” (≠).

Class Q1: milk pouches labelled 500 ml. H0 ⁣:μ=500H_0\colon \mu = 500H0​:μ=500, H1 ⁣:μ≠500H_1\colon \mu \ne 500H1​:μ=500.

Why must the equality live in H₀?

Because the test needs one exact number to work with. To ask “how surprising is my sample if H₀ is true?”, we must draw the sampling distribution centred on a specific value, μ₀. “μ ≥ 18” gives us that boundary value, 18. “μ < 18” doesn’t give us any single value to centre on. So the test is always run at the boundary, μ = μ₀, and the equality has to be on the null’s side.

The tricky case (class Q9)

A phone maker wants to advertise that fewer than 2% of its phones need repair. Now the company itself is making the claim, and it wants proof before the ad goes live. “Fewer than” has no equality, so the claim goes in H₁: H0 ⁣:p≥0.02H_0\colon p \ge 0.02H0​:p≥0.02, H1 ⁣:p<0.02H_1\colon p \lt 0.02H1​:p<0.02. Rule 1 beats everything: follow the equality.

Numbers never decide the tail

The sample size, α = 0.05, even the sample result: none of them changes how you write H₀ and H₁. Only the wording of the question does. Class Q3 hands you “α = 0.05” as a distraction; the hypotheses come purely from “no more than 5%” and “running higher”.

Two ways to be wrong

A verdict can be wrong in two different ways, exactly like a trial. Convicting an innocent person is a Type I error (its probability is α, which is why we keep α small). Letting a guilty person walk is a Type II error (probability β). You can’t drive both to zero with a fixed sample size; shrinking one grows the other. The full story, with the trade-off explorer, is on the Errors & p-value page.

Level 2 · Theory

Six steps, three formulas

Every test in this course, from a z-test on refunds to a chi-square on survey tables, follows the same six steps. Write them in this order on your answer sheet and you’ll rarely lose method marks.

  1. State H₀ and H₁. Name the parameter (μ for a mean, p for a proportion), put the equality in H₀, and note the tail.
  2. Choose the significance level α. The question usually gives it. If not, use 0.05.
  3. Choose the test statistic. σ known → z. σ unknown (you only have the sample s) → t with n − 1 degrees of freedom. A proportion → z for p. Write the formula.
  4. Write the rejection rule. Look up the critical value for your α and tail, then write e.g. “Reject H₀ if z ≤ −1.645”. (Or: “Reject H₀ if p-value ≤ α”.)
  5. Compute. Standard error first, then the test statistic, then (if asked) the p-value.
  6. Decide and conclude. Reject or do not reject H₀, then one sentence in plain business language that a manager could act on.

The t-distribution and degrees of freedom

When σ is unknown we plug in the sample’s s. That adds extra uncertainty (s itself wobbles from sample to sample), so the z curve is replaced by a t curve with fatter tails. How fat depends on the degrees of freedom, df = n − 1. Small samples mean fat tails and bigger cut-offs. With 30 or more observations the gap is small (the 5% two-tailed cut-off is 2.045 at df = 29 against 1.960 for z), and it keeps shrinking as n grows.

Reading the class t-table (shown below and on the tables page): go to the row for your df, then the column for your one-tail area. For a two-tailed test use the α/2 column (the bottom row of the table shows the two-tail α). For a left-tailed test put a minus sign in front of the table value. Example: df = 35, right-tailed, α = 0.05 → 1.690.

To get a p-value from the table, find where |t| falls in its row. Class problem 31 has t = 2.202 with df = 35: the row shows 2.030 (area 0.025) and 2.438 (area 0.01), so the one-tail p-value is between 0.01 and 0.025. That’s all the table can tell you, and it’s enough to decide at α = 0.05. Excel gives the exact value, 0.0172.

Two routes to the same decision

There are two ways to finish a test. They always agree, so pick whichever the question asks for (or show both, if it says “repeat using the critical value approach”).

Critical-value approach

Convert α into a cut-off on the z/t scale. Reject H₀ if the test statistic falls beyond it.

Easy with printed tables. Gives a yes/no answer only.

same verdict

Convert the test statistic into a tail area, the p-value. Reject H₀ if p ≤ α.

Tests and confidence intervals tell the same story

A two-tailed test at level α and a (1 − α) confidence interval are two views of the same calculation (see Estimation). The rule is simple: if μ₀ lies outside the 95% CI, a two-tailed test at α = 0.05 rejects H₀; if μ₀ is inside, it does not. Two class problems show both outcomes:

Which test do I use?

Before any calculation, answer the questions in this chart. Five questions get you to the right test every time. (The boxes for chi-square and ANOVA are links.)

The same chart as a checklist (easier on a phone)
  1. Two categorical variables in a cross-table? → chi-square test of independence.
  2. Yes/no data, one sample against a claimed share p₀? → z-test for a proportion. Two samples → two-proportion z-test (or chi-square).
  3. Numeric data, three or more group means? → ANOVA.
  4. Numeric data, two groups? Same units measured twice → paired t-test. Two separate groups → two-sample t-test.
  5. Numeric data, one group against a target μ₀? σ given → z-test for μ. Only the sample s → t-test with df = n − 1.
  6. Small sample, clearly skewed? → Mann–Whitney U (two groups) or Wilcoxon signed-rank (paired).

Worked example 1 · z-test for a mean

Are the atta bags underfilled?

That’s why the tail comes from the question, never from the data.

Level 3 · Application

Where managers use it

Any time someone says “our average is…”, “at least…”, “more than…” or “it has improved”, there is a hypothesis test waiting to happen.

Operations · quality

Fill-weight compliance

Milk pouches must average 500 ml. Too little invites a penalty, too much gives product away. A two-tailed z-test (H₁: μ ≠ 500) on each shift’s sample tells the supervisor when to stop and adjust the filler.

Digital marketing

Did the new ad lift click-through?

Old CTR 3%. Suppose a new video ad gets 178 clicks in 5,000 impressions (3.56%). Right-tailed z for p: z = 2.32, p = 0.010. The lift is real at α = 0.05, so shift budget to the new creative.

Finance

Is the fund beating its benchmark?

A t-test of a fund’s mean monthly return against the benchmark’s, or a two-tailed test like class problem 18 (mid-cap funds vs 4.1%), separates skill from a lucky year.

HR · L&D

Did the training work?

Measure the same salespeople before and after a programme and run a paired t-test. Measuring the same people twice removes the person-to-person noise (see the Pro section for why that matters so much).

Banking

Is the service promise true?

“80% of personal loans are processed within 48 hours.” A left-tailed z-test for p (class Q8) on 150 recent files tells the branch manager whether the complaints reflect a real slide.

Healthcare · hospitality

Has the new process cut waiting time?

Hospital triage (class Q7: was 45 minutes) or hotel check-in: a left-tailed t-test on a sample of waits checks the claimed improvement before it goes into the annual report.

Indian context

Packaged goods sold in India must carry a declared net quantity under the Legal Metrology (Packaged Commodities) Rules, 2011, and enforcement officers check samples from the market. Sensible FMCG plants don’t wait for the inspector: their QA teams weigh samples every shift and run exactly the left-tailed test of Worked example 1, so underfilling is caught by the company, not by the regulator.

Statistically significant is not the same as important Exam core

“Significant” in statistics means only “unlikely to be chance”. It says nothing about whether the effect is big enough to matter. With a huge sample, trivial differences become significant; with a tiny sample, important differences can fail to reach significance.

The habit to build: always report the size of the effect (the difference in rupees, minutes or percentage points, ideally with a confidence interval) next to the p-value. A manager can act on “returns are about 2 points above the claim”; she can’t act on “p = 0.02”.

One-tailed tests are not a trick to get significance

Take z = 1.80. Two-tailed, p = 0.072: not significant at 0.05. One-tailed, p = 0.036: significant. It is tempting to look at the data and then pick the tail that “works”. Don’t. That quietly doubles your real α.

  • The tail comes from the business question, decided before the data. “Has the new ad increased CTR?” is honestly one-tailed. “Has the new ad changed CTR?” is two-tailed.
  • A one-tailed test is blind in the other direction. If you test H₁: CTR > 3% and the new ad is actually much worse, a right-tailed test can never flag it.
  • When in doubt, use two tails. Reviewers, auditors and examiners all treat it as the safer default.
Related trap: running many tests

Test 20 things at α = 0.05 when nothing is really going on, and the chance that at least one comes out “significant” is 1−0.9520=0.641 - 0.95^{20} = 0.641−0.9520=0.64. If a dashboard tests every product category every week, some red flags are pure noise. Treat a lone surprise as a reason to re-test, not as proof.

Interview-style questions

“What does a p-value of 0.03 actually mean?”

If the null hypothesis were true, we would see a result at least this extreme in about 3% of samples. It is not the probability that H₀ is true, and 1 − p is not the probability that our idea is right. It’s a measure of how surprising the data are under the “nothing is going on” story.

“Your test didn’t reject H₀. So the new process has no effect, right?”

Not quite. “Not rejected” means “not proven”, like a “not guilty” verdict. The effect may exist but be smaller than this sample can detect. Look at the confidence interval: if it is wide and includes both zero and a useful effect, the honest answer is “we need more data”. (This is a Type II error risk; see Errors & p-value.)

“Why not always use α = 0.01 to be safe?”

Because “safe” cuts both ways. A smaller α reduces false alarms (Type I) but, for the same sample size, raises the chance of missing a real effect (Type II). Choose α by asking which mistake is more expensive for the business: approving a useless drug, or shelving a profitable product?

Quick check

A courier promises that deliveries take no more than 48 hours on average. A consumer forum suspects they take longer. Which hypotheses are right?

  1. H0 ⁣:μ≥48H_0\colon \mu \ge 48H0​:μ≥48, H1 ⁣:μ<48H_1\colon \mu \lt 48H1​:μ<48
  2. H0 ⁣:μ≤48H_0\colon \mu \le 48H0​:μ≤48, H1 ⁣:μ>48H_1\colon \mu \gt 48H1​:μ>48
  3. H0 ⁣:μ=48H_0\colon \mu = 48H0​:μ=48, H1 ⁣:μ≠48H_1\colon \mu \ne 48H1​:μ=48
  4. H0 ⁣:μ>48H_0\colon \mu \gt 48H0​:μ>48, H1 ⁣:μ≤48H_1\colon \mu \le 48H1​:μ≤48
“No more than” is ≤, which contains the equality, so it is H₀ (the courier’s claim). The suspicion “longer” is H₁: μ > 48, a right-tailed test. Option d breaks the rule that equality lives in H₀.

H₀: μ = 50 vs H₁: μ ≠ 50, σ = 12, n = 36, x̄ = 53.6, α = 0.05. What do you get?

  1. z = 1.80; reject H₀ because 1.80 > 1.645
  2. z = 0.30; do not reject H₀
  3. z = 1.80; do not reject H₀ because 1.80 < 1.96
  4. z = 3.60; reject H₀
Standard error = 12/√36 = 2, so z = (53.6 − 50)/2 = 1.80. The test is two-tailed, so the cut-off is 1.96, not 1.645 (that’s the one-tailed trap in option a). Two-tailed p = 0.072 > 0.05: do not reject.

A test of a new checkout design gives p = 0.03. Which statement is correct?

  1. The probability that H₀ is true is 0.03.
  2. There is a 97% chance the new design is better.
  3. The chance of a Type II error is 3%.
  4. If H₀ were true, a result at least this extreme would turn up in about 3 of every 100 samples.
A p-value is computed assuming H₀ is true: it is the probability of data at least as extreme as ours under H₀. It is not the probability that H₀ (or H₁) is true, and it says nothing directly about β.

HR records the job-satisfaction score of the same 25 employees before and after a new flexible-hours policy. Which test fits?

  1. Two-sample t-test for independent groups
  2. Chi-square test of independence
  3. Paired t-test on the before/after differences
  4. z-test for a proportion
The same people are measured twice, so the data are paired. Work with each employee’s difference (after − before) and run a t-test on those 25 differences (df = 24).

You test H₁: μ < 120 with raw data in Excel. =Z.TEST(B2:B41,120,15) returns 0.9713. What is the p-value of your test?

  1. 0.0287
  2. 0.9713
  3. 0.0574
  4. 0.4857
Z.TEST always returns the upper-tail area P(Z ≥ z). For a left-tailed test you need the lower tail: 1 − 0.9713 = 0.0287 (here z = −1.90). So reject H₀ at α = 0.05. Option c doubles it, which would be the two-tailed p.

One-glance summary

Hypothesis tests on one screen

  • H₀ = status quo / the claim on the label, and it always holds the equality (=, ≤, ≥). H₁ (= Hₐ) = what needs evidence (<, >, ≠).
  • Tail = direction of H₁: < left, > right, ≠ two. Decided by the wording, never by the data or α.
  • Six steps: 1 H₀/H₁ · 2 α · 3 statistic · 4 rejection rule · 5 compute · 6 decide and conclude in business words.
  • Statistics: z=xˉ−μ0σ/nz = \dfrac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}z=σ/n​xˉ−μ0​​ (σ known) · t=xˉ−μ0s/nt = \dfrac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{s/\sqrt{n}}t=s/n​xˉ−μ0​​, df = n − 1 (σ unknown) · z=p^−p0p0(1−p0)/nz = \dfrac{\hat{p} - p_0}{\sqrt{p_0(1 - p_0)/n}}z=p0​(1−p0​)/n​p^​−p0​​ (proportion).
  • z cut-offs: one-tailed 1.282 / 1.645 / 2.326 and two-tailed 1.645 / 1.960 / 2.576 for α = 0.10 / 0.05 / 0.01.
  • Decision: reject H₀ if the statistic is in the rejection region ⇔ p-value ≤ α. Two-tailed at α ⇔ μ₀ outside the (1 − α) CI.
  • Excel: NORM.S.DIST, NORM.S.INV, T.DIST, T.DIST.RT, T.DIST.2T, T.INV, T.INV.2T; Z.TEST = upper-tail p from raw data; T.TEST and the ToolPak t-Test tools for two samples.
  • Say it: “At the 5% level there is [enough / not enough] evidence to conclude that [H₁ in plain words] (z = …, p = …).” Then give the size of the effect.
