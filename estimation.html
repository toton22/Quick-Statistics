Estimation

Turn one sample into a range you can defend: point estimates, margins of error and confidence intervals for a mean or a proportion, and how big a sample you need.

The manager's question

The manager's question

Your direct-to-consumer skincare brand (hypothetical) surveyed 25 randomly chosen repeat customers. Average annual spend: ₹4,850, standard deviation ₹1,200. The CFO wants to budget next year on “₹4,850 per customer”. The CMO shrugs: “With 25 people it could just as easily be ₹4,000.”

Who is right? How sure can you be about ₹4,850? And how many customers would you need to pin it down to within ±₹150?

An estimate without a margin of error is a guess dressed up as a fact. “Point estimate ± margin of error” is the honest answer.

Level 1 · Basics

The idea in plain words

Estimation means using a sample to put a number on something you can't measure directly: the average spend of all customers, the share of all invoices with errors.

There are two ways to answer. A point estimate is one best number: “₹4,850.” An interval estimate is a range with a stated level of confidence: “₹4,355 to ₹5,345, at 95% confidence.” The point estimate is almost certainly not exactly right. The interval tells you how wrong it might be.

Think of it like

Telling a friend when you'll arrive. “I'll be there at 6:07” sounds precise, and it is almost certainly wrong. “Between 6:00 and 6:15” is less precise, but you'd bet on it. A confidence interval is the second kind of answer, with the bet stated: “95% confident.” Want to be 99% sure? Widen the window to 5:55–6:20.

Estimator
The formula or rule you use, like “take the sample mean”.
Point estimate
The single number it produces from your sample: x̄ = ₹4,850.
Interval estimate (CI)
A range likely to contain the parameter: point estimate ± margin of error.
Margin of error (E)
Half the width of the interval: critical value × standard error.
Confidence level
How often the method captures the true value in repeated sampling: 90%, 95%, 99%. Written 1−α1-\alpha1−α.
Standard error (SE)
How much the point estimate wobbles from sample to sample: σ/√n or s/√n for a mean.
Critical value
How many standard errors to go out: z (normal table) or t (t-table) for your confidence level.
Degrees of freedom (df)
For a one-sample t interval, df = n − 1. It picks the row of the t-table.

What makes a good estimator?

Think of each sample as one throw of a dart at the true value. A good estimator has two habits.

  • Unbiased: on average it hits the bullseye. Its throws are centred on the true value, not pulled to one side. The sample mean x̄ is unbiased for μ. (That's also why s divides by n − 1: dividing by n would underestimate σ² on average.)
  • Consistent: the bigger the sample, the tighter the throws cluster around the bullseye. x̄ is consistent because its SE, σ/√n, shrinks as n grows.

Anderson also mentions efficiency: among unbiased estimators, prefer the one with the smallest spread.

What “95% confident” really means

The true mean μ is a fixed number. It doesn't move. What moves is the interval, because every new sample gives a new x̄ and a new s. “95% confidence” describes the method: if you repeated the survey again and again, about 95% of the intervals you built would contain μ.

Right

“We are 95% confident the mean annual spend of all repeat customers is between ₹4,355 and ₹5,345.” Meaning: this interval came from a method that captures μ 95% of the time.

vs

Wrong

“95% of customers spend between ₹4,355 and ₹5,345.” No: the interval is about the average, not individual customers. Individual spends vary far more (SD ₹1,200).

Three levers that change the width

Confidence level ↑

Wider. More confidence needs a bigger critical value (1.645 → 1.960 → 2.576).

Sample size n ↑

Narrower, but only by √n. Four times the sample halves the width.

Variability σ ↑

Wider. Noisy data need bigger samples. You can't control σ, only measure it.

How big a sample do you need?

The CI and the two-tailed test are two views of one idea

A 95% confidence interval contains exactly the values of μ that a two-tailed hypothesis test at α = 0.05 would not reject. So if a claimed value sits outside the interval, the claim is rejected. This is how class problem 8.10(b) is answered, and it is the bridge to Hypothesis testing.

Using 1.96 here would give E = 1.96 × 240 = ₹470.40, an interval about 5% too narrow. With n = 25 and only s, the t value 2.064 is required.

Two Excel traps

(1) CONFIDENCE.NORM and CONFIDENCE.T return the margin of error, not the interval: you still add and subtract it from x̄. (2) Their first argument is α (0.05), not the confidence level (0.95). Typing 0.95 gives a tiny, meaningless margin.

Explore it yourself

Average spend per member: true μ = $1,500, σ = $200 (the class problem 8.15 setting). Draw 100 samples and count how many intervals catch μ.
  • At 95%, draw several batches. The count of hits hovers around 95, rarely exactly 95.
  • Drop the confidence to 80%. The intervals get shorter and the misses jump to about 1 in 5.
  • Cut n from 100 to 25. The intervals double in length, but the hit rate stays near 95%. Confidence is about the method, width is about the data.
Class problem 8.15: n = 100 Prime shoppers, x̄ = $1,500, s = $200. Check the interval $1,460.32 to $1,539.68.

Level 3 · Application

Where managers use it

Marketing

Average basket size

“Mean bill ₹1,240 ± ₹60” lets the category manager forecast festive revenue as a range, not a single hopeful number.

Finance & audit

Estimating a total

Audit a sample of invoices, build a CI for the mean error per invoice, then multiply both limits by the number of invoices to get a range for the total misstatement.

Operations

Process targets

Is the mean fill, cycle time or delivery time on target? If the target sits outside the interval, adjust the process.

HR

Training hours and attrition

Estimate average training hours per employee, or the share planning to leave, from a pulse survey instead of a full census.

Banking

Product adoption

A CI for the proportion of customers likely to take a pre-approved loan sizes the campaign budget and the call-centre staffing.

Hospitality

Revenue per room

Average spend per guest from a sample of folios, with a margin, before a hotel reprices its packages.

Indian context: what “±3%” means in a poll

With a random sample of 1,000 voters and a share near 50%, the 95% margin of error is 1.960.25/1000=0.0311.96\sqrt{0.25/1000} = 0.0311.960.25/1000​=0.031, about ±3.1 points. But a pollster's state-level figure from only 250 of those respondents has a margin of about ±6.2 points. Whenever a TV channel splits a national poll by state, age or region, the margins balloon. Ask for the subgroup sample size before you trust the subgroup number.

Mini-case: how much is stuck in pending insurance claims?

A (hypothetical) Dehradun hospital has 1,800 pending cashless-insurance claims. The CFO wants to know the total amount tied up before negotiating a working-capital line. Checking every file would take weeks, so the accounts team pulls a random sample of 64 claims: mean ₹42,500, SD ₹15,800.

  1. Interval for the mean claimσ unknown → t with df = 63, t=1.998t = 1.998t=1.998. SE =15800/64=1975= 15800/\sqrt{64} = 1975=15800/64​=1975. E=1.998×1975=₹3,946E = 1.998 \times 1975 = ₹3{,}946E=1.998×1975=₹3,946. Mean: ₹38,554 to ₹46,446.
  2. Check the FPCn/N = 64/1,800 = 3.6%, under 5%, so no correction is needed.
  3. Scale up to the totalMultiply the point estimate and both limits by N = 1,800: point estimate ₹7.65 crore; 95% interval ₹6.94 crore to ₹8.36 crore.
  4. DecisionAsk the bank for a line sized to the upper limit (₹8.4 crore), not the point estimate. If the true total is near the top of the range, the hospital won't come up short.

Finite population correction for intervals

If the sample is more than 5% of the population, multiply the SE by (N−n)/(N−1)\sqrt{(N-n)/(N-1)}(N−n)/(N−1)​, as on the Sampling page.

Example: a two-wheeler maker (hypothetical) surveys 100 of its 500 dealers. Mean monthly sales ₹3.2 lakh, s = ₹0.9 lakh, 95%. SE =0.9/100=0.09= 0.9/\sqrt{100} = 0.09=0.9/100​=0.09 lakh; FPC =400/499=0.8953= \sqrt{400/499} = 0.8953=400/499​=0.8953; adjusted SE =0.0806= 0.0806=0.0806 lakh; E=1.984×0.0806=0.160E = 1.984 \times 0.0806 = 0.160E=1.984×0.0806=0.160 lakh. Interval: ₹3.04 lakh to ₹3.36 lakh (without the FPC the margin would be ±₹0.179 lakh).

Margins of error in polls and market research

  • The margin covers sampling error only. Non-response, wrong frames and people not telling the truth are all outside it. A poll can be “±3%” and still miss by 8 points.
  • The margin on a lead is bigger than the margin on each share. If Party A has 42% and Party B 38% in a poll of 1,000, the 95% margin on the 4-point gap is about ±5.5 points. That lead is not clear at all.
  • Multi-stage designs widen intervals. Clustered samples (whole villages or polling booths) behave like smaller samples, so professional agencies inflate the simple formula by a “design effect”.
  • Seats are not votes. In first-past-the-post elections a small error in vote share can swing many seats, which is why seat projections miss more often than vote-share estimates.

One-sided bounds Beyond the syllabus

Sometimes only one side matters: “we're 95% confident mean spend is at least X.” Put all 5% in one tail: xˉ−t0.05, 24 SE=4850−1.711×240=₹4,439\bar{x} - t_{0.05,\,24}\,\text{SE} = 4850 - 1.711 \times 240 = ₹4{,}439xˉ−t0.05,24​SE=4850−1.711×240=₹4,439. Use it for guarantees and minimum-revenue commitments.

Interview insight

“Is there a 95% probability that μ is in your interval?” The careful answer: μ is fixed, so it's either in or not. The 95% belongs to the procedure. Over many samples, 95% of intervals built this way contain μ. Saying “we are 95% confident” is the accepted shorthand.

How a consultant would present it

“Based on a random sample of 25 repeat customers, average annual spend is ₹4,850, and we're 95% confident the true average is between ₹4,355 and ₹5,345. To narrow that to ±₹150 we'd need about 246 customers, roughly a week of calls.” Point estimate, range, confidence, and what it would cost to do better.

Exam tip

Write five lines for every CI question: (1) which formula and why (z or t, and df), (2) the critical value from the table, (3) SE, (4) margin and interval, (5) one sentence of interpretation in the problem's own words. For sample-size questions, show the unrounded n and then round up.

Common mistakes

WrongRight
Using z = 1.96 when the problem gives a sample SD s.σ unknown → t with df = n − 1 (2.064 for n = 25).
Reading the t-table column for 0.05 at 95% confidence.Use the upper-tail area α/2 = 0.025 (two-tail α = 0.05).
“95% of customers spend between the limits.”The interval is for the population mean, not for individual values.
“There's a 95% chance μ is in this interval.”“We are 95% confident…”: the 95% is the success rate of the method.
Treating CONFIDENCE.T's output as the interval, or typing 0.95 as its first argument.It returns the margin E; the first argument is α = 0.05.
Rounding a sample size down (245.86 → 245).Always round up (246).
Using p* = 0.37 from a hunch to get a smaller n.No reliable prior? Use p* = 0.5: the safe, largest n.
Believing a bigger sample removes bias.It only narrows the interval around whatever the method is aiming at.

Quick check

A 95% confidence interval for mean delivery time is 31 to 35 minutes. Which statement is correct?

  1. 95% of deliveries take between 31 and 35 minutes
  2. There is a 95% chance the sample mean is between 31 and 35 minutes
  3. The method used captures the true mean in about 95% of samples, so we are 95% confident μ is between 31 and 35
  4. If we took another sample, its mean would fall between 31 and 35 with probability 0.95
The 95% describes the long-run success rate of the procedure. (a) confuses the mean with individual deliveries; (b) is meaningless because x̄ is always the centre; (d) is not what a CI promises.

n = 36, σ = 12 (known), x̄ = 50. What is the 95% confidence interval for μ?

  1. 46.08 to 53.92
  2. 49.35 to 50.65
  3. 26.48 to 73.52
  4. 46.71 to 53.29
SE = 12/√36 = 2; E = 1.96 × 2 = 3.92; 50 ± 3.92. (b) divides by n instead of √n, (c) forgets to divide at all, (d) uses the 90% value 1.645.

At the same confidence level, what must you do to cut the margin of error in half?

  1. Double the sample size
  2. Halve the confidence level
  3. Use t instead of z
  4. Quadruple the sample size
E is proportional to 1/√n. Four times n gives √4 = 2 times smaller E.

A random sample of 15 branch managers gives a mean and a sample SD; the population is roughly normal. For a 95% interval for the mean, which critical value do you use?

  1. z = 1.960
  2. t = 2.145 (df = 14)
  3. t = 2.131 (df = 15)
  4. z = 1.645
σ is unknown (only s), so use t with df = n − 1 = 14; row 14, column .025 of the t-table gives 2.145.

A bank wants to estimate the share of customers who use UPI weekly, within ±5 points at 95% confidence, with no idea of the likely share. Minimum sample size?

  1. 384
  2. 196
  3. 385
  4. 1,068
n = 1.96² × 0.5 × 0.5 / 0.05² = 384.16, rounded up to 385. (a) rounds down; (d) is the answer for ±3 points.

One-glance summary

Estimation in 60 seconds

  • Point estimates: x̄ for μ, s for σ, p̂ for p. Good estimators are unbiased (centred) and consistent (tighter as n grows).
  • Every CI: point estimate ± critical value × standard error.
  • σ known: xˉ±zα/2 σ/n\bar{x} \pm z_{\alpha/2}\,\sigma/\sqrt{n}xˉ±zα/2​σ/n​ · σ unknown: xˉ±tα/2, n−1 s/n\bar{x} \pm t_{\alpha/2,\,n-1}\,s/\sqrt{n}xˉ±tα/2,n−1​s/n​ · proportion: p^±zα/2p^(1−p^)/n\hat{p} \pm z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n}p^​±zα/2​p^​(1−p^​)/n​.
  • Critical values: z = 1.645 / 1.960 / 2.576 for 90 / 95 / 99%. t from the table: row df = n − 1, column α/2.
  • Width: wider with more confidence or more variability; narrower with larger n (by √n).
  • Sample size: n=(zσ/E)2n = (z\sigma/E)^2n=(zσ/E)2; n=z2p∗(1−p∗)/E2n = z^2 p^*(1-p^*)/E^2n=z2p∗(1−p∗)/E2 with p* = 0.5 if unknown; round up.
  • Link to tests: a value outside the 95% CI is rejected by a two-tailed test at α = 0.05.
  • Excel: =CONFIDENCE.T(0.05,s,n) · =CONFIDENCE.NORM(0.05,σ,n) · =T.INV.2T(0.05,df) · =NORM.S.INV(0.975) · ToolPak Descriptive Statistics “Confidence Level”.
  • Interpretation template: “We are 95% confident that the mean [variable] of all [population] is between [lower] and [upper] [units].”
