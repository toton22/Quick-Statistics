The manager's question

The manager's question

You head products at a (hypothetical) general insurer in Gurugram. The team wants to sell a one-year phone-screen cover for ₹799. A pilot suggests that in a year about 8 phones in 100 need a screen repair (₹4,500) and 2 in 100 are written off (₹15,000 replacement). Is ₹799 enough? And how can the company feel safe when any single policy could cost it ₹15,000?

A random variable turns “what might happen” into a number. Its expected value tells you what happens on average if the bet is repeated many times, and its standard deviation tells you how wild a single round can be.

Level 1 · Basics

The idea in plain words

A random variable is a number attached to each outcome of something uncertain.

On the probability basics page we talked about events: “the customer buys”, “the part is defective”. Managers usually need a number instead. How many customers bought? How much did we pay out? How long did the delivery take? The moment you write that number down, you have a random variable.

We call it random because, before the event happens, you don't know which value you'll get. We write the variable with a capital letter, XXX, and a particular value with a small letter, xxx. So “X=3X = 3X=3” reads “the number of items in the order turned out to be 3”.

Think of it like

The scoreboard at a cricket match. The match itself is messy and full of things happening. The scoreboard boils it down to numbers: runs, wickets, overs bowled, boundaries. Each figure on the board is a random variable. Before the first ball, nobody knows what the board will show, but everyone knows what kind of numbers it can show.

Distributions, expected value and spread

1. The probability distribution of a discrete random variable

2. Where do the probabilities come from?

Classical

All outcomes equally likely, so count them. A fair die: f(x)=1/6f(x) = 1/6f(x)=1/6 for each face. Used in the class workbook's Expected Value sheet.

Relative frequency (empirical)

Use your records. If 64 of 200 days had 2 sales, f(2)=64/200=0.32f(2) = 64/200 = 0.32f(2)=64/200=0.32. This is how most business distributions are built.

Subjective

No data yet, so experienced people put numbers on scenarios. Typical for a new product launch or an M&A deal.

3. Expected value: the long-run average

Ask: “If this situation repeats again and again, what is the average value of XXX?” You can't just average the possible values (0 to 5 averages to 2.5), because some values happen much more often than others. So you weight each value by its probability.

Two things trip people up. First, the expected value doesn't have to be a value you can actually see: nobody sells 1.96 ACs. Second, “expected” doesn't mean “most likely”. The most likely day is 2 ACs. E(X) is the average over many days, which is exactly what you need for planning: 1.96 × 30 ≈ 59 ACs a month.

4. Variance and standard deviation: how much outcomes wander

Two products can have the same expected profit and totally different risk. Variance measures spread: take each value's distance from the mean, square it, and weight it by its probability.

5. Continuous random variables: probability is area

With a continuous variable there are infinitely many possible values, so you can't list them in a table. Instead, the distribution is a smooth curve called a probability density function. The rule changes too: probability is the area under the curve over a range.

  • The total area under any density curve is 1, the continuous version of “∑f(x)=1\sum f(x) = 1∑f(x)=1”.
  • P(X=a)=0P(X = a) = 0P(X=a)=0 for any single exact value. So for continuous variables P(X≤a)=P(X<a)P(X \le a) = P(X \lt a)P(X≤a)=P(X<a). The “or equal to” changes nothing.
  • You never integrate by hand in this course. Areas come from the z-table or from Excel. The most important density, the bell-shaped normal, is the second half of the Distributions page.

6. A special discrete case: the binomial experiment

One pattern shows up so often in business that it gets its own formula: a fixed number of yes/no trials. Call a customer: buys or not. Check a part: defective or not. Approve a loan: defaults or not. If the four conditions below hold, the count of “yes” results follows the binomial distribution.

Fixed nThe experiment is a sequence of nnn identical trials, decided in advance.
Two outcomesEach trial ends in success or failure (the “success” is whatever you are counting).
Same pThe probability of success ppp is the same on every trial.
IndependentOne trial's result doesn't change the next one's chances.

Then XXX = number of successes in nnn trials, with mean npnpnp. The formula, the tables and the Excel function BINOM.DIST are taught on the Distributions page. The class problems on defective parts and employee loyalty below practise checking the four conditions.

Explore it yourself

A café chain earns ₹120 margin on each order and hands every customer a scratch card. 5% of cards win a ₹500 voucher (profit 120 − 500 = −₹380), 25% win a ₹100 voucher (profit ₹20) and 70% win nothing (profit ₹120). Is the promotion still profitable on average?
  • Read off the expected profit: it should be ₹70 per card, with an SD of about ₹112.
  • Make the ₹500 prize more generous: raise its probability to 0.15 and cut “nothing” to 0.60. Expected profit falls to ₹20. Where is the break-even point? (Keep the ₹100 prize at 0.25: E = 95 − 500 × P(₹500 prize), so it hits zero when that probability is 0.19.)
  • Type a set of probabilities that adds to 1.05. What does the explorer tell you? That's requirement (2) in action.
Each part is defective with probability 0.03. With n = 2 the three bars are exactly the probabilities in the tree diagram in the class section.
  • Check the three bars: P(X = 0) = 0.9409, P(X = 1) = 0.0582, P(X = 2) = 0.0009.
  • Switch to P(X ≥ 1) and slide n from 2 to 10. The chance of finding at least one defect climbs from 0.0591 to 0.2626. That's why inspectors take bigger samples.
Level 3 · Application

Where managers use it

Insurance

Pricing a premium

Premium = expected claim per policy + expenses + profit loading. The screen-cover example: expected claim ₹660, so a ₹799 price leaves ₹139 for everything else.

Banking

Expected loss on a loan

Expected loss = probability of default × loss given default × exposure. A ₹10 lakh loan with a 2% default chance and 45% loss if it defaults has an expected loss of ₹9,000, which the interest margin must cover.

Retail

How much stock to carry

The distribution of daily demand gives expected sales and the chance of running out. Expected value sets the average order; the right tail sets the safety stock.

Marketing

Value of one mailer

If 3% of recipients respond with a ₹600 margin and each mailer costs ₹12, the expected value per mailer is 0.03 × 600 − 12 = ₹6. Positive, so send it, and test whether a better list pushes the response rate up.

HR

Offers vs joiners

If each campus offer is accepted with probability 0.7, then 20 offers bring 14 joiners on average (a binomial mean, npnpnp). Planning for 14, not 20, avoids an empty desk on day one.

Operations

Maintenance budget

Count breakdowns per week from the log, build the distribution, multiply its expected value by the average repair bill. That is the weekly maintenance budget; the SD tells you how much buffer to keep.

Indian context

Term life insurance is the cleanest example of expected value at work. Suppose (purely for illustration) a healthy 30-year-old has a 0.1% chance of dying within a year. On a ₹1 crore cover, the expected payout is 0.001 × ₹1 crore = ₹10,000 a year. That is why a cover worth crores can cost only thousands: the premium follows the expected payout plus a loading, not the size of the cover. And it works for the insurer only because it holds lakhs of such policies.

Level 4 · Pro

Nuance, pitfalls and depth

Linear transformations: E(aX + b) Light coverage

Risk vs expected value: why insurers profit

Back to the screen cover. One policy is a coin toss with ugly odds for the insurer: 90% of the time it keeps ₹799, but 10% of the time it pays ₹4,500 or ₹15,000. So how do insurers stay in business? They sell a lot of policies. The average payout per policy over nnn independent policies has the same mean (₹660) but an SD of only σ/n\sigma/\sqrt{n}σ/n​.

With 100 policies the insurer still loses money about 26% of the time. With 1,000 it's 3.7%. With 10,000 it's practically never. This is the law of large numbers: averages of many independent random variables settle down near the expected value. It's the same reason casinos, lotteries and large lenders can plan on expected values while individual customers can't.

And the customer? Her expected value from buying is negative (she pays ₹799 for an average ₹660 of cover). She buys anyway because a sudden ₹15,000 bill hurts more than its probability-weighted size suggests. Economists call this risk aversion. The deal makes sense for both sides: the insurer pools risk, the customer swaps a big uncertain loss for a small certain one.

How a consultant would present it

Never give a client just the expected value. Give three numbers: the expected outcome, a measure of spread (SD or a range), and the chance of the bad case. “Option A: expected ₹17 lakh, but a 3-in-10 chance of losing ₹40 lakh. Option B: expected ₹16 lakh, no loss in any scenario.” Then let the client's appetite for risk decide.

Interview-style question

“I'll toss a coin. Heads you win ₹1 lakh, tails you pay me ₹80,000. Will you play?” The expected value is 0.5(100000)+0.5(−80000)=+10,0000.5(100000) + 0.5(-80000) = +10{,}0000.5(100000)+0.5(−80000)=+10,000, so a large firm should say yes every time. A student with ₹50,000 in the bank should say no: one tails would be a disaster. The good answer mentions both the EV and the size of the downside relative to what you can afford.

Population SD vs sample SD

For a probability distribution, σ2=∑(x−μ)2f(x)\sigma^2 = \sum (x-\mu)^2 f(x)σ2=∑(x−μ)2f(x) is a population quantity: no n−1n - 1n−1 anywhere. When the probabilities come from 200 days of records and you treat those days as a sample from all possible summer days, Excel's STDEV.S (and the ToolPak) gives 1.2353 instead of 1.2322. With a few hundred observations the difference is tiny. With 10 observations it is noticeable, and the sample version is the honest one. The dispersion page explains the n−1n-1n−1.

Continuous random variables in practice

Real continuous variables are rarely perfectly normal. Delivery times and claim amounts are right-skewed (a few very long or very large ones). Returns have fatter tails than the bell curve. The idea “probability = area” always holds; only the shape of the curve changes. Checking the shape (histogram, normal probability plot) is covered in the Pro section of the Distributions page.

How to think about it

The tree is the slow, safe way to see where the binomial formula comes from. Count the branches that give the value you want (that's the (nx)\binom{n}{x}(xn​) part), and multiply the probabilities along one branch (that's the px(1−p)n−xp^x(1-p)^{n-x}px(1−p)n−x part). Quick check

Which of these is a continuous random variable?

  1. Number of customers waiting at a bank counter
  2. Number of defective bulbs in a carton of 50
  3. Time a visitor spends on a company's website
  4. Number of loan applications approved today
Time is measured, not counted, and can take any value in a range (2.71 minutes, 2.714 minutes…). The other three are counts with gaps between values, so they are discrete.

An insurer calculates that its expected payout on a policy is ₹660. What does this mean?

  3. No policy will cost the insurer more than ₹660.
  4. Averaged over a large number of policies, the payout works out to about ₹660 per policy.
Expected value is a long-run average. In the example, 90% of policies pay nothing and a few pay ₹4,500 or ₹15,000; nobody actually receives ₹660.

Which situation is not a binomial experiment?

  1. Calling 15 randomly chosen customers and counting how many renew, each renewing with probability 0.6
  2. Checking 20 parts from a stable machine that makes 3% defectives and counting the defectives
  3. Drawing 4 names one after another, without replacement, from a team of 6 (2 women, 4 men) and counting the women
  4. Tossing a fair coin 10 times and counting heads
In (c) the probability of picking a woman changes after every draw (2/6, then 1/5 or 2/5…), and the draws are not independent. With such a small group the “same p” and “independent” conditions fail. The others meet all four conditions.
