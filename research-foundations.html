The manager's question

The manager's question

You run marketing for a mid-sized apparel chain with stores across Uttarakhand. Last month you sent discount coupons to customers of a sister brand. Sales went up. Your CEO wants to know three things by Friday: who actually came in with the coupons, whether the coupon caused the extra sales, and whether you should run it again in Diwali week.

Your analyst sends you a file of 4,000 card transactions. Half the city names are spelt three ways, some bills say “₹ 1,250” as text, a few rows are duplicated and the age column has gaps. Where do you even start?

Good decisions come from good questions, the right kind of data and a clean worksheet. This module builds all three before you compute a single average.

This page covers Unit 1 of the syllabus end to end. It's examined with theory questions, so the focus is on clear ideas, the right vocabulary and examples you can write down in an exam. There are short numbers only where they make an idea click.

Level 1 · Basics

Statistics, data and research in plain words

“Statistics” means two different things in business, and you need both.

First, statistics are numbers: the median salary in your firm, last quarter's revenue growth, this month's inflation rate. Second, statistics is a discipline: the art and science of collecting, organising, analysing and interpreting data so a manager can act on it. When your boss says “give me the stats”, she means the first. When this course says “Statistics for Managers”, it means the second.

Think of it like

A doctor's check-up. The thermometer reading is a statistic (a number). Knowing which tests to order, how to read them and what treatment follows is statistics (the discipline). Nobody pays a doctor for reading a thermometer.

Why managers need it

Honestly, the manager's question is never “what is the mean?” It's “should we open the store, change the price, keep the supplier, hire more people?” Statistics turns a pile of data into an answer you can defend in a meeting. It does four jobs that gut feel can't:

  • Describe: present data so people see what's really there (Module 2).
  • Infer: draw conclusions about a big group from a small sample, with a stated margin of error (Module 3).
  • Forecast: predict next month's demand or cash flow with numbers, not hunches (Module 4).
  • Improve: measure variation in a process and act on it, like quality control on a packing line (Module 5).

Descriptive versus inferential statistics

These are the two branches, and the difference is simple. Descriptive statistics summarise the data you actually hold, using tables, charts and numbers like the mean. Inferential statistics use a sample to say something about a larger group you didn't fully observe, and they always come with a level of uncertainty attached.

The class deck (slide 8) borrows a neat example from the Anderson et al. Chapter 1 lecture slides (it isn't in the textbook itself). Hudson Auto Repair looks at 50 tune-up invoices and finds an average parts cost of $79. Reporting “the average for these 50 was $79” is descriptive. Using that $79 to estimate the parts cost for all its tune-ups is inferential.

The vocabulary of a data set

A handful of words have fixed meanings that you'll use all semester. Learn them now and every textbook chapter gets easier.

Element
The entity you collect data on: a customer, a store, an invoice, a company.
Variable
A characteristic of interest for the elements: city, bill amount, rating.
Observation
The set of measurements for one element: one row of the sheet.
Data set
All the data collected in a study. With nnn elements and kkk variables you hold n×kn \times kn×k data values.
Population
Every element you want a conclusion about (all 12,000 customers).
Sample
The part of the population you actually study (400 of them).
Parameter
A number describing the population, such as μ\muμ (mean) or ppp (proportion). Usually unknown.
Statistic
A number computed from the sample, such as xˉ\bar{x}xˉ or p^\hat{p}p^​. You use it to estimate the parameter.
Census
Collecting data on the entire population. A sample survey collects it from a sample instead.

How you pick the sample is a whole topic of its own. The short version is below in Sampling techniques, and the full treatment is on the Sampling page.

Level 2 · Theory

Business research: types, process and design

Business research is a planned, systematic way of collecting and analysing information to help a manager make a decision.

The key word is decision. Academic research may chase knowledge for its own sake. Business research exists because someone has to choose between options and wants less guesswork. That's why it pays for itself: it cuts the risk of a costly wrong call, it replaces opinion with evidence when two departments disagree, and it spots problems (and opportunities) before they show up in the profit and loss account.

Three types of research by purpose

Exploratory

Used when the problem itself is fuzzy. Small, flexible, mostly qualitative: in-depth interviews, focus groups, a scan of reports and past data. It produces ideas and hypotheses, not final answers.

Descriptive

Used when you know what to measure. Structured surveys, panels and transaction records answer who, what, where, when and how many. Most MBA projects are descriptive.

Causal

Used when you need to know whether X causes Y. That needs control over X, so it usually means an experiment such as an A/B test.

Experimental versus observational studies

How the data are obtained decides what you're allowed to claim. Get this one right in the exam.

Experimental study

You identify the variable of interest and then control one or more other variables to see their effect. Example: show half your website visitors layout A and half layout B, chosen at random, and compare conversions. Random assignment lets you claim cause and effect.

vs

Observational study

You don't control anything; you record what happens. Example: survey customers about how often they shop and how much they spend. You can find associations, but a third factor might explain them, so causal claims are risky.

A famous large experiment: the 1954 Salk polio vaccine trial involved nearly two million children (this example appears on the class slide). A famous observational one: studies comparing smokers and non-smokers, where the researcher obviously doesn't decide who smokes.

The six-step research process

Each step constrains the next. A brilliant analysis can't rescue a badly defined problem or a biased sample.

  1. Problem definition. Separate the management decision problem (“should we repeat the coupon in Diwali week?”) from the research question (“did coupon users spend more per visit than regular customers, and are they new to us?”). Write objectives you can actually measure.
  2. Research design. The blueprint: research type, variables and how each will be measured, population and sampling plan, data collection method, analysis plan, budget and timeline. More on this just below.
  3. Data collection. Run the survey or experiment, or pull the records. Then prepare the data: check, code, clean and validate it in Excel (the Excel section shows how).
  4. Analysis. Describe first (tables, charts, averages), then estimate, test and model as the objectives require.
  5. Interpretation. Translate results into business meaning. What does a 6% difference mean for revenue? How sure are we? What can't the data tell us?
  6. Reporting. Communicate so that a decision follows: the answer first, the evidence second, the method and limits in an appendix.
Good practice

Most research failures happen at step 1 or step 3. A perfectly executed analysis of the wrong question, or of a biased sample, is worthless, and no amount of Excel can repair it.

Research design basics

A research design is the plan that makes sure the data you collect can actually answer the question. A good design states, in writing:

Where data come from, and what kind they are

Primary and secondary sources

Primary data

You collect it yourself for this study: a survey you designed, an experiment you ran, observation of a store or process, interviews and focus groups.

Plus: fits your question exactly; you know how it was collected. Minus: slow and costly.

vs

Secondary data

Someone else collected it: internal records (sales, inventory, HR, CRM), government releases (MoSPI, RBI, Census of India), industry databases (CMIE), published reports and the web.

Plus: quick and cheap. Minus: may be stale, defined differently or of unknown quality.

Before you use any data set, check three things (class slide 13): time (is it current enough?), cost (data is rarely free, even from firms that don't sell data) and errors (data gathered carelessly, or reused just because it was handy, gives misleading answers).

Surveys

Ask people

Questionnaires online, by phone or face to face. Good for attitudes, intentions and reasons. Weak on actual behaviour, because people misremember.

Experiments

Change something, measure the effect

A/B tests, price tests, pilot launches in a few stores. The only clean route to cause and effect.

Transactional & big data

Records of what people did

Billing (POS) data, UPI and card payments, website clickstreams, app logs, sensors. Huge volume, arriving fast, in many formats: the “three Vs” of big data.

Indian context

Free, official secondary data you can use in projects: the Open Government Data platform (data.gov.in), RBI's Database on Indian Economy (DBIE), MoSPI releases such as the Consumer Price Index and the Periodic Labour Force Survey, and Census tables. Always note the release date, the definitions and the base year in your report.

Categorical or quantitative?

This is the first question to ask about any variable, because the analysis that's allowed depends on the answer.

Categorical (qualitative)
Labels or names for an attribute: payment mode, region, brand, satisfaction level. Can be text or numeric codes. Options for analysis are limited: counts, percentages, mode.
Quantitative
How many or how much, always numeric, and ordinary arithmetic makes sense: net sales, items bought, age.
Discrete
Quantitative values you count: items purchased, defects per hour, complaints per day.
Continuous
Quantitative values you measure, which can take any value in a range: bill amount, weight, time on site.

Cross-sectional versus time-series data

Cross-sectional data are collected at (about) the same point in time across many units: all stores' sales in October, or the 100 Pelican Stores transactions recorded on one day. Time-series data follow one unit over several periods: one store's sales quarter after quarter. Time series let you see trends and seasonal patterns, and project forward (that's the Time series & forecasting page in Module 4).

The four scales of measurement

The scale of a variable decides which summaries and tests are legitimate. Using the wrong one is the commonest silent error in student projects.

There are four scales. Each one keeps every property of the scale below it and adds one more. Remember the order with NOIR: Nominal, Ordinal, Interval, Ratio.

Nominal: labels only

Categories with no order. A numeric code carries no arithmetic meaning: coding Business = 1 and Humanities = 2 doesn't make Humanities “twice” Business. Examples: payment method, gender, city, PIN code, employee ID.

Ordinal: labels in order

Rank is meaningful, but the gaps between ranks aren't equal. The step from “Neutral” to “Satisfied” isn't necessarily the same as “Unsatisfied” to “Neutral”. Examples: satisfaction rating, bond rating (AAA, AA, A…), class rank, T-shirt size.

Interval: equal gaps, no true zero

Differences are meaningful, ratios aren't, because zero is arbitrary. 30°C is 10 degrees warmer than 20°C, but it isn't “1.5 times as hot”. Examples: temperature in °C or °F, calendar year, standardised test scores.

Ratio: equal gaps and a true zero

Zero means “none of it”, so ratios make sense: a customer who spent ₹4,000 spent twice as much as one who spent ₹2,000. Examples: sales, cost, age, weight, distance, time, number of items.

Notice two things. First, a higher scale can always be “downgraded” (you can turn ages into age groups, ratio to ordinal), but never upgraded. So collect data at the highest scale you can. Second, the scale belongs to the variable, not to the numbers: a PIN code like 248001 is a number, but averaging PIN codes is nonsense because the scale is nominal.

Exam tip

In a scale question, write the scale, the property that puts it there, and one statistic it allows. “Satisfaction (1–5) is ordinal: the order is meaningful but the gaps aren't equal, so report the median and the percentage in each category.” That's a full-mark answer.

Sampling techniques at a glance

You sample rather than take a census for four reasons: cost (surveying 400 customers is affordable; surveying all 12,000 isn't), time (a census finishes after the decision has been made), feasibility (some testing destroys the product: you can't crash-test every car you sell) and, surprisingly, accuracy (a small, careful sample often beats a big, sloppy census).

Target population
The complete set of elements you want the conclusion to apply to.
Sampling frame
The actual list you draw from. Gaps between the frame and the population create bias.
Sampling error
The natural gap between a sample result and the population value. Shrinks as nnn grows.
Non-sampling error
Bad questions, non-response, data-entry mistakes. A bigger sample does not fix these.

The four probability methods

In a probability sample every member of the population has a known, non-zero chance of selection. That's what makes valid confidence intervals and tests possible later.

MethodHow it worksUse it whenWatch out for

StratifiedSplit into similar-inside strata, sample randomly in each. With 600 metro, 360 tier-2 and 240 tier-3 customers and n=100n = 100n=100, proportional allocation gives 50, 30 and 20Subgroups differ from each otherYou must know stratum sizes in advance

Non-probability samples (convenience, judgement, quota, snowball) are fast and cheap, and common in student projects. But selection chances are unknown, so you can't compute a valid margin of error from them. Say so in your report if you use one.

Three biases to name in any report: selection bias (the frame leaves out part of the population), non-response bias (people who reply differ from those who don't) and measurement bias (the question itself pushes the answer). The full story, with an interactive sampler, is on the Sampling page.

Ethics in business research

The standard: be fair, thorough, objective and neutral when you collect, analyse and present data, and stay alert to bad practice in the numbers other people hand you.

Where research goes wrong

Biased sampling

Choosing a frame or method that quietly leaves out the people who'd disagree. Surveying only app users about whether the app is easy to use, for instance.

Leading questions

“Don't you agree our new menu is excellent?” pushes the answer. That's measurement bias by design.

Inappropriate analysis

Running a test whose assumptions the data plainly break, then reporting the p-value anyway. Or reporting a mean when the data are badly skewed and the median tells another story.

Cherry-picking

Showing only the results that support a decision already taken, or testing twenty things and reporting the one that “worked”.

Fabrication and falsification

Inventing responses to hit a sample-size target, or changing awkward values. It's misconduct, full stop, and it's easier to detect than people think.

Plagiarism

Using someone else's text, data, charts or code without credit. In your capstone, cite every data source and every borrowed idea.

Respecting respondents

Informed consent
Before anyone answers, tell them who you are, why you're asking, what you'll do with the data, how long it takes, and that they can refuse or stop. Then get a clear “yes”.
Anonymity
Even the researcher can't link an answer to a person (no names, IDs, phone numbers).
Confidentiality
The researcher can identify people but promises not to reveal them. Don't call a survey anonymous if it isn't.
Data minimisation
Collect only what the analysis needs. Don't ask for a phone number you'll never call.

Misleading charts

A chart can lie without a single wrong number. The classic trick is cutting the vertical axis.

The fixes are simple: start bar and column axes at zero; use percentages rather than raw counts when groups differ in size; drop 3-D effects and decoration; label axes and give the source every time. The Visualization page has more.

Data privacy law: GDPR and India's DPDP Act

If your research touches personal data (anything that identifies a person), the law applies to you, not just to big companies.

GDPR (European Union)

  • The General Data Protection Regulation (EU) 2016/679, applying since 25 May 2018.
  • Reaches beyond Europe: it covers organisations anywhere that offer goods or services to people in the EU or monitor their behaviour.
  • Seven principles: lawfulness, fairness and transparency; purpose limitation; data minimisation; accuracy; storage limitation; integrity and confidentiality; accountability.
  • Processing needs a lawful basis; for research surveys this is usually consent.
  • People have rights of access, correction, erasure, restriction, portability and objection, plus rights about automated decisions.
  • Fines can reach €20 million or 4% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher.

DPDP Act 2023 (India)

  • The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 covers digital personal data, including data collected offline and digitised later.
  • The person is the Data Principal; the organisation deciding why and how data are processed is the Data Fiduciary.
  • Consent must be free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous, given after a clear notice, and can be withdrawn.
  • For children (under 18), verifiable consent of a parent or guardian is needed.
  • Rights include access to information, correction and erasure, grievance redressal and nominating someone to act for you.
  • A Data Protection Board of India enforces it; penalties go up to ₹250 crore. The DPDP Rules were notified on 14 November 2025, with most duties phased in over 18 months.
Don't over-read the exemption

The DPDP Act exempts processing for research, archiving or statistical purposes only if the data aren't used to take decisions about a specific person and the processing follows prescribed standards. A “customer research” survey that feeds a targeted-offer list isn't research in that sense. And legal or not, consent and confidentiality remain ethical duties.

Preparing data for analysis in Excel

A clean worksheet isn't housekeeping. It's the first analytical step, and most errors in later modules trace back to it.

1. Worksheet design: one row per observation

  1. One column per variable. Give each a single short header in row 1: Spend_Rs, not “Spend (in ₹, incl. GST)” across two merged rows.
  2. One row per observation. Start in row 2 and give every row an ID.
  3. No blank rows or columns inside the data. A blank row silently cuts the range, and your mean is computed on half the data.
  4. No merged cells, no totals inside the data. Put results on a separate sheet.
  5. Units in the header, not in the cell. Type 4500, not “₹ 4,500”.
  6. Keep the raw data untouched. Leave it on its own sheet, clean on a copy, and record every cleaning decision on a Notes sheet.

Tip: select the data and press Ctrl+T (Insert › Table). An Excel Table grows automatically as you add rows, so formulas and PivotTables never miss new data.

4. Sorting and filtering

Excel · Sort & Filter

Data › Sort lets you sort on several levels (by City, then by Net sales, largest first). Always select the whole table, or let Excel expand the selection, so each row stays together. Sorting one column on its own scrambles your data.

Data › Filter (Ctrl+Shift+L) adds drop-downs to the header row so you can look at one group at a time: only promotional customers, only bills above ₹5,000. Sorting by a column is also the fastest way to find impossible values (age 250, negative sales) sitting at the top or bottom.

Careful: =AVERAGE(D2:D101) still uses hidden rows. To average only what the filter shows, use =SUBTOTAL(101,D2:D101).

5. Removing duplicates

Excel · duplicates
  1. See them first. Home › Conditional Formatting › Highlight Cells Rules › Duplicate Values on the ID column, or a helper column =COUNTIF($A$2:$A$4001,A2)>1 that shows TRUE for repeats.
  2. Decide what a duplicate is. Same transaction ID? Or same customer, date and amount? Pick the columns that define one real record.
  3. Remove on a copy. Data › Data Tools › Remove Duplicates, tick those columns, and note how many rows Excel removed. Once saved, it can't be undone.

6. Handling missing values

Ask why a value is missing before you decide what to do. A blank because the question didn't apply (no spouse's income for a single person) is a different problem from a blank because the respondent refused. Only the second kind threatens your conclusions.

Leave it blank

Excel's statistical functions ignore truly empty cells. Never type 0 or “NA” into a number column: 0 is a value and drags the mean down. Count blanks with =COUNTBLANK(D2:D101).

Delete the record

Simple and defensible when very few rows are affected and the gaps look random. Dangerous if missing values bunch up in one group: that's how bias creeps in.

Impute a value

Fill with the column mean or median, or a value predicted from other columns. It keeps the row, but it shrinks the variability, so say so in the report.

Return to the source

The best option when you can: go back to the bill, the form or the respondent and recover the true value.

Quick numbers

Why a typed 0 or a filled-in mean changes your answer

Monthly spend (₹) of six loyalty members: 2,400; 3,100; missing; 2,800; 3,500; 2,600.

8. The data-cleaning checklist

Before you analyse anything, tick all ten

  1. Raw file saved untouched; working copy created; a Notes sheet logs every change.
  2. One header row; one row per observation; one column per variable; no merged cells, blank rows or in-data totals.
  3. Every numeric column really is numeric (ISNUMBER, right-aligned); units live in headers.
  4. Dates are real dates; codes and PIN codes kept as text.
  5. Category labels standardised with TRIM, PROPER and Find & Replace; a filter drop-down shows only valid labels.
  6. Duplicates highlighted, defined, then removed on a copy; number removed recorded.
  7. Missing values counted (COUNTBLANK), reasons noted, and a decision (blank, delete, impute, recover) written down.
  8. Impossible or extreme values checked by sorting each numeric column both ways.
  9. Data Validation rules set for any data still to be entered.
  10. Personal identifiers removed or replaced with codes before the file is shared.

Concepts in practice: short scenarios

Theory questions in the exam often come wrapped in a mini-situation. Here are seven. Cover the answer, decide, then check.

Scenario 1

Descriptive or inferential?

Situation
(a) A bank reports that 4,812 of its 60,000 Dehradun account holders used UPI on its app last month, counted from its own records. (b) The same bank surveys 400 randomly chosen customers to estimate how many would take a new credit card.
Answer
(a) Descriptive · (b) Inferential
Why

(a) is a count of the whole population from complete records: there's nothing to infer. (b) uses a sample result to estimate a population share, so it needs a margin of error.

Scenario 2

Which type of research comes first?

Situation
A café chain's weekday footfall has dropped 15% in three months and nobody knows why.
Answer
Exploratory → descriptive → causal
Why

Start exploratory: talk to staff and regulars, run two focus groups, check what changed nearby (a new competitor? road works?). That gives hypotheses. Then a descriptive survey measures how common each reason is. If the fix is a weekday offer, a causal test (offer in six outlets, not in six similar ones) shows whether it really works.

Scenario 3

Classify the variables

Scenario 4

Pick the sampling method

Situation
A retail chain has 40 stores in 4 regions and wants a staff-engagement survey. North has 18 stores, the other regions 6 to 9 each. HR fears the North will drown out everyone else.
Answer
Stratified by region (cluster by store if travel is the constraint)
Why

Regions differ from each other, so strata guarantee each one is represented, and you can report region by region. If interviewers must travel, pick a few whole stores at random within each region and survey everyone there: stratified cluster sampling.

Scenario 5

Spot the ethical problem

Situation
An HR survey says “This survey is anonymous” but asks for employee code, department and age band. One department has three people.
Answer
False promise of anonymity; re-identification risk
Why

With an employee code it isn't anonymous at all, and even without it, department plus age band can identify someone in a team of three. Fix: drop the code, merge small departments into “Other”, report only groups of, say, ten or more, and describe the survey honestly as confidential.

Scenario 6

Clean this column

Situation
A Gender column contains: Male, male, M, Female, F , female, (blank). A PivotTable shows seven categories instead of two.
Answer
TRIM + PROPER, then Find & Replace, then validate
Why

=PROPER(TRIM(B2)) merges “male”/“Male” and strips the space in “F ”. Find & Replace with “Match entire cell contents” turns M → Male and F → Female. Leave the blank blank. Add a List validation (Male, Female, and any other option your form actually offers) so it doesn't happen again.

Scenario 7

Census or sample?

Situation
A spice company wants to check the moisture content of its 1 lakh packets produced this week. The test destroys the packet.
Answer
Sample (systematic from the line)
Why

A census would destroy the whole week's output. Take every kkk-th packet off the line (with a random start) and test those. The same logic applies to crash tests, lab tests and tasting.

Level 3 · Application

Where managers use it

Every specialisation in your MBA runs on this material. Some examples by function:

Accounting & audit

Statistical sampling of invoices

Auditors can't check every voucher, so they draw a systematic or random sample and estimate the error rate in the full ledger. The sampling frame (the voucher register) has to be complete.

Finance

Credit decisions

A loan file mixes ratio data (income, EMI), ordinal data (credit grade) and nominal data (employment type). The scale of each decides what the credit model can do with it.

Marketing

A/B testing and scanner data

Email subject line A vs B, randomly assigned, is an experiment, so the winner really caused the extra opens. POS scanner data on price and promotion is observational: useful, but watch for confounders like festivals.

Operations

Process data over time

Daily defect counts form a time series; comparing five plants in one month is cross-sectional. Control charts (Module 5) live on time-series data.

Human resources

Engagement surveys

Likert items are ordinal, so report the percentage agreeing, not just a mean of 3.7. Confidentiality isn't optional: people won't answer honestly if they think their manager can see their response.

E-commerce

Transactional big data

Millions of orders a month, so the problem isn't sample size. It's duplicates from app retries, test orders, and bots in the clickstream. Cleaning comes first.

Indian context

Under the DPDP Act, a food-delivery or fintech app that runs a customer survey is a Data Fiduciary: it must give a clear notice, collect only what it needs, honour withdrawal of consent, and protect the file. When your capstone uses a Google Form, the same habits apply: say why you're asking, skip phone numbers you don't need, and store the sheet securely.

Mini-case: why have enrolments fallen?

A coaching institute in Dehradun (hypothetical) saw enrolments for its banking-exam course fall from 640 to 520 in a year. The director asks you to find out why. Here's the six-step process in action.

  1. Define. Decision: cut fees, change the timetable, or move online? Research question: what are the main reasons enquirers didn't enrol, and how common is each?
  2. Design. Exploratory first (interviews with ten counsellors and ten non-enrolled enquirers), then a descriptive survey. Population: everyone who enquired in the last six months (1,800 people on the CRM: that's your frame). Plan a stratified random sample of 300 by enquiry channel (walk-in, website, phone).
  3. Collect. A short phone or online questionnaire with consent script; reasons coded as nominal, “likelihood to join next batch” on a 1–5 ordinal scale, fee quoted (₹) as ratio. Clean in Excel: remove duplicate enquiries, standardise city names, count blanks.
  4. Analyse. Percentage of non-enrollers citing each reason; cross-tab of reason by channel; median likelihood score by reason.
  5. Interpret. Suppose 41% of website enquirers cite timing but only 12% cite fees: the problem is the timetable, not the price, and only for online-first students.
  6. Report. Lead with the answer (“offer an evening batch; don't cut fees”), show one clear chart, put the method and the limits (non-response, self-reported reasons) at the back.

The 41% and 12% are illustrative, to show what an interpretation sentence looks like.

Level 4 · Pro

Nuance, pitfalls and depth

Reliability and validity Light coverage

A measure is reliable if it gives consistent results when repeated, and valid if it measures what you think it measures. You need both, and reliability alone isn't enough: a bathroom scale that always reads 2 kg light is perfectly reliable and wrong every time.

Two more you'll hear in research methods books (Cooper & Schindler; Zikmund et al.): internal validity (did X really cause Y in this study, or did something else?) and external validity (do the results carry over to other people, places and times?). Lab-style experiments score high on the first and often low on the second.

Correlation isn't causation

Air-conditioner sales and ice-cream sales move together, but neither causes the other: summer temperature drives both. That's a confounding variable. Observational data can't rule confounders out, which is why causal claims need experiments, or at least careful controls (the regression module shows how to hold other variables constant).

Are Likert scales ordinal or interval?

A single 1–5 agreement item is ordinal: nobody promises that the gap from 4 to 5 equals the gap from 1 to 2. In practice, researchers often add several items into a scale score and treat the total as interval, then use means and t-tests. That's widely accepted for multi-item scales with five or more points. For a single item, safer reporting is the median plus the percentage who agree (“top-two box”).

Bigger isn't better if the frame is wrong

In 1936 the Literary Digest magazine collected more than two million mail-in ballots and predicted Alf Landon would beat Franklin Roosevelt. Roosevelt won in a landslide. The sample was huge, but its mailing lists leaned on sources such as telephone directories and car registrations, which over-represented better-off voters in the Depression, and only about a quarter of those mailed sent a ballot back. A bigger nnn shrinks sampling error; it does nothing for selection or non-response bias. Big data has the same trap: ten million app users still aren't the people who don't use your app.

Panel data Beyond the syllabus

Track the same units over time (100 stores, monthly, for three years) and you have panel data: cross-sectional and time-series at once. It's what most real business data looks like, and it's the basis of more advanced econometrics.

An audit-ready workbook

The syllabus asks for analyses that are “reproducible and audit-ready”. In practice that means: raw data never edited; every number on the results sheet traceable by formula to the data; a Notes sheet listing each cleaning step, the number of rows affected, and why; and named sheets in order (Raw → Clean → Analysis → Charts). A reviewer should be able to re-create your answer without asking you a single question.

How a consultant would present it

One “Method” slide, always the same four lines: Who (population, frame, sample size, response rate), How (survey, experiment or records; dates), What (key variables and scales), Limits (biases you couldn't remove). Clients trust findings far more when the method is short, honest and up front.

Interview insight

“Tell me about a time data misled you” is a common placement question. A strong answer names the mechanism: a truncated axis, a biased frame, a typed 0 for missing, an observational result read as causal. Then say how you'd catch it next time.

Exam-style theory questions

Ten model questions in the three styles used in the end-semester paper. Write your own answer first, then open the model answer. Two-mark answers are a definition plus an example; five-mark answers are a structured comparison or list with examples; ten-mark answers add a diagram, an example worked through, and a closing line on managerial use.

State the two meanings of the word “statistics”.

Model answer

(1) Numerical facts that summarise a situation, such as averages, percentages or index numbers (“median salary ₹8.4 lakh”). (2) A discipline: the science of collecting, organising, analysing, presenting and interpreting data to support decisions.

Distinguish between a parameter and a statistic.

Model answer

A parameter is a numerical measure of a population (e.g. the mean spend μ\muμ of all 12,000 customers); it is usually unknown. A statistic is the corresponding measure computed from a sample (e.g. the mean xˉ\bar{x}xˉ of 400 surveyed customers) and is used to estimate the parameter.

What is the difference between cross-sectional and time-series data? Give one example of each.

Model answer

Cross-sectional data are collected at the same (or about the same) time across many units: sales of 40 stores in October. Time-series data are collected on one unit over several periods: one store's monthly sales for three years. Time series show trends and seasonality; cross-sections compare units.

Differentiate between descriptive and inferential statistics with suitable business examples.

Managerial use: descriptive tells you what happened; inferential tells you how far you can generalise it, which matters before spending money on a roll-out.

Explain exploratory, descriptive and causal research. Which would you use to test whether a new store layout increases sales?

Model answer
  • Exploratory: clarifies a vague problem and generates hypotheses; flexible, qualitative methods (interviews, focus groups, secondary data). E.g. “Why are renewals falling?”
  • Descriptive: measures and profiles; structured surveys, panels, transaction data. E.g. “What share of shoppers use coupons, by age group?”
  • Causal: establishes cause and effect by manipulating one variable and controlling others; experiments. E.g. “Does free delivery raise basket size?”

The store layout question is causal: use an experiment, with the new layout in randomly chosen stores and the old layout in comparable control stores, then compare sales over the same weeks.

Briefly describe four probability sampling methods, stating when each is suitable.

Model answer
  • Simple random: every sample of size nnn equally likely; needs a complete frame; suits fairly uniform populations.
  • Systematic: random start, then every kkk-th unit, k=N/nk = N/nk=N/n (5,000 invoices, n=250n = 250n=250 gives k=20k = 20k=20); suits long lists; risk of periodicity.
  • Stratified: divide into internally similar strata, sample randomly within each (often proportionally); more precise when subgroups differ.
  • Cluster: divide into natural groups (stores, villages), randomly select whole clusters, survey all inside; cheapest when the population is spread out, but less precise.

All four give each unit a known, non-zero chance of selection, which is what allows valid inference.

How should missing values be handled when preparing data in Excel? Explain the options and their risks.

Model answer
  • First ask why the value is missing (not applicable vs refused) and count blanks with COUNTBLANK.
  • Leave blank: Excel functions ignore empty cells. Never enter 0 or “NA”: with spends 2,400; 3,100; –; 2,800; 3,500; 2,600 the mean is ₹2,880 with a blank but ₹2,400 with a 0.
  • Delete the record: fine for a few random gaps; biased if gaps cluster in one group.
  • Impute (mean, median or predicted value): keeps the row but understates variability (SD fell from ₹432 to ₹387 in the example); must be disclosed.
  • Return to source: best when possible.

Record the choice and the number of rows affected on a Notes sheet.

Describe the steps of the business research process. Illustrate each step with an example of a firm evaluating a sales promotion.

Model answer

Draw the six-step flow (Figure 5) with a feedback arrow from reporting to problem definition. Then:

  1. Problem definition: separate the decision (repeat the coupon at Diwali?) from the research question (did coupon users spend more per visit, and are they new customers?); set measurable objectives.
  2. Research design: research type (descriptive, with a causal follow-up), variables and scales (customer type: nominal; net sales: ratio), population and frame (all card transactions during the promotion), sampling plan, data collection method, analysis plan, budget, timeline, ethics.
  3. Data collection: extract POS records or run the survey; prepare the data in Excel (tidy layout, validation, duplicates removed, missing values handled).
  4. Analysis: descriptive tables and charts first (percent of promotional customers, mean and median net sales by type), then inferential tests where needed.
  5. Interpretation: what do the differences mean in rupees? Are they large enough to matter? What can't the data show (observational design, card-only frame)?
  6. Reporting: lead with the recommendation, one or two honest charts, method and limitations at the end; the decision then feeds the next research cycle.

Close: most failures occur at step 1 (wrong question) or step 3 (biased or dirty data), so the early steps deserve the most care.

Explain the four scales of measurement with business examples. What statistical measures are appropriate for each? Why does the distinction matter to a manager?

Model answer

Draw the ladder (Figure 8). Each scale has all the properties of the one below plus one more:

  • Nominal: labels only (payment mode, region, PIN code). Counts, percentages, mode; bar or pie chart; chi-square tests.
  • Ordinal: adds order, but unequal gaps (satisfaction level, bond rating, rank). Adds median, percentiles, rank correlation.
  • Interval: adds equal intervals, no true zero (°C, calendar year). Adds mean, SD, t-tests, Pearson correlation; differences meaningful, ratios not.
  • Ratio: adds a true zero (sales, age, time, cost). All statistics, including ratios (“twice as much”), coefficient of variation and geometric mean.

Why it matters: the scale decides which summaries and tests are valid. Averaging nominal codes or saying 30°C is “1.5 times” 20°C produces numbers that look precise but mean nothing. Managers should also collect data at the highest scale possible (exact age rather than age band), because data can be converted downwards but never upwards.

Discuss the major ethical issues in business research. How do data-protection laws such as the GDPR and India's DPDP Act, 2023 affect a firm that conducts customer surveys?

Model answer

Ethical issues (with an example each): biased sampling (frame excludes dissatisfied customers); leading questions; inappropriate analysis or summaries (mean of skewed data, ignored assumptions); selective reporting and cherry-picking; misleading charts (truncated axes: a 4.8% change drawn as 400%); fabrication or falsification of data; plagiarism; lack of informed consent; breaches of confidentiality or false promises of anonymity.

GDPR (EU, applies since 25 May 2018; also to non-EU firms serving or monitoring people in the EU): lawful basis such as consent; principles of purpose limitation, data minimisation, accuracy, storage limitation, integrity and confidentiality, accountability; rights of access, rectification, erasure, portability and objection; fines up to €20 million or 4% of global turnover.

DPDP Act 2023 (India): the firm is a Data Fiduciary and respondents are Data Principals; notice and free, specific, informed, unambiguous consent that can be withdrawn; verifiable parental consent for under-18s; rights to access, correction, erasure and grievance redressal; a Data Protection Board; penalties up to ₹250 crore; Rules notified in November 2025 with phased compliance. The research exemption applies only where data aren't used for decisions about individuals.

Effect on a survey firm: write a clear notice and consent script, collect only needed fields, pseudonymise before analysis, restrict access, set a deletion date, honour withdrawal requests, and never reuse survey data for targeted marketing without fresh consent. Ethics and compliance together protect respondents and the credibility of the findings.

Quick check

A survey asks customers to rate a hotel stay as Poor, Fair, Good, Very good or Excellent. On which scale is this variable measured?

  1. Nominal
  2. Ordinal
  3. Interval
  4. Ratio
The categories have a meaningful order, but nobody can say the gap from Poor to Fair equals the gap from Very good to Excellent. Order without equal intervals is ordinal.

Why is temperature in °C an interval variable rather than a ratio variable?

  1. It can be negative, and ratio data can't
  2. It's measured with an instrument
  3. Its zero point is arbitrary, so ratios like “twice as hot” are meaningless
  4. The intervals between degrees aren't equal
0°C doesn't mean “no temperature”, so 30°C isn't 1.5 times 20°C. The gaps are equal (that's what makes it interval), but there's no true zero. Option (a) is a common half-truth: the real reason is the arbitrary zero.

Which of these is an example of inferential statistics?

  1. A bar chart of last month's sales by region from company records
  2. The median salary of all 85 employees in the Dehradun office
  3. The percentage of this week's 1,200 orders that were delivered late
  4. Estimating the share of all city households that would buy a new brand from a survey of 400
Only (d) uses a sample to draw a conclusion about a larger population. The others describe data that are already complete.

A bank draws its survey sample from the list of customers registered for internet banking. That list is the:

  1. Sampling frame
  2. Target population
  3. Sampling error
  4. Census
The frame is the actual list you sample from. If the target population is all customers, this frame leaves out branch-only customers, which is a source of selection bias.

Six monthly spends are ₹2,400, ₹3,100, (missing), ₹2,800, ₹3,500 and ₹2,600. A clerk types 0 in the missing cell. What happens to the AVERAGE?

  1. Nothing: Excel ignores zeros

One-glance summary

Research foundations on one screen

  • Statistics = numbers (facts) and a discipline (collect → analyse → interpret → decide). Four jobs: describe, infer, forecast, improve.
  • Descriptive summarises the data you hold; inferential generalises from a sample to a population, with stated uncertainty. Parameter (population) vs statistic (sample).
  • Research types: exploratory (what's going on?), descriptive (who, what, how many?), causal (does X cause Y?). Experimental studies control variables and can show cause; observational studies can't.
  • Process: define → design → collect (and clean) → analyse → interpret → report. Most failures start at steps 1 and 3.
  • Data: primary vs secondary; categorical (nominal, ordinal) vs quantitative (discrete, continuous); cross-sectional vs time series.
  • Scales (NOIR): nominal = mode; ordinal = + median; interval = + mean, SD; ratio = + ratios, CV. Collect at the highest scale you can.
  • Sampling: simple random, systematic (k=N/nk = N/nk=N/n), stratified, cluster. Bigger nnn cuts sampling error, not bias.
  • Ethics: no biased frames, leading questions, cherry-picking, fabrication or truncated axes; informed consent; confidentiality ≠ anonymity; GDPR and the DPDP Act 2023.
  • Excel prep: one row per observation; numbers as numbers; Data Validation; sort and filter on the whole table; Remove Duplicates on a copy; blanks not zeros; TRIM/CLEAN/PROPER, Text to Columns, Find & Replace; log every change.
