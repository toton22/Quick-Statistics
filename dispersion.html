Dispersion

Range, IQR, variance, standard deviation and the coefficient of variation: how to measure consistency and risk, because the average is never the whole story.

The manager's question

The manager's question

You buy fabric for a garment exporter in Tiruppur. Two mills have quoted the same price, and over the last ten weeks both delivered in 10 days on average. Your production head says “same average, so pick either”.

Should you? Here are the actual delivery times.

Nobody runs a factory on the average. You run it on the bad weeks. With Supplier B you must keep extra fabric in stock for the 17- and 18-day weeks, and that stock is cash sitting in a godown. The number that captures this is the spread of the data, and this page teaches you to measure it.

Two datasets can share the same average and still be completely different businesses. Spread is how you tell them apart.

Level 1 · Basics

The idea in plain words

A measure of dispersion (or variability) tells you how far the values typically sit from each other, or from their centre. Small spread = consistent and predictable. Large spread = risky.

Think of it like

Two batters both average 40 runs. One scores 35 to 45 every match. The other scores 0, 0, 120, 5, 75. Same average. Which one do you want at number four in a final? The difference between them is dispersion.

Range
Largest value minus smallest value. Quick, but decided by just two observations.
Quartiles (Q1, Q3)
The values that cut off the lowest 25% and the highest 25% of the sorted data.
Interquartile range (IQR)
Q3 − Q1: the width of the middle half of the data. Ignores the extremes.
Deviation
How far one value is from the mean, x−xˉx - \bar{x}x−xˉ. Positive above the mean, negative below.
Variance
The average squared deviation. Uses every value, but its units are squared (days², ₹²).
Standard deviation (SD)
The square root of the variance, back in the original units. Read it as “the typical distance from the mean”.
Coefficient of variation (CV)
SD as a percentage of the mean. Lets you compare spread across very different scales.
z-score
How many SDs a value sits above (+) or below (−) the mean.

Why divide by n − 1? (in plain words)

The sample mean xˉ\bar{x}xˉ is calculated from the same data, so it sits right in the middle of your sample, closer to your values than the true population mean usually is. Deviations measured from xˉ\bar{x}xˉ are therefore a little too small, and dividing by a slightly smaller number, n−1n-1n−1, corrects for that. Another way to see it: once you know n−1n-1n−1 of the deviations, the last one is forced (they must add to zero). Only n−1n-1n−1 of them are free to vary, hence “degrees of freedom”.

We checked this by simulation: 100,000 samples of 5 values from a population whose true variance is 25. Dividing by nnn gave 19.97 on average (20% too low). Dividing by n−1n-1n−1 gave 24.97. Rule: sample → divide by n−1n-1n−1 (STDEV.S); whole population → divide by NNN (STDEV.P).

SD as “the typical distance from the mean”

For Supplier B the plain average distance from the mean (ignoring signs) is 4.4 days. Excel calls it AVEDEV. The SD is 5.40 days, a bit larger, because squaring gives the big misses extra weight. Both say the same thing in words: a typical B delivery lands about 4–5 days away from the 10-day average. That sentence is what you say to a manager; the formula is what you write in the exam.

The empirical rule is sharper but needs a bell-shaped (roughly normal) distribution: about 68% of values within 1 SD of the mean, 95% within 2 SD, 99.7% within 3 SD. Chebyshev is weaker but works for any data, however skewed (for any k>1k \gt 1k>1). Figure 4 tests both on the class datasets.

Outliers: two rules

  • z-score rule: treat a value with ∣z∣>3|z| \gt 3∣z∣>3 as an outlier (use it for roughly bell-shaped data).
  • Fence rule (box plots): lower fence =Q1−1.5×IQR= Q_1 - 1.5 \times \text{IQR}=Q1​−1.5×IQR, upper fence =Q3+1.5×IQR= Q_3 + 1.5 \times \text{IQR}=Q3​+1.5×IQR. Anything outside the fences is an outlier.

The five numbers Min, Q1, Median, Q3, Max are the five-number summary. Draw them as a box with whiskers and you have a box plot; how to build and read one is on the Visualization page, and you will draw one by hand for the class problem below.

Explore: feel the spread

Two suppliers with the same 10-day average. Slide Supplier B's SD and watch range, IQR, SD and CV react.
  • Drag Supplier B's SD down until it matches Supplier A's (1 day). Mean, variance, SD and CV now agree exactly (range and IQR differ a little, because the two samples are different): on this evidence the two suppliers are interchangeable.
  • Push B's SD to the top of the slider and count the red rings (deliveries later than the 13-day promise). The mean hasn't moved at all. The late count has.

  • Watch the variance when you double the SD, say from 1.5 to 3 days: it goes from 2.25 to 9, four times bigger. That's the squaring at work.

Level 3 · Application

Where managers use it

Operations

Safety stock

Buffer inventory is sized from the SD of lead time and demand, not their averages. Halve the SD and you can roughly halve the buffer.

Finance

Risk of an investment

The SD of returns is the standard measure of risk; the CV or Sharpe ratio compares funds with different average returns.

Quality

Filling and packing

A 500 g pack line with SD 2 g needs a far smaller overfill than one with SD 8 g. Six Sigma is literally about shrinking the SD.

HR

Pay within a grade

The IQR of salaries in one grade shows whether pay is consistent or whether a few people are far out of band.

Banking

ATM cash loading

Average daily withdrawals set the base load; the SD (higher around paydays and festivals) sets how much extra cash avoids an empty machine.

Marketing

Campaign consistency

Two ad sets with the same average click-through rate: the one with lower spread across cities is the safer one to scale.

Indian context: read the risk ratios on a fund factsheet

Indian equity mutual-fund factsheets usually list “Standard Deviation” and “Sharpe Ratio” under risk measures, close to the returns table. The SD there is exactly this page's SD, computed on the fund's returns. Two funds with the same 3-year return but very different SDs are not the same product, whatever the brochure says.

Mini-case

What does Supplier B's spread cost?

The Tiruppur exporter uses 400 metres of fabric a day; fabric costs ₹180 a metre. As a simple rule, the buyer holds a buffer equal to 2 SDs of lead time worth of fabric. Holding stock costs about 15% a year (interest, storage). Supplier B is offering a small discount to win the order. How much is B's unreliability worth?

Buffer, A
2×1.155=2.312 \times 1.155 = 2.312×1.155=2.31 days → 2.31×400=9242.31 \times 400 = 9242.31×400=924 m → 924×180924 \times 180924×180 ≈ ₹1.66 lakh of fabric.
Buffer, B
2×5.395=10.792 \times 5.395 = 10.792×5.395=10.79 days → 4,316 m → ≈ ₹7.77 lakh of fabric.
Difference
About ₹6.11 lakh more cash locked in stock with B. At 15% a year, that costs about ₹0.92 lakh a year.
Decision

Supplier B's discount must save more than roughly ₹92,000 a year (and cover the disruption of late weeks) before it beats Supplier A. The two suppliers' identical averages hide a cost of almost a lakh a year.

The “2 SDs of lead time” rule is a simplification for this course; operations courses refine it with service levels and demand variability.

Level 4 · Pro

Nuance, pitfalls and depth

Sample or population? .S or .P?

Ask: are these data the whole group I care about, or a sample from a bigger one? The 50 states in PovertyLevel and the 30 DJIA stocks are complete groups, so strictly they are populations (σ\sigmaσ, STDEV.P). A survey of 200 customers is a sample (sss, STDEV.S). In practice the gap shrinks fast: DJIA gives 22.01 (.S) vs 21.64 (.P); the 50 poverty rates give 4.82 vs 4.77. Textbook exercises usually treat data as a sample unless told otherwise, and so does the ToolPak.

The SD is not resistant, and outliers can hide each other

Because deviations are squared, one extreme value can inflate the SD a lot. In LargeCorp, ExxonMobil and Walmart push the SD to $80.33 billion. Remove those two and the SD of the other 48 drops to $44.10 billion, and suddenly Chevron (z=4.09z = 4.09z=4.09) and ConocoPhillips (z=3.36z = 3.36z=3.36) look like outliers too. Analysts call this masking. It is one reason box-plot fences (built on quartiles, which outliers can't move) are the safer screening tool for skewed business data.

When the CV misleads

  • Mean near zero: a fund averaging 0.5% with SD 8% has a CV of 1,600%. Meaningless. Use the SD directly.
  • Negative means (losses, falling prices): the CV turns negative. Don't use it.
  • Interval scales with an arbitrary zero (temperature in °C, a 1–5 rating): the CV changes if you shift the scale, so it has no meaning there. Use it only for ratio data (money, time, weight, counts).

Interview-style insight

“Why is variance in squared units, and why do we still use it?” Because variances add: for independent quantities, the variance of a sum is the sum of the variances. Standard deviations don't add like that. So analysts do the maths in variances and report in SDs. That's also why portfolio diversification works, which you will meet in finance.

How a consultant would present it

“Both suppliers average 10 days. Supplier A's deliveries vary by about a day either way (SD 1.2 days); Supplier B's vary by more than five (SD 5.4 days), and 3 of its last 10 deliveries took 15 days or more. Covering that variability needs about ₹6 lakh of extra stock. Unless B's discount is worth more than ₹92,000 a year, stay with A.”

Quick check

Two bank branches both average 420 customers a day. You need to decide which one is harder to staff. Which statistic helps most?

  1. The median number of customers
  2. The standard deviation of daily customers
  3. The mode of daily customers
  4. The mean number of customers
The means are equal, so they can't separate the branches. The branch with the larger SD has more unpredictable days, which makes staffing harder.

A sample has the values 4, 6 and 8. What is the sample standard deviation?

  1. 4
  2. 1.63
  3. 2.67
  4. 2
xˉ=6\bar{x} = 6xˉ=6; squared deviations 4, 0, 4 sum to 8; s2=8/(3−1)=4s^2 = 8/(3-1) = 4s2=8/(3−1)=4; s=2s = 2s=2. 4 is the variance, 2.67 divides by nnn instead of n−1n-1n−1, and 1.63 is its square root (the population SD).

Fund X: mean return 12%, SD 6%. Fund Y: mean return 20%, SD 8%. Which statement is correct?

  1. Y has lower risk relative to its return (CV 40% vs 50%)
  2. X is less risky because its SD is smaller, end of story
  3. They carry the same relative risk
  4. Funds with different means can't be compared
CVX=6/12=50%CV_X = 6/12 = 50\%CVX​=6/12=50%, CVY=8/20=40%CV_Y = 8/20 = 40\%CVY​=8/20=40%. X has the smaller SD in absolute terms, but per unit of average return Y is the steadier fund.

For any dataset, whatever its shape, at least what percentage of values lie within 2 standard deviations of the mean?

  1. 95%
  2. 68%
  3. 75%
  4. 50%
Chebyshev: 1−1/k2=1−1/4=0.751 - 1/k^2 = 1 - 1/4 = 0.751−1/k2=1−1/4=0.75. The 95% figure is the empirical rule, which only holds for bell-shaped data.

For a set of daily sales, Q1 = 20 and Q3 = 30 (₹ thousand). Which value is an outlier by the 1.5 × IQR rule?

  1. 44
  2. 46
  3. 6
  4. 35
IQR = 10, so the fences are 20−15=520 - 15 = 520−15=5 and 30+15=4530 + 15 = 4530+15=45. Only 46 is outside. 44 and 6 are close to the fences but inside them.
