The manager's question

The manager's question

You run operations for a (hypothetical) food-delivery app in Bengaluru. Marketing wants to promise “delivered in 40 minutes or it's free”. Your data say delivery time is roughly bell-shaped with a mean of 32 minutes and an SD of 5 minutes. Three questions land on your desk:

  1. What share of orders would be free?
  2. A restaurant partner sends 20 orders on a Saturday night. What's the chance that 3 or more of them go free?
  3. If finance will only accept 2% free orders, what time should the promise say?

Most business uncertainty is either a count of yes/no results (binomial) or a measurement that clusters around an average (normal). Learn those two shapes, how to read their tables, and four Excel functions, and you can answer almost any probability question a manager asks.

Question 1 is a normal problem, question 2 is a binomial problem built on the answer to question 1, and question 3 is a normal problem run backwards. All three are solved in the Application section. By then you'll be able to do them yourself.

Level 1 · Basics

The idea in plain words

A probability distribution is the “shape of chance”: it tells you which values are likely and which are rare.

On the random variables page you built distributions by hand, from records or judgement. That works, but it's slow. The good news: a handful of standard shapes turn up again and again in business. If your situation matches one of them, the probabilities come straight from a formula, a printed table or one Excel function.

This course examines two of them in depth: the binomial (for counts of yes/no results) and the normal (for measurements). Two more, the Poisson and the uniform, are in the syllabus at a lighter level.

Think of it like

Ready-made clothing sizes. You could have every shirt tailored (build every distribution from scratch), but most people fit S, M, L or XL. You only need a tailor when nobody's size fits. Binomial, Poisson, uniform and normal are the standard sizes of probability.

Trial and success
One yes/no attempt (one call, one part, one customer). “Success” is simply the outcome you count, even if it's a defect.
nnn and ppp
Number of trials, and the probability of success on each trial. They are the two settings of a binomial.
μ\muμ and σ\sigmaσ
Mean (centre) and standard deviation (spread). They are the two settings of a normal.
z-score
How many SDs a value is above (+) or below (−) the mean: z=(x−μ)/σz = (x-\mu)/\sigmaz=(x−μ)/σ.
Standard normal
The normal curve with mean 0 and SD 1. Every normal question is translated into it via z.
Cumulative probability
Probability of being at or below a value, P(X≤x)P(X \le x)P(X≤x). The z-table and Excel's TRUE option give this.

Cumulative probabilities and the wording traps

Exam questions rarely ask for “exactly”. They say “at least”, “no more than”, “fewer than”. Each phrase picks a different set of bars, and the most common mistake on the whole paper is picking the wrong set. Use the complement for “at least”: it's far quicker to subtract a few bars from 1 than to add many.

B. The Poisson distribution Light coverage

Some counts have no fixed nnn: customers walking into a branch in 10 minutes, calls to a helpline per hour, defects per 100 metres of cable. If events happen independently and at a steady average rate, the count follows a Poisson distribution. It has one setting, the mean μ\muμ (often written λ\lambdaλ) for the interval you care about.

C. The uniform distribution Light coverage

The simplest continuous shape: every value between aaa and bbb is equally likely, so the density is a flat rectangle of height 1/(b−a)1/(b-a)1/(b−a). Probability is then just width × height.

D. The normal distribution Core

The bell curve. It fits measurements that cluster around an average with misses equally likely on either side: weights of packs from a filling machine, exam completion times, monthly bills, test scores, returns of a large group of funds. Two numbers pin it down completely: the mean μ\muμ (where the peak sits) and the SD σ\sigmaσ (how wide the bell is).

  • Symmetric about μ\muμ: mean = median = mode. Exactly half the area is on each side.
  • Total area = 1. Probability is area under the curve over a range, as for any continuous variable.
  • Tails never touch zero, but they get thin fast: almost everything is within 3 SDs.
Standardising: from any normal to the z-tableExam formula
z=x−μσ⟺x=μ+z σz = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x = \mu + z\,\sigmaz=σx−μ​⟺x=μ+zσ
zzz
number of SDs that xxx lies above (+) or below (−) the mean
left form
use it to find a probability from a value
right form

Why does this work? Every normal curve is the same shape, just shifted and stretched. Subtracting μ\muμ slides the peak to 0; dividing by σ\sigmaσ squeezes the width to 1. So one table, the standard normal table, serves every normal distribution in the world.

Reading the cumulative z-table (the one in your exam)

The table given in the exam is cumulative: each entry is the area to the left of z, P(Z≤z)P(Z \le z)P(Z≤z). It has a page for negative z and a page for positive z. Round z to two decimals; the first decimal picks the row, the second picks the column. You can practise on the site's z-table page.

Every normal question then becomes one of three areas. Draw the curve, shade what's asked, and pick the matching rule:

Working backwards: from a percentage to a value

“How big must a return be to make the top 10%?” “What cost puts a patient in the lowest 8%?” Here the probability is given and the value is unknown. Reverse the steps: find the area in the body of the table, read off z, then convert with x=μ+zσx = \mu + z\sigmax=μ+zσ.

Exam tip

Common z-values worth memorising: top 10% → z = 1.28 · top 5% → 1.645 · top 2.5% → 1.96 · top 1% → 2.33. Lower tails are the same numbers with a minus sign.

Worked example 3 · normal, working backwards

Who gets into the President's Club?

Monthly sales of a firm's field reps are normal with mean ₹18 lakh and SD ₹3.5 lakh. Only the top 5% get into the President's Club. What monthly sales does a rep need?

Given

x=μ+zσx = \mu + z\sigmax=μ+zσ

Watch the direction

“Top 5%” means 5% in the right tail, so look up 0.95. “Bottom 8%” means 8% in the left tail, so look up 0.08 and you get a negative z. If your cut-off for a top percentage comes out below the mean, you looked up the wrong area.

Explore it yourself

Class problem: 30% of workers take public transport daily. Start at P(X ≥ 3) = 0.6172.
  • Switch between P(X ≥ k) and P(X > k) with k = 3. The probability drops from 0.6172 to 0.3504, because one bar (X = 3, worth 0.2668) leaves the sum. That's the at-least / more-than trap in one click.
  • Set p = 0.5. The bars become perfectly symmetric. Set p = 0.1 and they lean right. Binomial shape depends on p.
  • Push n to 30 with p = 0.3. The bars already look like a bell. That's the normal approximation in the Pro section.
Class problem: drag a to $500 for part (a), use “between” with $300 and $400 for part (c), and “Find cut-off” for the lower 8% in part (d).
  • Part (a): P(X > 500) should read 0.0308. The table method gives 0.0307 because it rounds z = 1.8696 to 1.87.
  • Choose “Find cut-off” and set the area to 0.08: the bottom-8% line sits near $198.73.
  • Choose P(a < X < b) and set a = $236, b = $420 (one SD either side of the mean). The area is 68.3%: the empirical rule.
Poisson is light coverage: get the idea, don't drill it.
  • Check f(3) = 0.1404 against the Theory section, then set k = 2 and switch to P(X ≤ k): 0.1247.

Level 3 · Application

Where managers use it

Quality · binomial

Acceptance sampling

A buyer tests 20 items from each lot and accepts the lot if at most 1 is defective. If the supplier's true defect rate is 3%, P(accept) = P(X ≤ 1) = 0.8802. So about 12% of perfectly normal lots get rejected: the sampling plan itself has a cost.

Banking · binomial

Defaults in a loan pool

A pool of 50 small-business loans, each with a 4% chance of default, expects 2 defaults. The chance of 5 or more is 0.0490. Risk teams size the capital buffer for that tail, not for the average.

Marketing · binomial

Is the campaign working?

With a usual 3% response, 200 mailers should bring about 6 responses. Getting 10 or more by luck has probability 0.0808; getting 3 or fewer, 0.1472. Results outside that comfort zone deserve a closer look.

HR · normal

Setting a test cut-off

If aptitude scores are normal with mean 60 and SD 12, shortlisting the top 20% needs a cut-off of 60 + 0.84 × 12 = 70.08 (Excel: 70.10). Change the percentage and you can see instantly how the shortlist grows.

Operations · normal

Service-level promises

If counter service time is normal with mean 4 minutes and SD 0.8, then 89.4% of customers are served within 5 minutes. A “within 5 minutes” promise would be broken for about 1 customer in 10.

Finance · normal (with care)

A bad day for the portfolio

If daily returns were normal with mean 0.05% and SD 1.2%, the worst 5% of days would lose more than 1.645 × 1.2 − 0.05 = 1.92%. Real returns have fatter tails, so treat this as a floor for the risk, not a ceiling (see Pro).

Indian context

A (hypothetical) pharmacy distributor in Dehradun sees weekly demand for a paracetamol brand that is roughly normal, mean 1,200 strips and SD 180. To run out in only 5% of weeks it should start each week with 1200+1.645×180=1496.11200 + 1.645 \times 180 = 1496.11200+1.645×180=1496.1, so 1,497 strips (Excel =NORM.INV(0.95,1200,180) → 1,496.07). The extra 297 strips above average demand is the safety stock, and it is set by the normal curve.

Mini-case

Nuance, pitfalls and depth

Normal approximation to the binomial

For large nnn, binomial bars line up under a bell curve with the same mean and SD. Before computers this was the only practical way to get, say, P(at least 60 defaulters out of 400). It still matters because many tests for proportions (on the hypothesis testing page) rely on it.

Normal approximation to the binomial
use when np≥5 and n(1−p)≥5μ=np,σ=np(1−p)\begin{aligned} &\text{use when } np \ge 5 \text{ and } n(1-p) \ge 5 \\ &\mu = np, \quad \sigma = \sqrt{np(1-p)} \end{aligned}​use when np≥5 and n(1−p)≥5μ=np,σ=np(1−p)​​
continuity correction
a bar at x covers x − 0.5 to x + 0.5, so “X ≥ 60” becomes “X > 59.5” on the curve, and “X ≤ 60” becomes “X < 60.5”

Example: 400 borrowers, each with a 12% chance of default. Then np=48np = 48np=48 and n(1−p)=352n(1-p) = 352n(1−p)=352, both well over 5. σ=400(0.12)(0.88)=6.50\sigma = \sqrt{400(0.12)(0.88)} = 6.50σ=400(0.12)(0.88)​=6.50. For P(60 or more defaulters): z=(59.5−48)/6.50=1.77z = (59.5 - 48)/6.50 = 1.77z=(59.5−48)/6.50=1.77, table 1−0.9616=0.03841 - 0.9616 = 0.03841−0.9616=0.0384. The exact binomial answer is 0.0416. Without the continuity correction you'd get 0.0324, noticeably worse.

Is my data really normal?

Every normal calculation assumes the bell shape. Check it before you trust a probability. Three quick checks, in order of effort:

  1. Histogram (see visualization): one peak, roughly symmetric, no long tail on one side.
  2. Empirical rule count: about 68% of values within 1 SD, 95% within 2 SD.
  3. Normal probability plot: sort the data, pair each value with the z-score it “should” have, and plot. A straight line means normal.

Try it on the NYSE trading-volume class problem (13 days). Mean 199.69, SD 26.04. Within 1 SD (173.7 to 225.7): 10 of 13 values = 77%. Within 2 SD: 12 of 13 = 92%. The odd one out is 265, which also drives the sample skewness up to 1.05.

Verdict: with only 13 points, “approximately normal” is a fair working assumption, as the problem says, but the 265-million day is a warning that busy days may be more common than the bell curve predicts. In Excel: sort the data, put =NORM.S.INV((ROW()-1-0.5)/13) next to each value (if the data start in row 2), and insert a scatter chart. The Regression tool in the ToolPak also has a Normal Probability Plots tick-box (see regression).

What breaks each model

Binomial breaks when…

  • p drifts: a machine wears out during the shift; conversion falls late at night.
  • Trials are linked: defects come in clusters from one bad batch; delays on a rainy day hit every order.
  • Sampling without replacement from a small group: picking 4 of 6 team members. That's the hypergeometric distribution Beyond.
vs

Normal breaks when…

  • The data are skewed: incomes, claim sizes, house prices, delivery delays with a long tail.
  • Tails are fat: share returns crash more often than the bell curve allows.
  • Values are bounded: a percentage stuck near 0 or 100, a 1–5 rating scale.

Poisson as a shortcut for rare events Light coverage

When nnn is large and ppp small, the binomial is almost identical to a Poisson with μ=np\mu = npμ=np. For 1,000 card transactions each with a 0.3% fraud chance, the binomial P(exactly 2 frauds) is 0.2242 and the Poisson (μ = 3) gives 0.2240. Banks model fraud and insurers model claims this way.

How a consultant would present it

Translate probabilities into frequencies people can picture. Not “P = 0.0548” but “about 1 order in 18 would be free, so roughly 550 free orders a day at our volume of 10,000”. Not “z = 1.645” but “to be in the top 5% a rep needs about ₹5.8 lakh more than the average rep”.

Interview-style insight

“Why do so many business measures look normal?” Because many of them are sums or averages of lots of small independent effects (a delivery time is the sum of cooking, waiting, riding, parking). The central limit theorem says such sums tend towards a bell shape. It's also why the sample mean is normal even when the data aren't, which is the basis of the sampling and estimation pages.

  • Normal: z=(x−μ)/σz = (x-\mu)/\sigmaz=(x−μ)/σ. The table gives the area to the left. Right = 1 − table. Between = difference of two table values.
  • Backwards: find the area in the body → z → x=μ+zσx = \mu + z\sigmax=μ+zσ. Excel =NORM.INV(p,μ,σ), =NORM.S.INV(p).
  • Key z's: 1.28 (10%), 1.645 (5%), 1.96 (2.5%), 2.33 (1%). Empirical rule 68 / 95 / 99.7.
  • Pro: normal ≈ binomial if np ≥ 5 and n(1−p) ≥ 5, with continuity correction ±0.5. Check normality with a histogram or a normal probability plot.
