The manager's question

The manager's question

You plan purchasing for a cloud kitchen in Dehradun. Every Sunday night you decide how much paneer, rice and packaging to order for the week ahead. Order too little and Friday's customers get "item unavailable". Order too much and food goes in the bin on Monday. Last week you served 159 orders. Do you plan for 159 again? For the average of the last few weeks? And when Diwali week arrives, how much extra?

A forecast is a disciplined guess built from the past. The honest way to pick a forecasting method is to measure how wrong it would have been.

That's the whole topic in two sentences. Everything below is either a way of making the guess (moving averages, exponential smoothing, trend lines, seasonal indexes) or a way of scoring it (MAD, MSE, MAPE). The class has not covered this yet, so the page starts from zero.

What the exam can ask

Core: naive, moving-average, weighted-moving-average and exponential-smoothing forecasts by hand; MAD, MSE and MAPE; choosing the better method or α; a linear trend fitted with a sums table; seasonal indexes, deseasonalising and a trend × season forecast; reading Excel's Moving Average and Exponential Smoothing output. Light: seasonal dummy-variable regression, FORECAST.ETS. Beyond: the internals of Holt and Winters smoothing.

Level 1 · Basics

The idea in plain words

A time series is one variable measured again and again at regular intervals, kept in time order: weekly orders, monthly sales, quarterly profit, daily footfall.

Most of the statistics you have met so far used cross-sectional data: many units (customers, stores, employees) observed at one point in time. There, row order doesn't matter. Shuffle the rows and the mean is unchanged. In a time series the order is the information. This October tells you something about next October, and last week tells you something about this week.

Think of it like

Driving using the rear-view mirror. On a straight road with gentle, familiar bends, the road behind you is a decent guide to the road ahead. That's a stable pattern, and forecasting works. At a sudden hairpin (a lockdown, a new competitor, a GST change) the mirror is useless. Forecasting quietly assumes the road keeps behaving the way it has been behaving.

Five patterns to recognise

Before you choose any method, plot the series and name its shape. Honestly, this one habit prevents most forecasting mistakes.

The four components

Analysts think of a series as a mix of up to four ingredients. Each one has a business story behind it.

T · Trend

The long-run direction

Population growth, a brand gaining share, rising prices. UPI transaction counts in India have had a strong upward trend for years.

S · Seasonal

A pattern that repeats within a year

AC sales peak in April–June. Sweets peak around Diwali. Weekend spikes in restaurant orders are a seasonal pattern within a week.

C · Cyclical

Slow waves lasting years

Business cycles: real-estate and auto sales boom and slump over 3–8 years. Hard to forecast with past data alone.

I · Irregular

What's left: noise and one-offs

A rainy Saturday, a viral reel, a supply hiccup. Unpredictable by definition; we only try to keep it small.

With quarterly data the cyclical part can't be separated from the trend over just three years, so it hides inside TTT. That's normal for business forecasting horizons.

The simplest forecast of all

The naive method says next week will look like this week: Ft+1=YtF_{t+1} = Y_tFt+1​=Yt​. It sounds lazy, but it's an important yardstick. If your clever method can't beat "same as last time", it isn't adding anything. On a series that wanders up and down at random, the naive forecast is jumpy because it chases every wiggle. On a steadily rising series it lags by one period. Every method on this page is an attempt to do better than that.

Level 2 · Theory

Formulas and how they work

1. Scoring a forecast: error, MAD, MSE, MAPE

A forecasting method is only as good as its track record. So we pretend to forecast the past: stand at week 3, forecast week 4, compare with what actually happened, and repeat. Then we summarise the errors. Adding the raw errors is useless (positives and negatives cancel), so we use one of three fixes.

When you compare two methods, score them over the same periods. A 3-period moving average has no forecast for weeks 1–3, so compare everybody on weeks 4 onward. Write that sentence in your answer; examiners look for it.

Take the last kkk values, average them, and that average is next period's forecast. When a new week arrives, drop the oldest and add the newest; the window "moves". Cloud kitchen: weeks 6, 7, 8 were 152, 166, 159, so the 3-week MA forecast for week 9 is (152+166+159)/3=159(152+166+159)/3 = 159(152+166+159)/3=159.

Choosing k is a trade-off. A long window (large kkk) averages away more noise, so forecasts are calm, but it reacts slowly to real change. A short window reacts quickly but jumps with every random blip. Choose kkk by trying a few values and keeping the one with the lowest MSE (or MAD) over the same periods.

The lag problem. A moving average is the average of the past, so on a rising series it always sits below where the series is now. For a series rising by bbb per period, a kkk-period MA forecast trails by roughly b(k+1)/2b(k+1)/2b(k+1)/2. In Figure 5 the UPI series climbs about 2.59 lakh a month, so the rule predicts a lag of about 5.2 for k=3k = 3k=3 and 9.1 for k=6k = 6k=6. The actual average misses were 5.28 and 9.21. Close.

Read the right-hand version aloud: new forecast = old forecast + α × (how wrong the old forecast was). It's a self-correcting rule. Last week you forecast 150 orders, 160 turned up (error = 10).   • α close to 1: "the latest week tells me almost everything." Forecasts chase the data. At α=1\alpha = 1α=1 you get the naive forecast.
  • α close to 0: "one week is mostly noise; I trust the long history." Forecasts barely move.
  • Typical business values sit between 0.1 and 0.4 for stable series.

Why "exponential"? Substitute the formula into itself and every past value appears with a weight that shrinks by a factor (1−α)(1-\alpha)(1−α) each step back: α, α(1−α), α(1−α)2,…\alpha,\ \alpha(1-\alpha),\ \alpha(1-\alpha)^2, \dotsα, α(1−α), α(1−α)2,…. With α=0.2\alpha = 0.2α=0.2 the weights run 0.20, 0.16, 0.128… With α=0.6\alpha = 0.6α=0.6: 0.60, 0.24, … The weights add up to 1, and old data never quite drops to zero.

Choosing α. Compute the forecasts for several α values, work out the MSE of each over the same periods, keep the smallest. In Excel, Solver can do the search for you (see the Excel section). One honest caveat: like a moving average, simple smoothing has no idea of trend, so on a rising series it lags too. The MSE-best α then drifts towards 1, which is the series telling you "use a trend method".

6. Seasonality: indexes, deseasonalising, trend × season

A seasonal index says how a season compares with a typical period. An index of 1.456 for April–June means that quarter normally sells 45.6% above the trend; 0.719 for October–December means 28.1% below. The indexes average 1, so four quarterly indexes add up to 4 (twelve monthly ones to 12).

Why deseasonalise? AC sales went from 38 (Q4, year 1) to 47 (Q1, year 2). That looks like 23.7% growth in one quarter. But Q1 is simply a stronger season than Q4. Deseasonalised, the series moved from 52.84 to 54.98, a real increase of only 4.1%. Always deseasonalise before you judge a trend or celebrate a "record month".

Interpret

Summer (Q2) runs about 46% above the trend and winter (Q4) about 28% below, which fits Delhi's climate. Underneath the seasons the business grows by roughly ₹1.4 lakh a quarter, so next summer should bring close to ₹98 lakh. Stock and staff for that peak in March, not in May.

Why centre the moving average?

An average of quarters 1–4 belongs to the middle of that stretch, which is "quarter 2.5". There is no such quarter. The next average belongs to quarter 3.5. Averaging the two lands exactly on quarter 3, so we can divide Q3's actual sales by a trend value that belongs to Q3. With an odd window (say 3 months) the average already sits on a real period and no centring is needed. With 12-month windows for monthly data, centre in the same way.

Level 3 · Application

Where managers use it

Retail & FMCG

Demand planning

Weekly SKU forecasts drive orders to suppliers and stock at every store. Seasonal indexes set the festive build-up; smoothing handles the thousands of slow, flat SKUs.

Finance

Revenue and cash-flow budgets

Next year's budget starts from a trend × season forecast of monthly revenue. Treasury forecasts collections to plan working-capital borrowing.

Operations

Staffing a call centre

Forecast calls per half-hour (strong day-of-week and time-of-day seasonality), then convert to agents needed. A 5% forecast miss is a queue or idle staff.

HR

Hiring and attrition

Monthly attrition has seasons too (after bonus payouts, after appraisals). A forecast of exits tells recruitment how many offers to make, and when.

Marketing

Measuring campaign lift

What would sales have been without the campaign? Forecast the baseline from pre-campaign data; lift = actual − baseline. Without a forecast, every sale gets credited to the ad.

Hospitality & banking

Occupancy and ATM cash

Hotels forecast room-nights to set prices for each date; banks forecast ATM withdrawals (salary days, festivals) so machines don't run dry or hold idle cash.

Indian context

Festive season. Navratri, Dussehra and Diwali (September–November) drive the year's biggest spike for apparel, electronics, gold, sweets and two-wheelers. A catch: festival dates follow the lunar calendar, so Diwali falls in October one year and November the next. Monthly seasonal indexes then smear the peak across two months. Use quarterly data, or add a "festival month" dummy to a regression.

Monsoon. Mosquito repellents, umbrellas and cough syrups rise in July–September while ice creams and cold drinks dip. Rural FMCG demand follows how good the monsoon was. Forecasters often add rainfall (vs the long-run normal) as an extra regressor.

UPI growth. Our hypothetical payments start-up went from 43 to 86 lakh transactions a month in 18 months (Figure 5). That's a trend, so moving averages and simple smoothing will under-forecast every single month. Trend projection or Holt's method is the right tool.

Holdout testing: don't mark your own homework

Errors measured on the same data used to build the model flatter the model. The professional habit is to hide the most recent stretch (the holdout), fit on the rest, forecast the hidden periods, and only then compare with the actuals. The syllabus calls this model validation on a holdout sample.

Additive or multiplicative seasons?

Additive

Seasonal effect is a fixed amount: "Q2 adds ₹42 lakh." Swings stay the same size as the business grows. Dummy-variable regression is additive.

vs

Multiplicative

Seasonal effect is a percentage: "Q2 is 46% above trend." Swings grow with the business. Ratio-to-moving-average decomposition is multiplicative.

For the AC dealer, the two models agree on the quiet quarters but differ at the peak: next summer's Q2 forecast is 98.48 (multiplicative) vs 93.25 (additive). If the summer peak has been widening as the dealer grows (it has: 74, 82, 90), the multiplicative version is the better story.

When the past stops being a guide

  • Structural breaks. Lockdown months, a GST rate change, a new metro station next to the store. Don't let them silently drive the indexes: flag them, exclude or dummy them out, and say so in your report.
  • Cyclical swings are real but hard to forecast from the series alone. Link the forecast to leading indicators (credit growth, PMI, auto loans) or run scenarios.
  • Demand vs sales. If you were out of stock, recorded sales understate demand. Forecasting from censored sales bakes yesterday's stock-outs into tomorrow's orders.
  • MAPE's blind spots. With slow sellers (actuals of 0, 1, 2 units), MAPE explodes. Many firms use weighted MAPE instead: WMAPE=∑∣et∣/∑Yt\text{WMAPE} = \sum|e_t| / \sum Y_tWMAPE=∑∣et​∣/∑Yt​, which is total miss over total demand.

Forecasting is a business process, not a formula (S&OP)

In well-run companies the statistical forecast is step two of a monthly five-step cycle called Sales & Operations Planning:

Clean historyMark stock-outs, promotions and one-offs.
Baseline forecastThe statistical method that won on the holdout.
Market overlay & consensusSales and marketing add launches, price changes, festive dates; everyone agrees one number.
Supply, finance & sign-offCapacity, inventory and cash checked; leaders decide trade-offs.
Track accuracy & biasMAPE and bias every month, at every step. Feed back into step 1.

A neat idea from practice: forecast value added. Measure the MAPE of the naive forecast, the statistical forecast and the final consensus forecast. If the sales team's overrides make the forecast worse than the statistical baseline (common, honestly, thanks to optimism bias), the process should change.

How a consultant would present it

"We forecast ₹5.67 crore for the Diwali quarter, likely between ₹5.2 and ₹6.1 crore. That's a trend × season model which, on last year's quarters held out for testing, missed by 8% on average and showed no bias. The biggest risk is the festival dates moving into November. If October's first two weeks come in 10% below plan, we cut the second packaging order." One number, one range, one accuracy claim, one risk, one trigger.

Interview-style question

"Your forecast has a MAPE of 4%. Is that good?" A strong answer: "Compared with what? I'd check the naive forecast's MAPE on the same periods, check the bias (a 4% MAPE with all errors on one side is a problem), check it was measured on a holdout, and ask what a 4% miss costs this business."

Forecasting Food and Beverage Sales

A seafood restaurant on a Florida island wants to see a year ahead, month by month.

The situation

The Vintage Restaurant is on Captiva Island, near Fort Myers in Florida. Its owner, Karen Payne, has just completed three years in business and built a reputation for fresh seafood, along with some of the fastest growth on the island. Growth brings planning questions: how many staff to roster, how much stock to buy, when to schedule repairs. She wants a system that forecasts food and beverage sales for each month, up to a year ahead.

You have 36 months of sales, in thousands of dollars. That's three full cycles of a resort town's year: a busy winter when visitors arrive from the cold north, then quieter summer and autumn months. Three cycles is just enough to estimate twelve monthly seasonal indexes plus a trend, which is part of the lesson.

What the manager wants

  1. A time-series plot with a comment on the underlying pattern.
  2. Monthly seasonal indexes: which months run high and which run low, and whether that makes sense for an island restaurant.
  3. A deseasonalised series, and a judgment on whether a trend sits underneath.
  4. Forecasts for January–December of year 4 by time series decomposition.
  5. Forecasts for the same twelve months by regression with monthly dummy variables.
  6. Summary tables and charts for an appendix.
  7. A follow-up: January of year 4 comes in at $295,000. What was your forecast error? If it looks large, how would you explain it and reduce Karen's doubts about the method?

Data you need

One series of 36 monthly sales values ($1000s), January of year 1 to December of year 3. Add a period number t=1,…,36t = 1, \dots, 36t=1,…,36 and a month label. The data file is Vintage.xlsx on the textbook companion site (ask your instructor); the values are also printed in the book as Table 17.26. We don't reproduce them here.

How to crack it

  1. Lay out and plotColumns: t, Year, Month, Sales. Insert › Line chart. Describe what you see in one sentence: a repeating yearly shape plus a gentle rise.
  2. 12-month centred moving averageMonthly data, so the window is 12. The first 12-month average belongs between months 6 and 7. For month 7: =(AVERAGE(D2:D13)+AVERAGE(D3:D14))/2, copied down to month 30 (24 values).
  3. RatiosSales ÷ centred MA for months 7–30. That gives two ratios for every calendar month.
  4. Seasonal indexes=AVERAGEIF(C:C,"Jan",F:F) for each month, then multiply all twelve by 12 ÷ their sum so they add to 12.
  5. Deseasonalise and check the trendSales ÷ that month's index (use XLOOKUP on the month). Plot it. Then ToolPak Regression of deseasonalised sales on t: is the slope clearly positive (small p-value)?
  6. Decomposition forecastFor t = 37 to 48: trend value × that month's index.
  7. Dummy-variable regressionCreate 11 dummy columns (January to November, with December as the baseline) plus t. Run Data › Data Analysis › Regression with all 12 X columns, then forecast t = 37 to 48.
  8. Judge the January missError = 295 − forecast. Compare it with each method's typical in-sample miss (MAD or MAPE) before calling it large.
What a strong managerial report includes
  • One clear chart with actual sales, the deseasonalised line and the year-4 forecast, so Karen can see season and growth separately.
  • The twelve indexes as a bar chart with plain labels ("January is 44% above a typical month"). Our checkpoints, to tell you you're on track: the January index comes out at about 1.44 (the highest), September at about 0.63 (the lowest), and the top three months are January, March, February. That fits a winter-visitor economy, with the late-summer heat and hurricane season keeping visitors away.
  • The trend in one sentence: deseasonalised sales grow by roughly $1.02 thousand a month.
  • Both forecast tables side by side. Our January year-4 figures: about $299 thousand by decomposition and $287 thousand by dummy regression. Small differences from your numbers are expected (rounding, or software such as Minitab averaging the ratios with medians).
  • The January verdict. 295 − 299 ≈ −4 thousand (1.4%) for decomposition, 295 − 287 ≈ +8 thousand (2.8%) for dummies. The typical in-sample miss is about 1.2% for decomposition and 1.5% for the dummy model, so January is a normal month for decomposition and only a little wider than usual for the dummy model. Not a failure; say so plainly.
  • How to handle doubt when a miss is large: present forecasts as ranges, not points; track error and bias monthly; re-estimate as each new month arrives (a rolling forecast); look for causes outside the data (weather, events, a new competitor, an Easter date shift); combine the model with judgment such as reservations already on the books.
  • Recommendations tied to the indexes: rostering and purchasing by month, and a quiet-season window (September–October) for staff leave and maintenance.

Actuals were 100, 80 and 120; forecasts were 90, 88 and 114. What is the MAPE?

  1. 8.00%
  2. 8.33%
  3. 8.49%
  4. 25.00%
Percentage errors: 10/100 = 10%, 8/80 = 10%, 6/120 = 5%; average = 8.33%. 8.49% divides by the forecasts (wrong), 8.00 is the MAD in units, and 25% is the sum rather than the mean.

A brand's Q3 seasonal index is 0.85 and its Q3 sales were ₹170 lakh. Which statement is correct?

  1. Q3 sales were 15% above trend.
  2. Deseasonalised Q3 sales are 170 × 0.85 = ₹144.5 lakh.
  3. Deseasonalised, the business ran at about ₹200 lakh a quarter in Q3.
  4. Q3 sales were 85% of last year's Q3.
Deseasonalise by dividing: 170 / 0.85 = 200. An index of 0.85 means Q3 normally runs 15% below a typical quarter; it says nothing about last year's Q3.
